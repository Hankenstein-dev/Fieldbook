## Bermuda buttercup — Oxalis pes-caprae

Portuguese names in source: Azeda

The bright yellow flowers of this wood-sorrel belong to a plant native to South Africa. In its native populations, flowers occur with their reproductive parts at three different heights. Introduced populations often have only one form and may produce no seeds at all.

They can spread very successfully anyway. Numerous small underground bulbs establish new plants and make a patch difficult to remove. That explains how an apparently simple seasonal carpet can persist year after year. Oxalic acid gives the plant its sour character, while its golden petals have been used to make a yellow dye. Its chemistry and its hidden bulbs are as much a part of its story as the flowers.

## Silver wattle — Acacia dealbata

Portuguese names in source: Mimosa

Each yellow ball on silver wattle is a cluster of tiny flowers, rather than one flower. The finely divided, silvery foliage makes a striking backdrop, which is one reason its flowering branches are sold by florists as mimosa. In Italy, bouquets are traditionally given on International Women's Day, and the fragrant flowers are also used in perfume.

The tree comes from Australia, where Indigenous peoples have used its bark for cordage, its sap as an adhesive and its wood for tools. Its beauty has carried it far beyond its native range, but it is invasive in Portugal. This is a useful plant to recognise both for its cultural history and for the effect introduced trees can have on surrounding vegetation.

## Boar Thistle — Galactites tomentosus

Portuguese names in source: Cardo-dos-picos

Purple milk thistle has white-mottled leaves with woolly undersides and sharply spined edges. The pale covering explains both parts of its scientific name: Galactites refers to milk, while tomentosus describes its dense hairs.

Look closely at a flower head and its flowers have different roles. Those around the outside are sterile; the central ones contain the reproductive parts and are pollinated by insects. The resulting little fruits carry white hairy appendages. Its striking appearance is at home in sunny, disturbed places, including roadsides and uncultivated ground.

## Sage-leaved Rock-rose — Cistus salviifolius

Portuguese names in source: Estevinha

The small, wrinkled leaves of sage-leaved rockrose explain its name: they resemble those of sage. Its white flowers have yellow at the bases of their petals and a mass of yellow stamens in the centre. Bees visit them for the abundant pollen.

This is a shrub of dry hills, scrub and open woodland. It copes well with drought and can re-establish quickly after fire, making it part of the changing vegetation you may see as an open landscape recovers.

## cork oak — Quercus suber

Portuguese names in source: Sobreiro

Cork is bark that the tree can replace. A cork oak is usually first harvested at about 25 years old, then at intervals of roughly nine to twelve years. Skilled workers remove the outer layer while protecting the living tissues beneath. The first harvest is coarse and cracked; later harvests produce the more regular cork suited to bottle stoppers. A recently stripped trunk is reddish-brown, darkening as it ages.

The material's structure explains its usefulness. Cork is made of air-filled cells, with suberin helping repel water. It provides insulation in buildings, resilience in flooring and material for objects as varied as fishing-rod handles and cricket balls. On the living tree, the thick bark also insulates against fire, allowing a surviving oak to regrow its canopy.

Cork oak woodland is more than a source of bark. Its acorns feed animals, and its roots exchange products of photosynthesis with fungi that help supply water and nutrients. The open woodlands managed for cork in Portugal, called montados, support a rich mixture of plants and wildlife. A tree that can live for centuries connects a working landscape with a remarkably varied community of organisms.

## purple viper's-bugloss — Echium plantagineum

Portuguese names in source: língua-de-vaca; Soagem; Flor-roxa

Purple viper's-bugloss carries purple flowers above rough, hairy leaves, with the stamens projecting beyond the flower. It has become a serious invasive weed in some countries where it was introduced.

The plant contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids that can poison grazing animals, especially horses. Its striking colour can therefore be part of a very different story in a pasture: a flowering plant that is easy to admire can also be important to recognise for livestock health.

## strawberry tree — Arbutus unedo

Portuguese names in source: medronheiro

The strawberry tree gets its name from its rough, red fruits, although it is not closely related to a true strawberry. Its fruit takes about a year to ripen, so autumn can bring ripe berries and the next crop of small, bell-shaped flowers together on the same tree. That overlap is one of its loveliest features.

In Portugal the fruit is used to make medronho, a strong fruit spirit. It also goes into jams and other preserves; ripe berries bruise easily, which helps explain why they are less familiar as a fresh shop-bought fruit. Bees visit the flowers, and strawberry-tree honey has a distinctive bitter taste. Birds eat the fruit too, carrying its small seeds to new places.

## giant reed — Arundo donax

Portuguese names in source: Cana; Taboca; Taquara-do-reino

Giant reed has hollow canes that can tower well above a person. Those stems have been used for building, fishing rods and walking sticks, and they have a particularly important musical role: they provide the reeds used in instruments such as clarinets, saxophones, oboes and bassoons.

The plant spreads mainly through pieces of stem and underground rhizome rather than fertile seed. Floodwater can carry broken pieces downstream, where they take root and establish new stands. It can also regrow strongly after fire. These abilities make it useful as a source of plant material, but help it dominate waterways and displace other vegetation where it becomes invasive.

## mastic — Pistacia lentiscus

Portuguese names in source: Lentisqueira; Lentisco-verdadeiro; Aroeira

Mastic is a tough evergreen shrub of dry, rocky ground, including places close to the sea. Its leathery leaves are divided into pairs of smaller leaflets, and its little fruits change from red to black as they ripen. Birds find both food and shelter in the shrub and help disperse its seeds.

Its most famous product is the fragrant resin also called mastic. The resin hardens into brittle pieces but softens into a gum when chewed. It is used in chewing gum, sweets, breads and drinks, and helps give some Turkish ice creams their characteristic texture. Mastic can also hybridise with its relative, the terebinth, which can make the resulting plants difficult to identify.

## Pampas Grass — Cortaderia selloana

Portuguese names in source: Erva-das-pampas; Penachos

Pampas grass is best known for its tall plumes, but its leaves explain the name Cortaderia: it comes from a word meaning cutter, referring to their sharp edges. The plant naturally favours moist ground with plenty of light.

It has been planted widely for its appearance and has become invasive in many temperate regions. Disturbed, damp ground can suit it especially well, which helps explain why a grass associated with gardens also appears along roads and other altered landscapes.

## Olive — Olea europaea

Portuguese names in source: Oliveira; Zambujeiro

An olive eaten straight from the tree is very different from a prepared table olive. Raw fruit is intensely bitter, largely because of compounds such as oleuropein. Curing and fermentation remove much of that bitterness. Green and dark olives also reflect different stages of ripeness, alongside differences between cultivated varieties.

The tree has been cultivated for thousands of years. Its fruit supplies both food and oil, which once fuelled lamps as well as kitchens; its hard, patterned wood is used for decorative objects. The olive branch's association with peace adds a familiar symbol to that practical history.

An old olive can keep growing after losing much of its trunk, sending up new shoots from surviving parts below. That resilience helps it remain productive for centuries, but it also makes precise ages hard to establish: hollow trunks, irregular rings and repeated regrowth complicate the claim that a particular tree is thousands of years old.

## sea fig — Carpobrotus edulis

Portuguese names in source: Chorão-das-praias; Chorão-da-praia

Sea fig spreads as a mat of creeping stems and thick, succulent leaves. Its large yellow flowers open in bright morning light and close at night. The mats can expand over a considerable area, providing shelter for small animals while also covering the ground beneath them.

In its native South Africa, animals eat the flowers, leaves and fruit, and some help disperse its seeds. People also gather ripe fruit to make a sharply flavoured jam. Elsewhere the plant has spread beyond cultivation and become invasive, so its usefulness and its effect on a habitat depend on where it is growing.

## common bracken — Pteridium aquilinum

Portuguese names in source: feto-do-monte; feiteiro; Samambaia; Samambaia-do-campo

A hillside of bracken can contain far fewer individual plants than it appears to. The separate triangular fronds rise from long underground rhizomes, forming colonies of genetically identical growth. Some colonies are thought to be centuries old, even though the visible fronds die back each year.

New fronds emerge tightly coiled and gradually unfurl. Later, narrow bands of spore-producing structures develop beneath the fronds; this is a fern, so it reproduces without flowers or seeds. People have used bracken for roofing, lining and basketry, and it has food traditions in several countries. Those traditions coexist with established toxicity, so its historical use as a vegetable should not be read as a general endorsement of eating it.

## purple foxglove — Digitalis purpurea

Portuguese names in source: dedaleira

Foxglove usually spends its first year as a low rosette of leaves, then raises a tall stem of hanging, spotted flowers. Bees climb inside the tubes to reach nectar. A foxglove pug caterpillar takes a different approach: it can seal itself inside a flower with silk and feed on the developing parts.

Maritime pine has stout needles in pairs and thick, deeply cracked, reddish bark. ## Common St. John's Wort — Hypericum perforatum

Portuguese names in source: Erva-de-são-joão; Hipericão; Hipericão-de-S.João

The tiny pale dots in St John's wort leaves look like holes when light shines through them. They are actually translucent glands, and they explain the scientific name perforatum. Dark glands are visible too, especially around the leaf edges.

Its name and folklore are linked to midsummer and St John's feast day, when people traditionally gathered it and hung it near doors to ward off harm. The plant has also supplied dyes, producing different colours according to the extraction and treatment of the cloth. Its long medicinal history continues in herbal products today, but it can interact seriously with medicines; that history is not a reason to treat it as a harmless tea.

Underground, it is remarkably persistent. Creeping stems help it spread, and seeds can remain dormant in the soil for decades before disturbance gives them a chance to germinate.

## common fig — Ficus carica

Portuguese names in source: figueira; Figo; Figo-roxo

A fig hides its flowers on the inside. What looks like a single fruit is a hollow, fleshy structure lined with tiny flowers, which can each form a small seed-bearing fruit. A specialised fig wasp enters through a narrow opening to pollinate them. Some cultivated varieties, however, develop ripe figs without pollination, so the wasp relationship does not apply to every fig we eat.

Figs may have been cultivated more than 11,000 years ago. Archaeological finds of seedless forms have been interpreted as evidence that people deliberately propagated desirable trees before some cereal crops were domesticated. Many varieties can produce two crops: an early one on the previous year's shoots and a main crop on new growth.

Ripe figs are delicate and do not keep well, which helps explain their long history as dried fruit, preserves and fig cakes. The tree's milky sap has a different character from the ripe fruit: on skin, exposure followed by sunlight can cause inflammation.

## mophead hydrangea — Hydrangea macrophylla

Portuguese names in source: Hidrângea; Hortênsia; Hortense

A hydrangea's colour can reflect the chemistry of its soil. In many coloured varieties, acidic conditions allow aluminium to become available to the plant; its interaction with flower pigments shifts the display towards blue. In less acidic conditions, pink is more usual. The colour difference can therefore reflect chemistry beneath the shrub.

The flower heads have their own story. Wild plants in Japan have small fertile flowers in the centre, surrounded by a few much larger, usually sterile ones that help attract pollinators. Lacecap varieties retain that arrangement, while the familiar mopheads have been selected for a rounded mass of showy flowers. Widely planted for these displays, the species has also become invasive in parts of Portugal.

## Bay laurel — Laurus nobilis

Portuguese names in source: Loureiro

Bay leaves come from an evergreen tree that can grow far larger than a kitchen-garden shrub. Its male and female flowers are carried on separate plants, and female trees can develop shiny black fruits. The leaves supply the familiar aromatic seasoning, while oil from the fruit has a history in soap-making.

Laurel wreaths were symbols of honour and victory in ancient Greece and Roman culture. That association survives in poet laureate and the expression resting on your laurels. In one version of the myth of Apollo and Daphne, Daphne becomes a laurel tree, giving the wreath a place in mythology as well as public ceremony.

Bay also belongs to an older ecological story. It is a surviving member of laurel forests that were once more widespread under wetter climates. Some remnants persist in countries including Portugal. The tree beside a path connects a cooking ingredient, a symbol of achievement and the history of a changing landscape.

## Borage — Borago officinalis

Portuguese names in source: Borragem

Borage's blue, five-pointed flowers explain its other name, starflower. They rise above coarse, bristly leaves and produce plentiful nectar for honeybees. The plant also readily seeds itself, so an annual can return to the same garden year after year.

Its cucumber-like flavour has given it a long culinary history, including cooked greens, sauces and flower garnishes. Today it is also grown commercially for seed oil. The food history comes with a chemical complication: borage produces pyrrolizidine alkaloids that can damage the liver, so traditional use does not make it an unrestricted edible. Older herbals associated it with joy and relief from melancholy, a belief that became part of its folklore.

## Pale flax — Linum bienne

## Mediterranean juniper — Juniperus turbinata

Portuguese names in source: Sabina-da-praia

This juniper releases pollen in autumn, unlike its close relative Juniperus phoenicea, which does so in spring. That difference in timing accompanies differences in the cones and DNA. Plants once treated together under one species name are now distinguished as separate species.

The adult leaves are tiny scales pressed close to the twigs, giving the foliage a very different texture from a broad-leaved shrub. Its seed cones are slightly elongated and usually hold several seeds. Those small details, along with the season of pollen release, tell more of its identity than its overall shape alone.

## Tree heath — Erica arborea

Tree heath can grow into a substantial shrub or small tree, with needle-like leaves and masses of tiny white bells that smell of honey. Despite its larger size, the flowers make its family resemblance to smaller heathers easy to appreciate. It prefers acid soil.

## Pyrenean Oak — Quercus pyrenaica

Portuguese names in source: Carvalho-negral

Young Pyrenean oaks often keep their dead leaves attached through winter, a habit called marcescence. Their deeply lobed leaves have star-shaped hairs on both surfaces. Looking closely at the foliage reveals a texture that the tree's outline alone cannot show.

Its relatively short growing season is thought to help it avoid summer drought, fitting a long-lived tree's annual activity into the more favourable part of the year.

## purple loosestrife — Lythrum salicaria

Portuguese names in source: erva-carapau; salgueirinha

Purple loosestrife's flowers come in three arrangements. The pollen-receiving style may be short, medium or long, with the pollen-bearing stamens occupying the other two levels. Matching these levels between different flower types helps pollen travel between plants rather than simply fertilising the flower it came from.

The tall purple spikes grow in wet places such as ditches, marshes and lake margins. They support a varied insect community: some beetles eat the leaves, a root weevil's larvae feed underground, and a tiny flower weevil develops inside the buds. Several of these insects have been used to control introduced loosestrife abroad. The same close relationships that belong to its natural history can become useful when it spreads beyond its native range.

## blue lily — Agapanthus praecox

The rounded flower heads of agapanthus are made up of many separate blue, purple or white flowers carried together on a tall stalk. They rise above long, strap-shaped leaves, and are followed by capsules containing black seeds.

## ragwort — Jacobaea vulgaris

Portuguese names in source: Erva-de-Santiago; Tasna

Ragwort's yellow flowers provide nectar and pollen for many insects, and its leaves are home to one particularly recognisable caterpillar: the black-and-yellow striped young of the cinnabar moth. ## Mediterranean fan palm — Chamaerops humilis

Portuguese names in source: palmeira-das-vassouras; palmeira-anã

The dwarf fan palm can recover after fire by sending up growth from underground stems and damaged trunks. Its tough, fan-shaped leaves have supplied fibre for baskets, mats and brooms, while the living plant provides cover and helps hold soil in place.

Its relationship with a small weevil is especially intricate. During flowering, the palm attracts these pollinators with scent released by its leaves. The beetles find food and shelter among the flowers and lay eggs there. Their larvae can complete development in old male flower stalks, but fail to do so in female flowerheads as the seeds develop. There is a trade-off: seeds stripped of pulp can also become more vulnerable to insect attack. A low clump of palms supports a surprisingly complicated exchange between plants, beetles and mammals.

## Beet — Beta vulgaris

Portuguese names in source: Acelga-brava; Beterraba

Beetroot, chard, sugar beet and the livestock crop mangelwurzel are cultivated forms of the same species. Their wild ancestor is sea beet, a plant of coastal rocks, beaches and salt marshes. Over generations, growers selected different parts: swollen roots, large leaves and stalks, or roots containing more sugar.

The deep red of beetroot comes from betalain pigments, chemically different from the anthocyanins that colour vegetables such as red cabbage. Different mixtures also produce yellow beets. When cells are damaged during cutting or cooking, pigment escapes and makes the familiar stain.

Beet cultivation is ancient, but the sugar industry developed much later. Eighteenth-century experiments showed that beet sugar was the same substance as cane sugar. Selective breeding increased its concentration, and disruption to cane-sugar supplies during the Napoleonic Wars helped drive a new beet-sugar industry.

Oregano's familiar flavour comes from a mixture of aromatic compounds, including carvacrol and thymol. Their proportions vary with the plant and its growing conditions, so two patches need not smell or taste equally strong. Drying can make the flavour more intense.

In Portugal, dried oregano is a familiar seasoning for tomato-and-cucumber salads, as well as meat and fish. Other traditions include its use on Greek salads and in Italian cooking. It is closely related to sweet marjoram, which helps explain the alternative name wild marjoram.

The word 'oregano' can also be applied to plants outside this species, including some that are not especially close relatives. A shared kitchen name can describe a similar flavour without guaranteeing the same botanical identity.

## Shrubby Seablite — Suaeda vera

Portuguese names in source: Valverde-dos-sapais; Barrilha

Shrubby sea-blite grows in salty places such as salt marshes and salt flats. Its tolerance of salt connects it with an old industry: it was one of the plants burned to produce soda ash for glassmaking and soap. These sodium-rich plants were collectively known as barilla, and their ash was traded in substantial quantities.

A plant's ability to live with salt does not mean every stage of its life benefits from it. Sea-blite seeds germinate more readily in fresh water than salt water. The conditions that suit an established shrub and those that let a seed begin growing can be quite different.

## milk thistle — Silybum marianum

Portuguese names in source: cardo-mariano; cardo-de-santa-maria

Milk thistle is recognisable by its shiny green leaves marked with milky white veins and edged with strong spines. Purple flowerheads later release fruits with long white tufts that help carry them on the wind. Parts of the plant also have a food history, including prepared leaf stalks and flowerheads cooked in a similar way to artichokes.

Its fruits contain the mixture of compounds known as silymarin, used in milk-thistle supplements. The plant has a long history in traditional medicine, especially for liver complaints, but clinical studies of liver disease have produced conflicting or limited results. Two studies funded by the United States' National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health found no benefit from silymarin supplementation for the conditions they examined. A long medicinal history does not establish effectiveness.

## Crimson Pea — Lathyrus clymenum

Portuguese names in source: Cizirão-das-torres

Spanish vetchling has a long agricultural history in Greece, where its seeds are used for a dish called fava: a cooked pulse purée commonly served with onion, olive oil and lemon. In this case, 'fava' does not mean that the dish is made from broad beans; the crop is this species of Lathyrus.

Seeds identified as the plant have been found in ancient storage jars dating to around 1600 BCE. Cultivation has continued in island conditions where volcanic soil, dryness and moisture from the sea shape the crop. It is a useful reminder that a plant encountered as a wild flower in one country can be a treasured food crop in another.

## Common Purslane — Portulaca oleracea

Portuguese names in source: Beldroega

Purslane's fleshy leaves and reddish, spreading stems belong to a plant that is both a familiar weed and a vegetable. In Portugal it goes into sopa de beldroegas, a traditional soup often served with bread, eggs or goat's cheese. Other cuisines use it in salads, with yoghurt or as a cooked green. Its slightly sour taste comes partly from oxalic and malic acids.

Its handling of carbon dioxide is unusually flexible. Purslane can combine two photosynthetic pathways, called C4 and CAM, using CAM activity during drought. This water-saving chemistry produces malic acid overnight, linking the plant's physiology with changes in its sourness.

Small yellow flowers open for only a few hours on sunny mornings, and a large plant can produce a remarkable number of tiny seeds. The seeds germinate best in warm, well-lit conditions; even a shallow covering of soil reduces their chances. That helps explain its success on open, disturbed ground.
