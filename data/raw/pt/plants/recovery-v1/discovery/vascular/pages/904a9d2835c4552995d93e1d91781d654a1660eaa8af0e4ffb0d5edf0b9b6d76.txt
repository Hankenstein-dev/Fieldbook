L7@P0: Abstract
L8@P0: A preliminary study of the intertidal benthic macroalgal flora of the island of Rotuma (120301S
L9@P0: 177°05'E; politically attached to the Fiji Island group) has revealed a total of 88 taxa, including 41
L10@P0: Rhodophyceae, 11 Phaeophyceae and 36 Chlorophyceae, representing the first published records of
L11@P0: marine algae for this island. Of these, 30 represent new records for the Fijian flora. The Rotuman flora
L12@P0: is distinct from that of Fiji, a probable consequence of habitat limitations and high exposure regimes on
L13@P0: Rotuman reefs that have led to a predominance of low-profile, robust algal species. A distinct
L14@P0: north-south distribution pattern was found, brought about by variations in exposure regimes.
L15@P0: Biogeographic considerations further dissociated the Rotuman and Fijian floras, the former being more
L16@P0: equatorial and in the path of oceanic currents dispersing algal species from donor areas in the central
L17@P0: and western Pacific.
L18@P0: Introduction
L19@P0: The tropical Pacific Ocean, including areas between approximately 30°N and 30°S, is a
L20@P0: rich and varied habitat for algae (Hoek 1984) but has only been studied phycologically fairly
L21@P0: recently. Indeed, most phycological study has taken place in temperate regions, and the
L22@P0: tropics, particularly the South Pacific and tropical regions of the Indian Ocean, have been the
L23@P0: subject of relatively few investigations.
L24@P0: In working with tropical Pacific algae, the main limitation is the lack of a comprehensive
L25@P0: flora for the region, as well as the lack of generally available and accurately identified
L26@P0: herbarium collections. At present, South Pacific algae are held in scattered herbaria,
L27@P0: including the Bernice P. Bishop Museum at the State Museum for Natural and Cultural
L28@P0: History of Hawaii (BISH); the University of Hawaii (UH); the Department of Botany at the
L29@P0: University of Auckland; the British Museum (BM) and the University of California at
L30@P0: Berkeley (UC). A steadily growing collection of tropical algae recently has been deposited at
L31@P0: the South Pacific Regional Herbarium at the University of the South Pacific, Suva, Fiji
L32@P0: (SUVA). There is a conspicuous absence of monographs on most of the tropical algal genera,
L33@P0: and one has to look for information in widely scattered publications, many of which are not
L34@P0: illustrated, or consist of incomplete checklists. Among the few monographs are those of
L35@P0: Halimeda (Hillis-Colinvaux 1959), Polysiphonia (Hollenberg 1968a, 1968b), and Codium
L36@P0: (Silva 1960).
L37@P0: The knowledge of algal floras of islands in the tropical Pacific is of great importance,
L38@P0: firstly from a biogeographic point of view. The world can be divided into a number of
L39@P0: regions as defined by their temperature regimes, and these latitudinal boundaries often (but
L40@P0: not always) coincide with floristic boundaries, as particular species of algae each have their
L41@P0: own range of water-temperature tolerances (Hoek 1984). Comparisons between floras of
L42@P0: different isolated islands within the same phytogeographic boundaries enable one to
L43@P0-1: extrapolate possible dispersal routes and patterns for algae from their presumed Indo-Pacific centre of origin, which may be linked to e.g. ocean currents or artificial dispersal agents.
L44@P1: This knowledge can in turn broaden our appreciation of world-wide algal distribution
L45@P1: patterns over geological time. Also, endemism rates can indicate evolutionary rates of algal
L46@P1: species. Secondly, the study of algal distribution patterns on islands can yield useful
L47@P1: information about the state of the particular reef systems they are associated with. Tropical
L48@P1: reefs are very delicate and balanced ecological systems, exhibiting surprising productivity in
L49@P1: otherwise nutrient-poor tropical oceanic waters. Peculiar macro-algal distribution patterns
L50@P1: (e.g. sudden algal blooms) often reflect damaged or polluted reef environments. Phycological
L51@P1: knowledge is therefore one of the essential prerequisites when undertaking such activities as
L52@P1: environmental impact assessments or other baseline ecological surveys. Thirdly, there is an
L53@P1: intrinsic value in any inventory of a new flora, as it contributes to a documentation of the
L54@P1: world's biota.
L55@P1: Rotuma Island is the most northerly island in the Fijian Island group and has been
L56@P1: neglected in surveys of the Fijian algal flora. Few aspects of the island have received any
L57@P1: scientific attention. Snow (1969) published a bibliography of Fiji, Tonga and Rotuma which
L58@P1: includes several historical, sociological, and scientific entries for Rotuma. To the author's
L59@P1: knowledge, however, no phycological survey of the algal flora of the island has yet been
L60@P1: undertaken, despite fairly extensive surveys of nearby Fiji. The only previous records of
L61@P1: Rotuman algae that could be traced by the author are two collections of Meristotheca by W.
L62@P1: E. Booth in October, 1975 and August, 1977, housed in the phycological herbarium at the
L63@P1: Bernice P. Bishop Museum, Honolulu, Hawaii (F. M. Norris, pers. comm.).
L64@P1: Owing to its isolation from the rest of the Fiji group (and for that matter any other island
L65@P1: group), it could be expected that there might be noticeable differences in the macroalgal
L66@P1: species composition and distribution of that island. Being 6" north of Fiji, Rotuma also
L67@P1: possesses a more equatorial climate than the remainder of the island group, leading to
L68@P1: interesting speculations about floristic comparisons with the rest of Fiji and the Pacific in
L69@P1: general. In addition, the fringing Rotuman reefs are essentially non-polluted, and a study of
L70@P1: these could offer a useful means of comparison with other reef systems in the region. In
L71@P1: order to address some of the above questions, it was decided to conduct a phycological
L72@P1: survey of Rotuma and compile a preliminary checklist, as well as to make observations on
L73@P1: the local habitats, species composition and distributions, and ecological interactions on the
L74@P1: island's reefs and other marine habitats.
L75@P1: Historical Background
L76@P1: The earliest reports of marine benthic algae from the Fijian island group were by Gmnow
L77@P1: (1874), who listed 37 species from Ovalau. In 1876, Dickie listed nine species, including
L78@P1: four new records, from the same locality. Askenasy (1888) published a list of 10 species
L79@P1: from Matuku Island collected during the H.M.S. 'Gazelle' Expeditions. It was almost 85
L80@P1: years later before Chapman (1971) published a checklist of some 79 species for Fiji, to
L81@P1: which he added a further 17 in 1977. Further additions to the Fijian flora were published by
L82@P1: Kapraun and Bowden (1978), and the early history of algal collecting in Fiji, together with
L83@P1: biogeographic and ecological aspects, was reviewed by McRaild (1978). Five years later,
L84@P1: Booth et al. (1983) made some preliminary attempts at culturing Eucheuma striatum in Fiji.
L85@P1: In 1984, Itono and Ajisaka published taxonomic notes on the Fijian marine algae, while
L86@P1: Itono (1985a, 1985b) described some Fijian Rhodophyta. In 1984, Kraft documented a deepwater form of Callophycus serratus from Fiji. The most detailed recent reports of Fiji algae
L87@P1: have been by Kasahara (1985, 1988), who studied collections made by two Kagoshima Maru
L88@P1: Expeditions to Fiji in 1982 as part of a scientific survey of the South Pacific by Kagoshima
L89@P1: University in Japan, as well as extensive collections that he made on Viti Levu. Kasahara's
L90@P1: 1985 thesis listed 76 Chlorophyta and Rhodophyta (including 37 new records), while his
L91@P1: profusely illustrated 1988 report listed 43 Chlorophyta. Unfortunately, he did not publish his
L92@P1: Phaeophyta collections, although duplicates were donated to the South Pacific Regional
L93@P1: Herbarium and are awaiting identification. In 1991, Garbary et al. listed many new
L94@P1: distribution records for Fijian algae (82 species listed, based on collections made in August
L95@P1-2: 1980, August 1981 and February 1982). Later that same year, South (1991) published a Benthic Marine Algae of Rotuma Island 363
L96@P2: checklist of marine benthic algae from Dravuni Island, in the Astrolabe Reef region, listing a
L97@P2: total of 71 species, 5 of which were new records for Fiji. South (1992) also published an
L98@P2: illustrated catalogue of the Halimeda species reported from Fiji, and in 1993 reported on the
L99@P2: edible seaweeds of these islands. The most comprehensive overview of Fijian algae to date is
L100@P2: the 1992 checklist by South and Kasahara, listing a total of 314 species including 11
L101@P2: Cyanophyceae, 99 Chlorophyceae, 36 Phaeophyceae and 168 Rhodophyceae. Since then,
L102@P2: further additions to the flora were made by a number of researchers (Raj 1993; South 1993;
L103@P2: South and N'Yeurt 1993; South et al. 1993; N'Yeurt et al. 1995, 1996a, 1996b), bringing the
L104@P2: total Fijian macroalgal flora (including Rotuma) to 422 taxa. In addition, recent visits by
L105@P2: D. S., and M. M. Littler, from the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, DC., to the Great
L106@P2: Astrolabe Reef, Kadavu, have yielded many new records of benthic algae that await
L107@P2: publication. However, most of the Fijian algal flora is still poorly known, as much of the Lau
L108@P2: Group, Kadavu, Lomaiviti Group, Yasawas, Taveuni and Vanua Levu have yet to be
L109@P2: investigated in detail.
L110@P2: Materials and Methods
L111@P2: Field Survey Methods
L112@P2: An initial survey of the island was carried out, from January to May 1992. A further survey was
L113@P2: conducted from December 1992 to February 1993, with additional specimens collected between
L114@P2: September 1993 and January 1994, and between November 1994 and January 1995.
L115@P2: A preliminary visual survey of the coastline was carried out, and 19 representative sites around the
L116@P2: island were chosen for inspection (Fig. 1). Surveys were carried out either by wading or snorkelling.
L117@P2: Considering the sparse distribution of the algae, and the turbulent and exposed conditions on the often
L118@P2: very narrow fringing reefs, it was deemed impractical to lay transects. Instead, the area was visually
L119@P2: inspected for algal growth, with salient distribution and reef profile features being recorded on an
L120@P2: underwater writing board. Owing to the exposed nature of the reefs and the lack of diving facilities and
L121@P2: trained partners, it was considered dangerous to sample subtidal habitats. Consequently, all collections
L122@P2: were from the intertidal region, imposing obvious limitations on the overall results obtained.
L123@P2: Photographs of algae in situ and of the general habitats were taken using an Olympus 35 mm camera
L124@P2: and a Pentax SLR variable-focus camera. Algal specimens were hand-collected or scraped off the
L125@P2: substratum using a knife, put into polyethylene plastic bags or vials, promptly preserved in 5%
L126@P2: formaldehyde in seawater, and stored in light-proof plastic containers for eventual shipment back to Fiji.
L127@P2: Laboratoiy Methods
L128@P2: Hand-sections or freezing-microtome sections were made for general observation of internal
L129@P2: anatomy. Slides were stained with either crystal violet or 1% acidified aniline blue, and made
L130@P2: permanent if necessary by embedding in glycerine jelly (Drury et al. 1967) following impregnation of
L131@P2: the material in 50% glycerol water solution for 2-3 h. Slides were examined using a Zeiss compound
L132@P2: microscope. A camera-lucida attachment (Abb6 Drawing Tube, Carl Zeiss) was used to make pencil
L133@P2: drawings of the specimens. Observations on larger specimens and general sorting of the collections
L134@P2: were performed using a Labova dissecting microscope. Slides are housed in the South Pacific Regional
L135@P2: Herbarium in a numbered series prefixed by 'S' (slide).
L136@P2: Voucher specimens of larger algae were first dried using an algal press, then mounted onto
L137@P2: herbarium paper. Specimens were deposited into the South Pacific Regional Herbarium housed at the
L138@P2: University of the South Pacific in Suva. Collection numbers are preceded by 'USP'. Ektachromes and
L139@P2: black-and-white photographs of identified algae were obtained in the laboratory, either using formalinpreserved or pressed specimens. Macrophotography was undertaken with a Nikon F2A using a 55 mm
L140@P2: 1 : 3.5 lens, and a ring flash (Sunpak GX8R ring flash attachment, Sunpak Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) in
L141@P2: conjunction with Kodak EClOO (Ektachromes) or Kodak PX125 (Black-and-white) film.
L142@P2: Photomicrographs were taken with a Zeiss Photoscope I11 (*I S = slide collections. Accession numbers
L143@P2: refer to specimens housed at the Phycological Herbarium, South Pacific Regional Herbarium, The
L144@P2: University of the South Pacific (SUVA) and are preceded by 'USP' to distinguish them from the
L145@P2: angiosperm collection).

L151@P3: recent (1992) handbook on Australian Great Barrier Reef turf Rhodophyta proved to be a very useful

L162@P3: temperate species are often used that are subsequently found to have been wrongly applied.
L163@P3: Study Site
L164@P3: Physiography
L165@P3: The island of Rotuma (12'29's and 177'05'E) is situated about 465 km north-west of Cikobia, the
L166@P3: northern-most island in the Fiji group. It is fairly isolated from other island groups in the central South
L167@P3: Pacific (Fig. 2). Rotuma lies in a general depth of 3.7 km on a plateau that includes the Tongan and Fiji
L168@P3: groups. The island itself, however, is a totally separate basaltic shield volcano edifice of late Pleistocene
L169@P3: age (about 0.2 Ma) situated on a 200 km2 submarine bank of Pleistocene or earlier limestone which in
L170@P3: turn lies over a former volcanic edifice of Tertiary age (Woodhall 1987). The submarine bank (remnants
L171@P3: of a former atoll) results in fairly shallow coastal waters (3746 m) seaward of the fringing reef (Fiji
L172@P3: Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries 1983). There are no real lagoons, although in two places (Maka
L173@P3: Bay and Hapmafau Bay) there are shallow areas of relatively calm waters inside a reef approaching the
L174@P3: barrier class.
L175@P3: The island is of more recent volcanic origin than the nearby Fiji group, which dates from the late
L176@P3: Eocene (Menard and Hamilton 1963). The most recent Rotuman lavas are about 15000 years old, and
L177@P3: the island has a basaltic rocky core covered with rich volcanic soil (Gardiner 1898). Occupying about
L178@P3: 44 km2, the main island is about 14.5 km long and 4.5 km wide at its largest point. It is surrounded by
L179@P3: nine small islets (Fig. 3), five of which occur on the surrounding reef (Hauati'u; Hauamea'me'a;
L180@P3: Solnohu; Solkope; Afgaha). Of the remaining four, two have no reefs (Uea (77 ha; 262 m high; cliffbound); Hofliua (< 1 km2; 58 m high; volcanic; cliff-bound)) and the rest (Hatana (4 ha; 18.3 m high;
L181@P3: volcanic); Hofhavunglola (small islet)) share the same reef platform. They are important nesting sites
L182@P3: for seabirds (Booby (Sula sp.); Noddy Tern (Anous sp.)). The coastline is about 39.6 km long and backs
L183@P3: a reef area estimated to be about 16 km2. The majority of the reef is fringing, with a maximum width of
L184@P3: 1.5 km at Noa'tau on the eastern coast and a minimum of less than 100 m wide between Halafa and
L185@P3: Maftoa, and Losa and 'Anmosega Point. The highest peak (on Uea Island) reaches about 262 m. There
L186@P3: are no real rivers on the island, although two perennial streams occur on Uea Island. A small stream
L187@P3: flowing from the crater of the Solele cone has carved a shallow valley 10-15 m deep west of Motusa
L188@P3: (Woodhall 1987). Rotuma consists of two distinct parts (east and west) joined by a narrow, sandy,
L189@P3: vegetated isthmus at Motusa (possibly human-made over an intervening reef, according to Rotuman
L190@P3: tradition supported by geological data (Gardiner 1898; Woodhall 1987)). An extensive submerged sand
L191@P3: and coral bank extends approximately 8 km from Malhaha towards the north-west, and a submarine
L192@P3: bank (Whale Bank) approximately 5 X 1.5 km with a mean depth of 30-33 m lies to the west (Fiji
L193@P3: Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries 1983).
L194@P3: A noticeable feature of the island is its remarkably regular U-shaped range of hills (Fig. 4), which
L195@P3: reaches a maximum elevation of 262 m. The hills either have a flat depression at the top or rise into a
L196@P3: ridge, although one (Mt Satarua) has characters of both (Gardiner 1898). The soil at the foot of these hills
L197@P3: is extremely rich and arable, which accounts for the Rotuman custom of locating plantations on hill slopes.
L198@P3: Everywhere there are medium to large blocks of a very vesicular lava, which among other things are used
L199@P3: by Rotumans to make the ubiquitous pig fences around the island. There are about 14 coastal villages
L200@P3: circling the island, with a total population of approximately 3000. Most of the surrounding islets are
L201@P3: volcanic in origin, and while they have a gentle slope towards the mainland, their seaward face often
L202@P3: consists of precipitous cliffs above a narrow fringing reef 10-20 m broad (e.g. Hauatia Island). Freshwater
L203@P3: seeps on the beach from the underground water table occur in numerous places around the coast.
L204@P3: There are no large marine swamps or coastal mangroves on Rotuma, although shallow subtidal
L205@P3: seagrass beds (Syringodium isoetifoliurn (Ascherson) Dandy) exist at one site (Maka Bay). The only
L206@P3: mangroves on the island are found at Paptea on the east coast, although the small swamp is located
L207@P3: close to pig sties about 300 m inland and consists of Bruguiera gymnorhiza trees intermixed with
L208@P3: coconut palms (Cocos nucifera) and other vegetation. The water level can rise up to 30 cm at high tide,
L209@P3-4: and the substratum is sandy to muddy. Seawater appears to seep in underneath the intervening village Benthic Marine Algae of Rotuma Island 365
L210@P4: and road to reach the swamp, which occurs in depressions at or below sea-level. This observation seems
L211@P4: to contradict an earlier report by Dunlap and Singh (1980) that mangroves do not occur on Rotuma,
L212@P4: although perhaps the authors were referring only to coastal mangrove habitats.
L213@P4: The island is 6" north of Fiji and is noticeably warmer. July temperatures rarely drop below 25OC,
L214@P4: and daytime temperatures can rise up to 35°C. Water temperature ranges from 26-32°C (pers. obs.).
L215@P4: The prevailing winds are east to south except during December, January, February and March, in these
L216@P4: months, they vary from north to west (Gardiner 1898). A west-north-west current at approximately
L217@P4: 1 kph surrounds the island (Fiji Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries 1983). Rain is frequent and
L218@P4: regular (weekly; about 3550 mm yrl; Woodhall 1987) and hence droughts are uncommon. Hurricanes
L219@P4: which normally hit neighbouring Fiji every 3 years or so rarely affect Rotuma, the last major hurricane
L220@P4: to hit the island being Bebe in 1972 which caused widespread destruction.
L221@P4: Reef Structure
L222@P4: The reefs are in most cases fringing except in two places (Maka and Hapmafau Bays), where they
L223@P4: approach the barrier class and enclose a shallow lagoon. The reefs are between 100-1500 m wide and
L224@P4: can be quite exposed at low tide, drying between 0.6 to 1.5 m at each new and full moons (when tidal
L225@P4: ranges are greatest). There are no mudflats or coastal marine mangrove areas on Rotuma, but Maka Bay
L226@P4: has extensive seagrass beds of Syringodium isoetifolium and a somewhat muddier substratum owing to
L227@P4: its lagoon and relative protection from wave action leading to sediment accumulation. At the other
L228@P4: extreme, Lopta reef is probably the narrowest on the island, being only 60-100 m wide in places and
L229@P4: considerably exposed. Consequently, algal habitats in the various Rotuman reefs differ according to
L230@P4: their extent and degree of exposure. Among the main constituents of the reefs are Acropora spp., except
L231@P4: at Maka Bay and 'Ahau where brittle Pavona decussata Dana rubble is dominant. The latter species of
L232@P4: coral forms an almost continuous pavement on the outer reef at Maka Bay.
L233@P4: Dominant Species
L234@P4: A distinct north-south disjunct of algal distribution was observed on the island, chiefly a result of
L235@P4: the different habitats on opposite coasts (fairly deep and sandy on the north, more rocky and exposed at
L236@P4: low tide in the south). Typical and dominant northern species include a wide range of Halimeda and
L237@P4: Caulerpa spp., and widespread plants of Melanamansia glomerata that inhabit rubble of Acropora sp.
L238@P4: on reefs from Mea to Oinafa. In the south, however, only one species of Halimeda (H. opuntia) was
L239@P4: dominant, the rest of the species being chiefly small rhodophytes (Heterosiphonia, Ceramiales such as
L240@P4: Herposiphonia) although the larger red alga, Meristotheca procumbens, was fairly abundant on the
L241@P4: south-west coast. The eastern and western stations exhibited characters from both northern and southern
L242@P4: habitats. The two small lagoons on the island had characteristic floras associated with their relatively
L243@P4: calm waters. Maka Bay on the north coast was dominated by Sargassum polycystum, Rhodymenia
L244@P4: divaricata, Laurencia sp., Gracilaria sp., and Enteromorpha jlexuosa; while Hapmafau Bay on the
L245@P4: south of the Motusa isthmus was dominated by Caulerpa racemosa, Caulerpa serrulata and
L246@P4: Chlorodesmis major.
