L27@P1: Bushy shrub to 3 m tall. Branches erect, old stems brown or grey; branchlets green, pubescent or glabrous, hairs
L28@P1: bifarious or uniform; internodes (2-) 5-41 mm; leaf decurrencies obscure or weakly evident (with a faint ridge along
L29@P1: medial line). Leaf bud distinct; sinus absent. Leaves erecto-patent to recurved; lamina lanceolate or linear or oblong
L30@P1: or oblanceolate or elliptic (often narrowly), coriaceous, m-shaped in transverse section, (23-) 45-110 (-163) x (5-)
L31@P1: 9-22 (-32) mm; apex acute to obtuse or apiculate or sometimes acuminate; base cuneate or truncate;
L32@P1: brochidodromous secondary veins sometimes evident in fresh leaves; margin narrowly cartilaginous, ciliolate or
L33@P1: glabrous; upper surface green or dark green, usually glossy, without evident or rarely with few stomata, hairy along
L34@P1: midrib (usually) or glabrous; lower surface light green. Juvenile leaves crenate, ciliolate (and with scattered hairs
L35@P1: above midrib). Inflorescences with (13-) 25-85 flowers, lateral, unbranched, 3-13.2 cm, shorter to longer than
L36@P1: subtending leaves; peduncle 0.6-1.9 (-3.6) cm; rachis (2-) 3-11.3 cm. Bracts alternate (apart from lowermost pair in
L37@P1: most cases), lanceolate or deltoid (sometimes narrowly) or oblong, obtuse to acute or acuminate. Flowers
L38@P1: hermaphrodite. Pedicels 1.5-5.5 mm, sometimes recurved in fruit. Calyx (2-) 2.5-3.7 (-4.2) mm; lobes lanceolate or
L39@P1: elliptic or ovate or deltoid, acute to obtuse, very rarely hairy outside. Corolla lube hairy inside, (2.2-) 3.2-5.5 x
L40@P1: 2.8-4.2 mm, funnel form and contracted at base, at least slightly longer than calyx; lobes white or tinged with pink
L41@P1: or mauve at anthesis, white with age, ovate or elliptic, obtuse, erect to patent (usually only posterior lobe patent),
L42@P1: shorter to longer than corolla tube, sometimes ciliolate or hairy inside; corolla throat white or violet. Stamen
L43@P1: filaments white, 5.5-12.2 mm; anthers mauve or pink or violet or yellow, 2.3-3 mm. Ovary very rarely hairy,
L44@P1: 1-1.6 mm; ovules approximately 8-10 per locule; style 5-11.5 mm. Capsules acute or subacute, 3.8-10 x 3-6.5 mm,
L45@P1: loculicidal split extending ¼-½-way to base. Seeds flattened (sometimes strongly), broad ellipsoid to discoid,
L46@P1: winged, more or less smooth, brown (sometimes pale), 1-2.5 (-3.2) x 0.9-1.7 mm, micropylar rim 0.2-0.6 mm.
L47@P1: SIMILAR TAXA
L48@P1: Distinguished from most large-leaved “Occlusae” (see Bayly & Kellow 2006) by its: leathery leaves; large, broad
L49@P1: flowers, with corolla tubes longer than calyces, and long filaments; and large fruit.
L50@P1: Generally, V. var. macrocarpa is distinguished from V. var. latisepala by having white flowers, compared to the
L51@P1: mauve or violet flowers of the latter (see further notes below).
L52@P1: V. macrocarpa is possibly closely related to, and may grade into V. corriganii (see notes under that species). It
L53@P1: probably hybridises with V. stricta var. stricta at a range of sites, and the name Hebe xaffinis probably applies to
L54@P1: this hybrid combination.
L55@P1: DISTRIBUTION
L56@P1: North Island, from near Whangarei to near Kawhia, including islands of Hauraki Gulf and the Mercury Islands.
L57@P1: HABITAT
L58@P1: It occurs in coastal to upland areas, in scrub, at forest margins or in open areas in forest, and on rocky sites

L66@P1-2: Panoxis macrocarpa (Vahl) Raf., Hebe macrocarpa (Vahl) Cockayne et Allan var. macrocarpaTAXONOMIC NOTES
L67@P2: Moore (in Allan 1961) recognised three varieties of V. macrocarpa. One of these is treated by Bayly & Kellow (2006)
L68@P2: as a distinct species, V. punicea. The other two, var. macrocarpa and var. latisepala, are probably worthy of
L69@P2: recognition (and are treated by some authors, e.g. Druce 1980, 1993, as distinct species). It has not, however, been
L70@P2: possible to delimit these varieties to an extent that they could each be mapped and described separately by Bayly &
L71@P2: Kellow (2006), var. macrocarpa, as traditionally defined, has while flowers, 2n=80 chromosomes and occurs on the
L72@P2: North Island mainland, including the Coromandel Peninsula. var. latisepala, as traditionally defined, has violet
L73@P2: flowers, 2n=120 chromosomes and occurs on Great Barrier and Little Barrier islands, and possibly near Whangarei
L74@P2: Harbour and on Coromandel Peninsula. The two varieties are difficult to discriminate, particularly using herbarium
L75@P2: specimens because: they are variable in leaf shape and size; specimens do not always have flowers (and colour
L76@P2: may not always be retained); and the true limits of the chromosome races are unknown. Their discrimination is
L77@P2: further confused because the geographic and morphological boundaries are not as clear-cut as outlined by Moore
L78@P2: (in Allan 1961). For example, white-flowered plants occur on Great Barrier Island (M. Bayly personal observation),
L79@P2: some of which have 2n=80 chromosomes (de Lange & Murray 2002); plants with 2n=120 occur on Coromandel
L80@P2: Peninsula (de Lange & Murray 2002) and the Hunua Ranges (Hair 1967), the former at least with white flowers; both
L81@P2: white-flowered and violet-flowered plants with 2n=120 are sympatric with white-flowered plants with 2n = 80 on
L82@P2: Bream Head; violet-flowered plants with 2n=120 are sympatric with white-flowered plants with 2n = 80 on Mount
L83@P2: Manaia (P. J. de Lange pers. comm. 2005). The limits of the two varieties are worthy of further investigation.
L84@P2: Included in Bayly & Kellow (2006) under V. macrocarpa are narrow-leaved plants from Great Barrier Island
L85@P2: considered by Druce (1980, as H. “sp. (w)”; 1993, as H. “Great Barrier”) to constitute an undescribed species, and by
L86@P2: de Lange & Murray (2002) possibly to be hybrids between V. macrocarpa and V. pubescens subsp. rehuarum.
L87@P2: There are no clear grounds for either treating them as a distinct species or as hybrids.

L100@P2: ETYMOLOGY
L101@P2: veronica: Named after Saint Veronica, who gave Jesus her veil to wipe his brow as he carried the cross through
L102@P2: Jerusalem, perhaps because the common name of this plant is ‘speedwell’. The name Veronica is often believed to
L103@P2: derive from the Latin vera ‘truth’ and iconica ‘image’, but it is actually derived from the Macedonian name Berenice
L104@P2: which means ‘bearer of victory’.
