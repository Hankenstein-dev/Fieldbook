L3@P2: by JOHN H. ALEXANDER III
L4@P2: The lilac is a favorite flower in New England where it is the state
L5@P2: flower of New Hampshire. Its popularity also extends across the
L6@P2: northern half of the United States and into the southern half of
L7@P2: Canada. At the Arnold Arboretum, undoubtedly more visitors come
L8@P2: to view the lilacs than to visit any other single collection.
L9@P2: We have a special fondness for this plant which is as foreign to
L10@P2: our shores as our forefathers were. No species of lilac is native to the
L11@P2: New World; Syringa vulgaris, the common lilac, is indigenous to the
L12@P2: mountains of Europe within the countries of Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Roumania and Yugoslavia. But botanists did not
L13@P2: discover it growing wild until 1828 when it was found in western
L14@P2: Roumania. Cultivated for centuries in Turkey, it was brought from
L15@P2: there to Vienna by 1563 and was common in Paris by the turn of the
L16@P2: century. French, Dutch, and German nurserymen began selecting
L17@P2: superior seedlings and by the early 1800’s offered named clones for
L18@P2: sale. An example is the cultivar ’Lucie Baltet’ which originated in
L19@P2: France before 1888 and is still considered to be one of the best pinkflowered varieties. So many cultivars originated in French nurseries,
L20@P2: and in particular at the nursery of Victor Lemoine, that all selections
L21@P2: of Syringa vulgaris have commonly been known as French Hybrid
L22@P2: Lilacs.
L23@P2: It is these French Hybrid Lilacs that we particularly love. How
L24@P2: many of us can walk by a lilac bush in bloom and not bend close
L25@P2: for a draught of perfume, the mind conjuring up memories of springs
L26@P2: past? With the advent of the smaller home, this plant, so deeply
L27@P2: rooted in tradition, has been put aside for the contractor’s Taxus.
L28@P2: Not only the lilac’s size, but the inferior quality of late summer foliage and the profuse suckering tendencies of some cultivars have discouraged many people from growing them. But now lilacs other than
L29@P2: the common Syringa vulgaris and its cultivars are being discovered
L30@P2: by gardeners. Many of these uncommon lilacs are smaller, some even
L31@P2: small enough to be tubbed for the patio, and most sucker little if at
L32@P2: all. They also are more resistant to mildew and leaf roll-necrosis.
L33@P2-3: Leaf roll-necrosis is typified by the interveinal and marginal chlorosis or yellowing of the leaf, and in severe cases by the inward curling66
L34@P3: of the leaf so that the margins actually touch. A product of our urban
L35@P3: environment, the disorder is believed to be caused by a combination
L36@P3: of air pollutants. (6) Since different pollutants are found in varying
L37@P3: quantities in different environments, conclusive evidence of resistance
L38@P3: is not available. My statements of implied resistance to mildew and
L39@P3: leaf roll-necrosis are based on observations at Rochester, New York
L40@P3: (5) and on observations by the staff and volunteers of the Arnold
L41@P3: Arboretum.
L42@P3: With few exceptions the lilacs mentioned here may be seen in the
L43@P3: collection of the Arnold Arboretum. Most are available in the nursery
L44@P3: trade. The best source list is available from the International Lilac
L45@P3: Society, an organization of amateur and professional lilac enthusiasts.
L46@P3: Requests for information on publications and membership should be
L47@P3: addressed to Mr. Walter W. Oakes, Secretary, International Lilac Society, P.O. Box 315, Rumford, Me. 04276.
L48@P3: Syringa X chinensis
L49@P3: S. X chinensis is a garden hybrid that originated in the Botanic
L50@P3: Garden at Rouen, France, about 1777. Its parents are generally believed to be S. laciniata and S. vulgaris. A beautiful shrubby plant,
L51@P3: it can reach a height of 15 feet but is more likely to be 8 to 10 feet
L52@P3: tall. Its flowers are lilac-colored and somewhat smaller than those
L53@P3: of the common lilac, with which it blooms. An attractive advantage
L54@P3: is that it produces clusters of flowers not only at the terminals, but
L55@P3: from lateral buds down the stems, often forming an inflorescence 2
L56@P3: or more feet long.
L57@P3: Syringa X chinensis suckers only enough to be considered multistemmed and is certainly not a nuisance. The foliage is similar to
L58@P3: that of the common lilac, but smaller and appears to be resistant to
L59@P3: leaf roll-necrosis, although it is susceptible to mildew. Appearing in
L60@P3: late summer, mildew does no serious damage to the plant and might
L61@P3: well be tolerated in exchange for the great spring beauty and profuse
L62@P3: flowering habits of this hybrid.
L63@P3: Syringa X chinensis has proved itself to be hardy in Zone II. (3)
L64@P3: Two forms also are available: alba, white, and sangeana, lilac-red.

L87@P5: weeks after those of the common lilac. S. josikaea is a very hardy
L88@P5: plant, even to Zone II. (3) It grows to 12 feet, and has an upright,

L90@P5: Prunus. They are resistant to mildew and leaf roll-necrosis.

L99@P5: This multi-stemmed but non-suckering shrub is usually a 6- to 8-
L100@P5: foot mound of slender branches. Hardy to at least Zone V, it blooms
L101@P5: with the common lilac. Unlike S. vulgaris, its pale-lilac panicles appear from lateral as well as terminal buds, making the flower clusters
L102@P5: appear much larger. The small leaves are not troubled by mildew,
L103@P5: but have exhibited slight leaf roll-necrosis.
L104@P5: The cultivar ’Hers Variety’ has gracefully arching branches that
L105@P5: are more weeping than those of any other lilac. This cultivar, when

L109@P6: Formerly known as S. persica var. laciniata, the Cutleaf Lilac is
L110@P6: now considered to be a true species, and S. X persica a hybrid. The
L111@P6: leaves of S. laciniata are deeply lobed and present a dainty, feathery
L112@P6: appearance; they also are resistant to mildew and leaf roll-necrosis.
L113@P6: This Chinese native is hardy to Zone V and forms a graceful
L114@P6: many-stemmed, non-suckering 6-foot mound of a shrub. Pale lilac
L115@P6: flowers are produced in season with S. vulgaris. Because they break
L116@P6: from lateral and terminal buds, the small clusters of flowers often
L117@P6: cover 18 or more inches of the branch tips.
L118@P6: The unique foliage, attractive flowers and graceful habit make this
L119@P6: lilac a favorite of all who know it.
L120@P6: Syringa meyeri
L121@P6: S. meyeri was unknown to the western world until 1908 when F. N.
L122@P6: Meyer of the U.S.D.A. found it being cultivated in China. Purple- lilac flowers grace this handsome lilac that blooms in season with S.
L123@P6: vulgaris, but unfortunately lacks its fragrance. The foliage is small,
L124@P6: less than 2 inches long, and is not troubled by mildew or leaf rollnecrosis. A multi-stemmed, compact shrub not usually more than 6
L125@P6: feet tall and as broad, it is hardy to Zone II. (3) Specimens at the
L126@P6-7: Morden Arboretum, Morden, Manitoba, are given a hardiness rating of 9 and 9.5 on a scale of 10.70 I
L127@P7: The correct name of a cultivar of S. meyeri known in the nursery
L128@P7: trade as S. palibiniana, and "Dwarf Korean Lilac," (but in reality
L129@P7: from China) has been the subject of much confusion. Consternation
L130@P7: regarding the correct name of this cultivar prompted me to write
L131@P7: Peter S. Green, Keeper of the Herbarium, Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew,

L141@P7: be to graft it high on a standard. Potted up, this lilac might well

L143@P7: of leaves or a mound of wood chips protecting the root ball probably
L144@P7: would be sufficient. If your climate is colder, or if your patio is a
L145@P7: raised wooden deck where the root ball would be exposed to cold air

L148@P7: Syringa microphylla
L149@P7: S. microphylla is a most handsome small shrub; its height is usually 6 feet and it may grow to be twice as wide. The ovate, mildewresistant leaves are usually less than 2 inches long, giving the plant
L150@P7: its common name, the Littleleaf Lilac. Unfortunately, the leaves
L151@P7: are somewhat susceptible to leaf roll-necrosis. The flowers of the
L152@P7: species are a light pink or pale lilac color. They open at the same
L153@P7: time as the common lilac and frequently rebloom in August or September. This second bloom is somewhat sparse and not really significant
L154@P7: in the landscape, but in late summer it is most enjoyable to have a
L155@P7: vase full of these fragrant lilac blossoms gracing the dinner table.
L156@P7: The deep pink flowers of the cultivar ’Superba’ are even more effective, but its foliage seems more likely to display leaf roll-necrosis than
L157@P7: the species. S. microphylla has proved itself hardy in Zone III and
L158@P7: survives in Zone II. (3)

L223@P11: Syringa X persica
L224@P11: S. X persica is a handsome, many-stemmed shrub about 6 feet in
L225@P11: height and as much or more in width. Never found in the wild, it is
L226@P11: apparently a hybrid that perhaps originated in the gardens of Persia,
L227@P11: where it was cultivated for centuries. S. laciniata, which was formerly known as S. persica var. laciniata, is now considered to be one
L228@P11: of the parents. The many small clusters of lilac flowers originate
L229@P11: from both terminal and lateral buds forming a huge inflorescence
L230@P11: that may extend 18 or more inches from the branch tip. These
L231@P11: flowers are produced so abundantly that they weigh down the branches
L232@P11: into graceful arches. The Persian Lilac blooms about the same time
L233@P11: as the common lilac, but lacks the pleasant fragrance of the latter.
L234@P11: S. X persica is hardy to Zone II (3) and has shown good resistance
L235@P11: to mildew and leaf roll-necrosis. The foliage and flowers are both
L236@P11: small, but the number of flowers and the graceful appearance of this
L237@P11: shrub make it easy to see why it is as valuable to today’s gardens as
L238@P11: it was to those of long ago.
