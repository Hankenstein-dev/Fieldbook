L154: Thapsia villosa - cite74†MHNT L155: Thapsia villosa, commonly known as the villous deadly carrot, is a cite75†species of cite76†poisonous cite77†herbaceous cite62†plants in the cite78†genus cite69†Thapsia . It grows to about 70 to 190 cm (28 to 75 in) in height. It has cite79†pinnate hairy leaves with sheath-like cite80†petioles . The flowers are yellow in color and borne on compound cite81†umbels . They develop into fruits with four wings characteristic of the genus.
L156: It is native to southwestern cite82†Europe and northwestern cite83†Africa surrounding the cite84†Mediterranean Sea . The plant was used extensively for cite85†traditional medicine since around the 3rd century BC.
L157: ## Taxonomy
L158: 
L159: [cite86†edit ]
L160: 
L161: Thapsia villosa was first described by cite71†Carl Linnaeus in cite87†Species Plantarum (1753).^{cite88†[2] } It is classified under the cite78†genus cite69†Thapsia , cite89†subfamily cite90†Apioideae , of the cite91†carrot and cite92†parsley cite93†family cite68†Apiaceae .^{cite94†[3] } Two cite95†varieties are recognized: T. villosa var. villosa and T. villosa var. dissecta.^{cite96†[4] }
L162: The cite78†generic name Thapsia is derived from the cite97†Ancient Greek name θαψία (thapsía) for the members of the genus. The Greeks believe it to have originated from ancient cite98†Thapsos in cite99†Sicily . The cite100†specific epithet is from cite101†Latin cite102†villōsus†en.wiktionary.org ("hairy" or "shaggy").^{cite96†[4] }^{cite103†[5] }
L163: It is commonly known in English as the 'cite104†villous†en.wiktionary.org deadly carrot' or simply 'deadly carrot'.^{cite105†[6] }^{cite106†[7] } It is known under a large number of common names in other languages, including bu-nefar in cite107†Arabic ; zumillo and cañaheja in cite108†Spanish ; baladra in cite109†Catalan ; croca in cite110†Majorcan ; turbit-da-terra in cite111†Portuguese ; bermidor in cite112†Basque ; turbith bâtard in cite113†French ; and turbito falso in cite114†Italian .^{cite88†[2] }^{cite115†[8] }
L164: ## Description
L165: 
L166: [cite116†edit ]
L167: 
L168: Thapsia villosa is a cite117†perennial cite77†herb growing to a height of 70 to 190 cm (28 to 75 in). It has a robust and smooth tapering cite118†stem about 5 to 25 mm (0.20 to 0.98 in) in diameter, arising from a thick root resembling a white cite91†carrot or cite119†turnip .^{cite103†[5] }^{cite120†[9] }
L169: 
L170: cite79†Pinnate leaves and sheath-like cite80†petioles of T. villosa
L171: The leaves, like the name of the species suggests, are hairy. The leaves around the base of the stem have well-developed sheath-like cite80†petioles about 20 to 80 mm (0.79 to 3.15 in) wide. Like the leaves, they are also hairy. They are whitish, yellowish, green, or (rarely) purple in color. The leaf blades are triangular to deltoid in shape and are cite79†pinnate , subdivided once to thrice. The last cite121†leaflet subdivision is usually about 8 to 32 mm (0.31 to 1.26 in) wide in T. villosa var.
L172: villosa; while they are only about 1.5 to 3 mm (0.059 to 0.118 in) wide in T. villosa var. dissecta.^{cite96†[4] } The margins of the leaves are curled (cite122†revolute ) with small teeth-like and triangular serrated edges, each tipped with small spike.
L173: The main leaf cite123†midrib (cite124†rachis ) is densely covered in hair and light to dark green on the upper side, while it is smooth and grayish to greenish on the underside.^{cite96†[4] } The leaves are largest at the bottom and progressively become smaller the higher they are on the stem.^{cite120†[9] } In the upper parts of the stem, the leaves are often reduced to nothing more than petioles which form sheaths around the stem.^{cite125†[10] }
L174: Like other members of the genus, the cite126†inflorescence of Thapsia villosa is a compound subhemispherical to globose cite81†umbel . It has 9 to 29 spokes and is about 6 to 12 cm (2.4 to 4.7 in) in diameter. cite127†Bracts are usually absent, though in rare cases, one to three may be present, each around 6 to 40 mm (0.24 to 1.57 in) in length and lanceolate in shape. The cite128†umbellules are hemispherical to globose. cite129†Bracteoles are also usually absent, though rarely 1 or 2 may be present.
L175: Each umbellule has about 18 to 43 flowers, each with five bright yellow elliptical petals. The fruits are oblong and around 9 to 15 mm (0.35 to 0.59 in) long and 6 to 11 mm (0.24 to 0.43 in) wide. When mature, they are light to dark brown in color with four yellowish to brownish wings characteristic of the genus.^{cite96†[4] }^{cite120†[9] }^{cite125†[10] } The main umbel has cite130†hermaphroditic flowers, containing both cite131†stamen and cite132†pistil .
L176: Smaller umbels may develop from the sides of the stem which usually contains only cite131†stamens . These umbels wither away quickly.^{cite103†[5] }
L177: Thapsia villosa is highly variable in morphology. The two varieties, T. villosa var. dissecta and T. villosa var. villosa are usually distinguished by how many times the leaf subdivides into leaflets. T. villosa var. dissecta has leaves that are deeply subdivided two or three times into narrow and small leaflets. T. villosa var. villosa, in contrast are usually subdivided only once or twice.
L178: However, there are numerous intermediate forms between the two varieties and distinguishing between them can be very difficult. The cite133†diploid chromosome number (2n) of Thapsia villosa is 22, 33, 44, or 66.^{cite96†[4] }
L179: ## Ecology
L180: 
L181: [cite134†edit ]
L182: 
L183: Flowers of an cite128†umbellule of Thapsia villosa Thapsia villosa in cite135†Matera , Italy
L184: 
L185: Unlike most members of the family Apiaceae, Thapsia villosa flowers early, during the months of May, June, and July. The flowers are cite136†pollinated by a variety of cite137†insects . The flowers develop into winged fruits that dry out around August, while other members of the family are usually still in bloom.^{cite103†[5] }^{cite120†[9] }
L186: ## Distribution and habitat
L187: 
L188: [cite138†edit ]
L189: 
L190: Thapsia villosa in bloom in cite139†Puertollano , cite140†Spain L191: The native range of Thapsia villosa extends from southern cite141†France and the cite142†Iberian Peninsula (cite140†Spain , cite143†Portugal , and cite144†Gibraltar ) to northwestern cite83†Africa (cite145†Morocco , cite146†Algeria , and cite147†Tunisia ).
L192: It has also been introduced into cite148†Corsica .^{cite96†[4] }^{cite149†[11] } They can be found growing at altitudes of 0 to 1,800 m (0 to 5,906 ft) cite150†above sea level in cite151†scrublands , clearings, and near cite152†recently disturbed land like roads or cultivated fields.^{cite96†[4] }
L193: In its native range, it is frequently confused with the similar-looking cite153†Ferula communis , the giant fennel. Thapsia villosa and Ferula communis share some common names in Spanish like cañaheja.^{cite103†[5] } Reports of Thapsia villosa in the cite154†Balearic Isles is likely the result of confusion with cite155†Thapsia gymnesica .^{cite96†[4] }

L196: [cite156†edit ]

L200: ## Uses
L201: 
L202: [cite161†edit ]
L203: ### Traditional medicine
L204: 
L205: [cite162†edit ]
L206: The medicinal properties of the members of the genus cite69†Thapsia were recognized as early 300 BC.^{cite163†[15] } In cite85†traditional medicine , the roots of Thapsia villosa were used as a cite164†purgative and cite165†emetic .
L207: cite166†Resin from the Thapsia villosa was used as a cite167†blister-producing agent (vesicant) or a cite168†counterirritant , similar to resin derived from cite169†Thapsia garganica .^{cite160†[14] }^{cite170†[16] } In cite140†Spain , the resin is also traditionally used in cite171†Segarra as treatment for cite172†scabies .^{cite96†[4] } cite173†Poultices made from root bark infused with oil was used as a pain-reliever and for treating cite174†rheumatism .
L208: These were also used in cite175†Salamanca for treating cracks in cite176†horse hooves .^{cite120†[9] }
L209: Traditional medicinal uses of the plant, however, are now considered largely ineffective and highly inadvisable due to the danger posed by the poisons of Thapsia villosa.^{cite103†[5] }^{cite120†[9] }^{cite177†[17] }
L210: ### Medical research
L211: 
L212: [cite178†edit ]
L213: 
L214: Along with cite169†Thapsia garganica and cite179†Thapsia transtagana , Thapsia villosa var. villosa contains C-19 cite180†terpenolides with strong cite181†SERCA -inhibiting properties cite182†in vitro . They are of interest to medical researchers for their potential in treating cite183†neurodegenerative disorders like cite184†Alzheimer's or cite185†Parkinson's disease .^{cite186†[18] }
L215: ### Other uses
L216: 
L217: [cite187†edit ]
L218: Poison from the roots of Thapsia villosa is traditionally used by fishermen in cite188†Catalonia as an cite189†ichthyotoxin for cite190†stunning fish , making them easier to catch.^{cite120†[9] }^{cite191†[19] } Extracts from the root bark of Thapsia villosa have been used since ancient times as a yellow cite192†dye .^{cite115†[8] } In the 18th century, yellow dye from the flowers were used to color cite193†wool .^{cite115†[8] }^{cite194†[20] } Thapsia villosa are also grown as cite195†ornamentals for their bright yellow flowers.^{cite196†[21] }
