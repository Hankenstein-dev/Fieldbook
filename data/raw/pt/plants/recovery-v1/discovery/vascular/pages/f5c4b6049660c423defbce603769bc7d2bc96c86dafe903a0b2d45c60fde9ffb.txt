L34: # Aloe candelabrum
L35: Taxonomy
L36: Scientific Name Aloe candelabrum A.Berger
L37: Higher Classification Monocotyledons
L38: Family ASPHODELACEAE
L39: National Status
L40: Status and Criteria Near Threatened B1ab(iii)
L41: Assessment Date 2019/03/12
L42: Assessor(s) H. Mtshali
L43: Justification This species has a restricted distribution range (EOO 14 786 km²), but is still fairly common, in spite of more than 50% historical habitat loss. It continues to decline due to ongoing habitat loss and degradation, and therefore nearly meets the criteria threshold for Vulnerable under Criterion B.
L44: Distribution
L45: Endemism South African endemic
L46: Provincial distribution Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal
L47: Range It is endemic to east-central KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, from Pietermaritzburg southwards to the Umtamvuna River.
L48: Habitat and Ecology
L49: Major system Terrestrial
L50: Major habitats Eastern Valley Bushveld, Ngongoni Veld, Midlands Mistbelt Grassland, Pondoland-Ugu Sandstone Coastal Sourveld, KwaZulu-Natal Coastal Belt Grassland, KwaZulu-Natal Sandstone Sourveld, KwaZulu-Natal Hinterland Thornveld, Mooi River Highland Grassland, Moist Coast Hinterland Grassland, Dry Coast Hinterland Grassland, Southern KwaZulu-Natal Moist Grassland, Scarp Forest
L51: Description It occurs in variable habitats, including rocky hill slopes, open and bushy areas.
L52: Threats
L53: About 50-56% of its habitat is already irreversibly modified, mainly through loss to sugarcane cultivation, timber plantations and urban expansion around Pietermaritzburg and Durban. There is ongoing habitat loss and degradation, particularly due to the expansion of rural settlements and subsistence agriculture, as well as spreading, unmanaged alien invasive plants.
L54: This species is also utilised for construction materials, and decline of two subpopulations, one in southern KwaZulu-Natal and one Pondoland in the Eastern Cape, due to harvesting was recently recorded (K.W. Grieve, pers. comm. 2019).
L55: Population
L56: In spite of extensive habitat loss across its restricted distribution range, a large number of recent (2010-2018) records indicate that Aloe candelabrum is still common, persisting at at least 20 locations. A continuing population decline is inferred from ongoing habitat loss and degradation.
L57: It is a long-lived species (generation length estimated 30-50 years), but the rate of recent habitat loss, recorded between 1990 and 2014, indicates that most habitat loss is historical, over a period longer than three generations, and therefore a population reduction of more than 30% over three generations cannot be inferred.
L58: Population trend Decreasing
