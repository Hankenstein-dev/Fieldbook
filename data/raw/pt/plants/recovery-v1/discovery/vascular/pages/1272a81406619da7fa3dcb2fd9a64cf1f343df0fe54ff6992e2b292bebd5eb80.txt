L166: Colutea is a cite91†genus of about 28 species of cite92†deciduous flowering cite93†shrubs in the legume family, cite84†Fabaceae , growing from 2–5 metres (6+1⁄2–16+1⁄2 ft) tall and native to the Old World. They are sometimes called bladder sennas, although they are not particularly closely related to the true cite94†sennas .
L167: ## Description
L168: 
L169: [cite95†edit ]
L170: 
L171: The leaves are cite96†pinnate and light green to glaucous grey-green. The flowers are yellow to orange, pea-shaped and produced in cite97†racemes throughout the summer. These are followed by the attractive inflated seed pods which change from pale green to red or copper in colour.

L214: [cite130†edit ]
L215: 
L216: Colutea species are used as food plants by the cite131†larvae of some cite132†Lepidoptera species including cite133†Coleophora colutella .
L217: ## Cultivation
L218: 
L219: [cite134†edit ]
L220: 
L221: Colutea arborescens, is in general cultivation in the UK. It was imported early, before 1568, probably for medicinal purposes,^{cite135†[3] } but now is grown mostly for its attractive cite136†seed pods, used in dried arrangements. Though in Virginia cite137†Thomas Jefferson had it and it appears in Lady Jean Skipwith's lists of plants,^{cite138†[4] } in US gardens, it is little more than a marginal curiosity.^{cite139†[5] }
L222: Colutea arborescens will grow in poor sandy cite140†soils in preference to heavy or cite141†loamy soils. It has become naturalised in the UK, where it established itself in the sharp drainage of railway embankments.^{cite142†[6] } It is easy to propagate from seed. It is generally pest resistant, though garden cite143†snails will climb up the plant in wet weather to eat the leaves. The cite144†hybrid Colutea × media (C. arborescens × cite76†C. orientalis ) is also cultivated for its coppery flowers.
