L172: Genista-spartium Duhamel (1755)
L173: Ulex (commonly known as gorse, furze, or whin) is a cite103†genus of cite93†flowering plants in the cite104†family Fabaceae. The genus comprises about twenty cite105†species of thorny cite106†evergreen shrubs in the subfamily cite98†Faboideae of the pea family cite97†Fabaceae . The species are native to parts of cite107†western Europe and cite108†northwest Africa , with the majority of species in cite109†Iberia .
L174: Gorse is closely related to the cite110†brooms , and like them, it has green stems and very small leaves, and is adapted to dry growing conditions. However, it differs from the brooms in its extreme thorniness, the cite111†shoots being modified into branched cite112†thorns 1–4 centimetres (0.4–1.6 inches) long, which almost wholly replace the leaves as the plant's functioning cite113†photosynthetic organs.
L175: The cite114†leaves of young plants are cite115†trifoliate , but in mature plants, they are reduced to scales or small spines.^{cite116†[2] } All the species have yellow flowers, generally showy, some with a very long flowering season.
L176: ## Species
L177: 
L178: [cite117†edit ]
L179: The greatest diversity of Ulex species is found in the Atlantic portion of the cite109†Iberian Peninsula , and most species have narrow distribution ranges. The most widespread species is the common gorse (cite118†Ulex europaeus ): it is the only species native to much of western Europe, where it grows in sunny sites and usually on dry, sandy soils.
L180: It is also the largest species, reaching 2–3 m (7–10 ft) in height; this compares with typically 20–40 cm (8–16 in) heights for the western gorse (cite119†Ulex gallii ). This latter species is characteristic of highly exposed cite120†Atlantic coastal cite121†heathland and montane habitats. In the eastern part of Great Britain, the dwarf furze (cite122†Ulex minor ) replaces the western gorse. Ulex minor grows to a height of approximately 30 cm (12 in), a cite123†habit characteristic of sandy lowland heathlands.
L181: Fruiting at cite124†Mallaig , Scotland
L182: Common gorse flowers a little in late autumn and through the winter, coming into flower most strongly in spring. Western gorse and dwarf furze flower in late summer (August–September in Ireland and Great Britain).
L183: Between the different species, some gorse is nearly always in flower, hence the old country saying: "When gorse is out of blossom, kissing's out of fashion".^{cite125†[3] } Gorse flowers have a distinctive coconut-like scent, experienced very strongly by some individuals but only weakly by others.^{cite126†[4] }^{cite127†[5] }

L236: ## Ecology
L237: 
L238: [cite149†edit ]
L239: Gorse may grow as a cite150†fire-climax plant, well adapted to encourage and withstand fires, being highly flammable^{cite151†[10] } and having seed pods that are, to a large extent, opened by fire: thus allowing rapid regeneration after fire. The burnt stumps will readily sprout new growth from the roots. Where fire is excluded, gorse soon tends to be shaded out by taller-growing trees, unless other factors, such as exposure, also apply. Typical fire recurrence periods in gorse-stands are 5–20 years.
L240: Gorse thrives in poor growing areas and conditions, including drought;^{cite152†[11] } it is sometimes found on very rocky soils,^{cite153†[12] } where many species cannot thrive. Moreover, it is widely used for land reclamation (e.g. mine tailings), where its cite154†nitrogen-fixing capacity helps other plants establish better.
L241: Gorse is a valuable plant for wildlife, providing dense, thorny cover that's ideal for protecting bird nests. In Britain, France and Ireland, it is particularly noted for supporting cite155†Dartford warblers (Sylvia undata) and cite156†European stonechats (Saxicola rubicola); the common name of the cite157†whinchat (Saxicola rubetra) attests to its close association with gorse.
L242: The flowers are sometimes eaten by the cite158†caterpillars of the cite159†double-striped pug cite160†moth (Gymnoscelis rufifasciata), whilst those of the cite161†case-bearer moth cite162†Coleophora albicosta feed exclusively on gorse. The dry wood of dead gorse stems provides food for the caterpillars of the cite163†concealer moth cite164†Batia lambdella .

L253: ## Management
L254: 
L255: [cite176†edit ]
L256: 
L257: Gorse readily becomes a dominant plant in suitable conditions. Where this is undesirable for agricultural or ecological reasons, control is required either to remove gorse completely, or to limit its extent. Gorse-stands are often managed by regular burning or flailing, allowing them to regrow from stumps or seed. Denser areas of gorse may be bulldozed.

L261: ## Uses
L262: 
L263: [cite178†edit ]
L264: ### Foods
L265: 
L266: [cite179†edit ]
L267: 
L268: Gorse flowers are edible and can be used in salads, teas and to make a non-grape-based cite180†fruit wine .^{cite181†[15] }
L269: As fodder, gorse is high in protein^{cite182†[16] } and may be used as feed for livestock, particularly in winter, when other greenstuff is not available.
L270: Traditionally, it was used as fodder for horses and cattle,^{cite183†[17] } being made palatable either by bruising (crushing) with hand-held mallets, grinding it to a moss-like consistency with hand- or water-driven mills, or being finely chopped and mixed with straw chaff.^{cite184†[18] } Gorse is also eaten as cite185†forage by some livestock, such as feral ponies, which may eat little else in winter. Ponies may also eat the thinner stems of burnt gorse.
L271: ### Fuel
L272: 
L273: [cite186†edit ]
L274: 
L275: Gorse-bushes are highly flammable; in many areas, bundles of gorse were used to fire traditional bread ovens.^{cite187†[19] }
L276: 
L277: On the island of cite188†Guernsey , cite189†Channel Islands , many traditional farms had cite190†furze brakes . The prolific gorse and bracken would be cut, dried and stored to be used as fuel, with farmhouses having purpose-built furze (clome or cloam) ovens.^{cite191†[20] }^{cite192†[21] }
L278: ### Wood
L279: 
L280: [cite193†edit ]
L281: 
L282: Gorse wood has been used to make small objects; being non-toxic, it is especially suited for cutlery. In spite of its durability, it is not used for construction because the plant is too small and its wood is unstable, being prone to warping. Gorse is useful for garden ornaments, because it is resistant to weather and rot.

L299: ## In popular culture
L300: 
L301: [cite207†edit ]
L302: The flammability of gorse rendered it a symbol for things that were quick to catch fire and burn out; for example, cite208†Arthur Conan Doyle , in his book cite209†Sir Nigel , has Sir cite210†John Chandos say: "They flare up like a furzebush in the flames, but if for a short space you may abide the heat of it, then there is a chance that it may be cooler ... If the Welsh be like the furze fire, then, pardieu! the Scotch are the peat, for they will smolder and you will never come to the end of them."^{cite211†[23] }
L303: In many parts of Britain, especially cite175†Devon and cite205†Cornwall , where it is particularly prevalent on the cite212†moors , the expression "kissing's out of fashion when the gorse is out of blossom"^{cite213†[24] } is a traditional jest, as common gorse is thought to be always in bloom. Sprigs of gorse—or rather, furze, as it was usually known in the West Country—were a traditional gift between young lovers on May Day, when the blossom is at its peak.
