L330@P3: Preface. Brief History of Florida, its Environment, Humans, and Botany (herbarium acronyms
L331@P3: follow Thiers 2025).
L332@P3: Approximately 700–450 million years ago (mya), the basement rocks underlying the Florida
L333@P3: platform were located in the Gondwana continent in the southern latitudes, during which time
L334@P3: the so-called Cambrian explosion occurred (~530 mya). Around 350–500 mya, many major
L335@P3: terrestrial lineages arose or diversified, including embryophytes (land plants), dikarya, hexapods,
L336@P3: arachnids, and tetrapods, at a time when atmospheric O2 levels were probably <20% by volume
L337@P3: (Mills et al. 2023). The amalgamation of Baltica, Laurentia, and Gondwana formed Pangea ~320–
L338@P3: 400 mya (Wu et al. 2021). Atmospheric O2 levels were probably >30% (Mills et al. 2023), just
L339@P3: before the Permian-Triassic extinction event (~250 mya). The Florida basement moved
L340@P3: northward through the equator ~200–300 mya and detached from the Africa and South America
L341@P3: landmasses to reach its current position as a peninsula ~100–200 mya (Veevers 2004; Hine et
L342@P3: al. 2017). The Turonian period, ~90–94 mya, marked the highest sea level (~+150–200 m) and
L343@P3: hottest temperatures of the past 500 million years (Miller et al. 2011; Judd et al. 2024). The large
L344@P3: asteroid impact at Chicxulub, ~66 mya, caused a mass extinction event (Morgan et al. 2022). A
L345@P3: sea level highstand as much as +100 m or more probably completely submerged all parts of the
L346@P3: Florida platform ~50 mya (Miller et al. 2011). At least some parts of the Florida platform have
L347@P3: probably remained emergent since the past 10–40 million years. Continued depositions of
L348@P3: carbonates, evaporites, and siliciclastics formed the modern terrestrial land (Bostick 2018;
L349@P3: Upchurch 2019). Modern humans (Homo sapiens) arose roughly 300,000–800,000 years ago
L350@P3: in Africa, perhaps also partly in Eurasia (Bergström et al. 2021). The most recent sea level
L351@P3: highstand at +6 m was ~120,000 years ago (Georgiou et al. 2024), submerging much of the
L352@P3: southern Florida peninsula. Indelible, artistic depictions of animals were made by humans as early
L353@P3: as 50,000 years ago in Indonesia (Brumm et al. 2021; Oktaviana et al. 2024). The last glacial
L354@P3: maximum ~20,000–26,000 years ago had sea levels -120 m, exposing much more of the Florida
L355@P3: platform, and the emergence of Beringia allowed humans to colonize North America (Bennett et
L356@P3: al. 2021), during the coolest global temperatures in the past 500 million years (Judd et al. 2024).
L357@P3: Knowledge of the Florida flora began in the panhandle as early as 15,000 years ago, when
L358@P3: humans occupied the Aucilla River region (Brown 1994; Halligan et al. 2016; Smith 2018; Faught
L359@P3: & Pevny 2019) and the Florida landmass was significantly larger, with sea levels of -80 m. Pollen
L360@P3: of Corylus and Picea was present (Watts et al. 1992; Carr 2012). Shortly after humans arrived,
L361@P3: numerous large animals went extinct or were extirpated (e.g. American lion, armadillo, bison,
L362@P3: camelids, carcara, dire wolf, ground sloth, horse, jaguar, mammoth, mastodon, peccary, sabertoothed tiger, spectacled bear, tapir; Webb 1974). Sea level rose rapidly, reaching ca. -10 m
L363@P3: ~7,000 years ago. Around this time or shortly thereafter, shell mound architecture may have
L364@P3: started in Florida (Marquardt 2010; Saunders & Russo 2011).
L365@P3: About 5,000–6,000 years ago, some of the earliest extant illustrations of plant species
L366@P3: appeared in Eurasia (e.g. Chogha Cheshmeh pottery, Battlefield Palette, Abydos tombs). Some
L367@P3: of the earliest extant botanical texts (or herbals) are traced to Assyrian and Babylonian
L368@P3: cuneiform, ~4,500 years ago (Thompson 1924; Borchardt 2002), and later examples include the
L369@P3: Ebers Papyrus (~3,500 years ago) and The Hippocratic Corpus (~2,500 years ago). Some 2,000
L370@P3: years ago, books with plant illustrations began to appear (Chavannes-Mazel 2023).
L371@P3: Humans reached the Florida Keys by 2,500 years ago (LeFebvre et al. 2022). By the early
L372@P3: 1500s, hundreds of thousands of peoples probably inhabited Florida across 100s of villages and
L373@P3: towns, subsisting from wild foods and cultivated crops in some areas, including beans, corn, and
L374@P3: cucurbits. Modern names for peoples historically inhabiting Florida include the Ais, Apalachi,
L375@P3: Calusa, Chacato, Hobe, Jaega, Jororo, Mayaca, Mayaimi, Matecumbe, Pansacola, Tequesta,
L376@P3: Timucua, and Tocobaga (Romans 1775; Hrdlička 1922; Smith 1933; Milanich 2017; Liebmann
L377@P3-4: 2021).-2-
L378@P4: A fleet of three ships led by Ponce de León (including peoples from Spain, Africa, and the
L379@P4: Caribbean) landed in peninsular Florida in 1513 (3 April–24 June, 3 July–17 July), introducing the
L380@P4: moniker ‘La Florida’ and marking the first known human visitation from across the Atlantic Ocean
L381@P4: and Florida Straits, whereupon they encountered and skirmished with indigenous peoples, a few
L382@P4: of which were taken aboard (Murga Sanz 1959: 105; Palencia-Roth in Williams & Lewis 1993;
L383@P4: Feder 1994; Fuson 2000; Seidemann 2001; Peck 2003; Turner 2013). From this and subsequent
L384@P4: landings, Spain made a claim to the land. Further visits to Florida included the mainly Spanish
L385@P4: entourages of Miruelo in 1516, de Cordova in 1517, Pineda in 1519, de León again in 1521, Narvaez
L386@P4: in 1527, de Soto in 1539, and de Luna y Arellano in 1559, as well as the French entourage of Ribault
L387@P4: in 1564.
L388@P4: Also around the mid-1500s, the art of preserving plants as herbarium specimens took shape
L389@P4: in Italy (Baldini et al. 2022; Thijsse 2023), ca. 100 years after Gutenberg’s printing press (in
L390@P4: Germany). The modern herbarium was enabled by a growing paper-making industry, especially
L391@P4: the innovative paper construction made from old rags, developed and refined in northern Italy
L392@P4: around the 1200s–1500s (Fahy 2003; Kittler 2015; Iannaccone 2021). Also preceding the
L393@P4: physical herbarium was the art of nature printing, developed in the 1400s in Italy (Reeds
L394@P4: 2006:215).
L395@P4: The Spanish founding of St. Augustine in 1565 and Pensacola in 1698 (Griffen 1959) along
L396@P4: with their associated missions throughout Florida (Boyd 1939; Hann 1990), and the British
L397@P4: founding of Charles Town (South Carolina) in 1670, precipitated the most severe demographic
L398@P4: change of indigenous peoples of present-day Florida and adjacent states, concomitant and
L399@P4: followed by increased migration of peoples from across the Atlantic Ocean, Gulf of Mexico,
L400@P4: Florida Straits, and adjacent regions over the ensuing years (Andrews 1943; Williams, Jr. 1949;
L401@P4: Smith 1951; Sturtevant 1953; Neyland 1965; Ellsworth & Dysart 1981; Hann 1989; Johnson 1989;
L402@P4: Friedson 1994; Fishman 1995; Riordan 1996; Eltis 2001; Palmer 2002; Worth 2003; Sturtevant &
L403@P4: Cattelino 2004; Wasserman 2010; Weatherford 2015; Broadwell 2021; Broadwell & Dubcovsky
L404@P4: 2023; Bassi 2024; FSU 2024; Miccosukee Tribe Tribe 2024; Poarch Creek Indians 2024;
L405@P4: Schneider et al. 2024; Seminole Tribe 2024). These early maritime travels introduced hogs, cattle
L406@P4: (Arnade 1961; Yarlett 1985; Delsol et al. 2023), horses (Conant et al. 2011), and citrus as well as
L407@P4: weeds to Florida e.g. through stone ballast (Gifford 2014), while the American lineage of
L408@P4: domestic dogs virtually disappeared (Zimmer 2007; Leathlobhair et al. 2018; Manin et al. 2025).
L409@P4: The bison may have expanded its range into Florida during this time, seemingly absent or perhaps
L410@P4: sparse until the late 1600s (Rostlund 1960).
L411@P4: The Latin binomial system for naming plant species was established by Linnaeus in 1753.
L412@P4: Through the study of material outside of Florida (e.g. Carolinas, Jamaica, etc.), from 1753–1771,
L413@P4: Linnaeus coined the epithets of ~677 plant species that are also native to Florida. Linnaeus’s
L414@P4: access to any material from present-day Florida was negligible. In the late 1700s–early 1800s,
L415@P4: several other authors formally published around 500 accepted Latin binomials that were later
L416@P4: applied to Florida native plants, mainly Aiton, Gmelin, Kunth, Jacquin, Miller, Muhlenberg, Poiret,
L417@P4: Pursh, Swartz, Walter, and Willdenow. The works of Linnaeus and the preceding authors applied
L418@P4: Latin binomials to ~1/3 of Florida’s native plant species, practically without studying any Florida
L419@P4: material.
L420@P4: In 1763, Spain ceded its claim of sovereignty over Florida to the British (Arnade 1955), and
L421@P4: herbarium studies in Florida were soon initiated with the collection of specimens as early as 1765.
L422@P4: The first plant species given a Latin binomial based on Florida material was Illicium floridanum in
L423@P4: 1770, from Pensacola material sent by W. Clifton to J. Ellis (Franck 2018). Some specimens from
L424@P4: the Plukenet herbarium (predating Clifton) bear “Florida” as their provenance (Dandy 1958) but
L425@P4-5: the taxa involved suggest these were collected outside of present-day Florida and that this early -3-
L426@P5: usage of “Florida” referred to a more loosely defined, larger geographic region (“Spanish Florida”)
L427@P5: in the eastern USA (Schenk 1710; Sturtevant 1962; Boniface 1971).
L428@P5: In the 1760s–1770s, William Bartram made trips to Florida and later published his accounts
L429@P5: (Bartram 1791). He is the author of ~15 species epithets of plants native to Florida, four described
L430@P5: by him based on observations in Florida, three credited to him by other authors, and the rest
L431@P5: described by him from observations outside of Florida. Some Bartram specimens from Florida
L432@P5: sent to Barclay and Fothergill reside at BM (Ewan 1968); any Florida specimens collected by John
L433@P5: Bartram and sent to Collinson remain unaccounted for (J.T. Fry, pers. comm.; Stafleu and Cowan
L434@P5: 1976; Berkeley and Berkeley 1992: 673–674).
L435@P5: At the end of the Revolutionary War (1775–1783), the 1783 Treaty of Paris ceded Florida to
L436@P5: Spain. That year, Lamarck described Vaccinium myrsinites, from material (at P) collected near
L437@P5: St. Augustine, and he also described Asclepias floridana (=A. tuberosa). Sesbania vesicaria was
L438@P5: described by Jacquin in 1787 from plants in Vienna that were sent from Florida. In 1789, Linnaeus
L439@P5: filius authored Zamia integrifolia based on material supplied by Ellis in 1768. In the same year,
L440@P5: Aiton (1789) referenced Florida for the distribution of several species in cultivation at Kew, also
L441@P5: describing Ilex vomitoria. In 1791, Lamarck named Florida as part of the distribution of two more
