L168: Salicornia is a cite96†genus of cite97†succulent , cite98†halophytic (salt tolerant) cite90†flowering plants in the family cite93†Amaranthaceae that grow in cite99†salt marshes , on beaches, and among cite100†mangroves . Salicornia species are native to North America, Europe, central Asia, and southern Africa.
L169: Common names for the genus include cite101†glasswort , cite102†pickleweed , picklegrass, and marsh cite103†samphire ; these common names are also used for some species not in Salicornia.^{cite104†[1] } To French speakers in cite105†Atlantic Canada , they are known colloquially as tétines de souris ('mouse tits').
L170: The main European species is often eaten, called marsh samphire in Britain, and the main North American species is occasionally sold in grocery stores or appears on restaurant menus as sea beans, samphire greens, or sea asparagus.
L171: ## Description
L172: 
L173: [cite106†edit ]
L174: 
L175: Illustration of cite107†Salicornia maritima L176: The Salicornia species are small annual herbs. They grow prostrate to erect, their simple or branched stems are succulent, hairless, and appear to be jointed.
L177: The opposite cite108†leaves are strongly reduced to small fleshy scales with a narrow dry margin, hairless, unstalked and united at the base, thus enclosing and forming a succulent sheath around the stem, which gives it the appearance of being composed of jointed segments.^{cite109†[2] }^{cite110†[3] }^{cite111†[4] } Many species are green, but their foliage turns red in autumn. Older stems may be somewhat woody basally.
L178: All stems terminate in spike-like apparently jointed cite112†inflorescences . Each joint consists of two opposite minute bracts with a (1-) 3-flowered cite113†cyme tightly embedded in cavities of the main axis and partly hidden by the bracts. The flowers are arranged in a triangle, both lateral flowers beneath the central flower. The hermaphrodite cite114†flowers are more or less radially symmetric, with a cite115†perianth of three fleshy cite116†tepals united nearly to the apex.
L179: There are 1–2 cite117†stamens and an ovary with two cite118†stigmas .^{cite110†[3] }
L180: The perianth is persistent in fruit. The fruit wall (pericarp) is membranous. The vertical seed is ellipsoid, with yellowish brown, membranous, hairy seed coat. The seed contains no cite119†perisperm (feeding tissue).^{cite110†[3] }
L181: 
L182: Like most members of the subfamily cite94†Salicornioideae , Salicornia species use the cite120†C_{3} carbon fixation pathway to take in carbon dioxide from the surrounding atmosphere.^{cite121†[5] }
L183: ## Taxonomy
L184: 
L185: [cite122†edit ]
L186: 
L187: cite86†Salicornia europaea L188: 
L189: The genus Salicornia was cite123†first described in 1753 by cite95†Carl Linnaeus .^{cite124†[6] } The annual cite86†Salicornia europaea was selected as the cite125†type species .^{cite126†[7] }
L190: The genus probably originated during the cite127†Miocene in the region between the cite128†Mediterranean Basin and Central Asia. Evolving from within the perennial and frost-sensitive former genus Sarcocornia (now shown to be cite129†paraphyletic ),^{cite130†[8] } the annual, strongly inbreeding and frost-tolerant Salicornia diversified during the late cite131†Pliocene to early cite132†Pleistocene . By events of intercontinental dispersals, they reached southern Africa twice and North America at least three times.
L191: Two tetraploid lineages expanded rapidly, with the ability to colonize lower belts of the cite99†salt marshes than their diploid relatives. Inbreeding and geographical isolation led to a large number of reproductive isolated species that are only weakly differentiated.^{cite133†[9] }
L192: The taxonomic classification of this genus is extremely difficult (with one paper calling it a "taxonomic nightmare").^{cite133†[9] } The determination of species seems almost impossible for non-specialists. The reasons for these difficulties are the reduced habit with weak morphological differentiation and high phenotypic variability.
L193: As the succulent plants lose their characteristics while drying, herbarium specimens often cannot be determined with certainty and are less suited for taxonomic studies.^{cite133†[9] }
L194: cite134†Molecular phylogenetic studies have regularly revised the cite135†circumscription of the genus. It was considered distinct from Sarcocornia in 2007 and 2012 studies.^{cite133†[9] }^{cite136†[10] } A 2017 study resulted in Sarcocornia being cite137†sunk into Salicornia – despite being the first perennial,^{cite138†[11] } substantially increasing the size of the genus, which was divided into four subgenera.^{cite130†[8] }

L255: ## Distribution and habitat
L256: 
L257: [cite192†edit ]
L258: 
L259: Salicornia at cite193†Ballona Wetlands , Southern California
L260: 
L261: The species of Salicornia are widely distributed over the cite194†Northern Hemisphere and in southern Africa, ranging from the cite195†subtropics to cite196†subarctic regions. There is one species present in New Zealand.^{cite197†[12] }
L262: They grow in coastal salt marshes and in inland salty habitats like shores of cite198†salt lakes .^{cite133†[9] } Salicornia species are cite199†halophytes and can generally tolerate immersion in salt water (hygrohalophytes).
L263: ## Ecology
L264: 
L265: [cite200†edit ]
L266: 
L267: Salicornia species are used as food plants by the cite201†larvae of some cite202†Lepidoptera species, including the cite203†Coleophora case-bearers C. atriplicis and C. salicorniae; the latter feeds exclusively on Salicornia spp.

L272: ### Culinary
L273: 
L274: [cite205†edit ]
L275: 
L276: cite145†Salicornia bigelovii L277: 
L278: S. europaea is edible, either cooked or raw,^{cite206†[13] } as are cite140†S. rubra and S. depressa.^{cite207†[14] } In England, S. europaea is one of several plants known as samphire (including cite208†rock samphire ); the term samphire is believed to be a corruption of the French name, [herbe de] Saint-Pierre, which means "St. Peter's herb".^{cite209†[15] }
L279: In Hawaii, where it is known as "sea asparagus", it is often blanched and used as a topping for salads or accompaniment for fish.^{cite210†[16] }^{cite211†[17] }
L280: 
L281: In addition to S. europaea, the seeds of cite145†S. bigelovii yield an edible oil. S. bigelovii's edibility is compromised somewhat because it contains cite212†saponins , which are toxic under certain conditions.^{cite206†[13] }
L282: Umari keerai (cite147†S. brachiata ) is cooked and eaten or pickled. It is also used as fodder for cattle, sheep, and goats.^{cite213†[18] } In cite214†Kalpitiya , Sri Lanka, it is used to feed donkeys.
L283: On the east coast of Canada, the plant is known as "samphire greens" and is a local delicacy. In cite215†southeast Alaska , it is known as beach asparagus. In cite216†Nova Scotia , Canada, they are known as crow's foot greens. In cite217†British Columbia , they are known as sea asparagus.^{cite218†[19] } In the United States, they are known as "sea beans" when used for culinary purposes. Other names include sea green bean, sea pickle, and marsh samphire.^{cite219†[20] }
L284: In India, researchers at the cite220†Central Salt and Marine Chemicals Research Institute developed a process to yield culinary salt from S. brachiata. The resulting product is known as vegetable salt and sold under the brand name Saloni.^{cite221†[21] }
L285: 
L286: Dehydrated, pulverized Salicornia is sold under the brand name "Green Salt" as a cite222†salt substitute claimed to be as salty in taste as table salt, but with less sodium.^{cite223†[22] }^{cite224†[23] }
