L21@P1: SUMMER GARDEN PARTY
L22@P1: We held our Summer Garden Party on 1 March, and it was yet another wonderful day. Purists will point out that 1 March is, in fact,
L23@P1: the first day of Autumn, but we’re a “glass half full” type of organisation and like to extend the good things in life. We had a great
L24@P1: summer, so why not extend it by a day?
L25@P1: Hannah Moloney from ABC’s Gardening Australia was there and
L26@P1: officially opened our new composting stage. She was very entertaining and gave a composting masterclass after the opening ceremony. This is a wonderful new addition to our
L27@P1: infrastructure. Funds were provided by the Burnie City Council (and thanks again to the Mayor and Councillors), and the build was imagined, designed, and completed by Baz and Tess Morrell. What a team these two
L28@P1: are– thanks guys, you do an amazing job and are such an asset to the garden.
L29@P1: James McGregor was also there, talking about our resident platypus. In conjunction with James, we have
L30@P1: recently been awarded a grant to “monitor platypus using citizen science in potential climate refugia.” This is
L31@P1: a huge project and will assist in our understanding of our favourite resident.
L32@P1: We had music, courtesy of “Synch or Swing” and “Black Current Tango.” Deviant Gin and Communion
L33@P1: Brewing were there, and our catering teams supplied delicious jacket spuds and sausages. Forbidden sweets
L34@P1: and “I serve ice-cream” were on site too. We had roaming fairies, plant finding activities and a best hat
L35@P1: competition. Thanks must go to our volunteers, without whom none of this would have been possible.
L36@P1: Thank you, you are amazing.

L51@P2: Hello from the Volunteers Tourism Team
L52@P2: Meredith, Frances and David still smiling in the buggy after a busy day with passengers
L53@P2: from the Silver Muse.
L54@P2: As you can see from the accompanying photo, our volunteers are coming to the end of another cruise
L55@P2: ship season. Our small team have been greeting the visitors as they come off their buses, handing out
L56@P2: maps and giving directions for the garden facilities. Visitors have the option of guided walks, self-guided
L57@P2: walks, buggy rides or just sitting on the balcony with a delicious Devonshire tea and enjoying the
L58@P2: marvellous view.
L59@P2: The aforementioned options do depend on what the specific tour companies have booked for. Some are
L60@P2: inclusive, some options require payment on the day and some just provide garden entry and transport to
L61@P2: and from the Burnie CBD. Our team have had an enjoyable summer chatting to visitors from all over
L62@P2: the world, sharing holiday stories, memories of favourite gardens visited, gardening tips and in general
L63@P2: being great ambassadors for EVRG, Burnie and Tasmania. After all, it is the people as well as the garden
L64@P2: that make us an award-winning tourist location.
L65@P2: If this is something you think you would like to try, we would love to hear from you. It is fun, interesting and you definitely get back as much as you put in. Once cruise ship season finishes, we still have
L66@P2: coach groups from Tassie and interstate during the year, plus we have requests for buggy tours all year
L67@P2: around. Training for doing buggy driving, tour guiding and especially what to say is provided and you
L68@P2: get to work with an experienced volunteer until you are comfortable on your own. You can do as much
L69@P2: or as little as you like, although practice does make perfect, so the more you do, the better and more
L70@P2: experienced you will become. We have a list of upcoming tours and contact team members to ask who
L71@P2: is free on any given day. Buggy tour drivers get a group text as bookings are often requested within a 24-
L72@P2: hour period.
L73@P2: So, if you are interested, or know someone who you think would be, please make contact with me at
L74@P2-3: volunteers@emuvalleyrhodogarden.org.au MeredithPage 4

L95@P5: Rhododendron rubropilosum
L96@P5: Rhododendron rubropilosum is an evergreen azalea found growing
L97@P5: on Mt. Morrison Taiwan on grass covered slopes at elevations of
L98@P5: 2400-3400m, where it grows into an upright shrub from 1-3m high
L99@P5: (around 2m in our garden).
L100@P5: The young shoots are covered with grey or reddish-brown hairs.
L101@P5: Leaves are oblong-lanceolate or elliptic-lanceolate, the upper surface
L102@P5: dark green, under surface pale green, both surfaces have scattered
L103@P5: hairs similar hairs to the shoots. Flowers are funnel shaped, pale
L104@P5: pink sometimes spotted rose and usually appear in clusters of 3-4 in
L105@P5: September early October.
L106@P5: Rhododendron rubropilosum is closely related to R. subsessile, the
L107@P5: main difference is the flowers are violet-purple or rose-pink on R.
L108@P5: subsessile and the stamens are longer and is native to the Philippine
L109@P5: Islands.
L110@P5: This species was first collected by G. Nakahara in 1906 and was introduced by Wilson in 1918 and by J.L. Creech in 1968. At the Emu Valley Rhododendron Garden it is growing quite well and can be seen on either side of the Noel Sullivan walk in our Taiwan section. Maurie
