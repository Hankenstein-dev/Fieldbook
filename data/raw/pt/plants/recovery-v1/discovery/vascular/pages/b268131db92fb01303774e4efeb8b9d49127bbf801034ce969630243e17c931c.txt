L45@P1: Introduction
L46@P1: The Ecology of Sydney plant species aims to provide ecological information on native
L47@P1: and naturalised Sydney plants. Particular emphasis has been given to data that are
L48@P1: relevant for ecologists and natural area managers, for people involved in bush
L49@P1: regeneration, land rehabilitation and landscape design, for researchers in many fields
L50@P1: and for a wide range of people who are interested in bushland and native plants.
L51@P1: Species-specific information is also relevant to environmental impact assessment and
L52@P1: to studies involving changes in drainage, water movement, wind exposure, nutrient
L53@P1: conditions and fire regimes on plant species and plant communities. Information
L54@P1: included covers growth form, flowering and fruiting times, longevity and maturation
L55@P1: periods, pollination and seed dispersal data, distribution, habitat and plant community,
L56@P1: as well as responses to fire and disturbance where available and is complementary to
L57@P1: the descriptive texts in taxonomic handbooks such as the Flora of New South Wales
L58@P1: (Harden 1990–93).
L59@P1: Part 9 of the Ecology of Sydney plant species covers the Monocotyledon families
L60@P1: alphabetically from Agavaceae to Juncaginaceae. Part 10, Monoctyledon families
L61@P1: Lemnaceae to Zosteraceae 2003, Part 11, Monocotyledon family Poaceae 2004.

L117@P2: Information categories
L118@P2: For each species or subspecies, information is presented under headings relating to
L119@P2: different life-history aspects, each with further subdivisions. Emphasis has been
L120@P2: given to understanding the species in its wild habitat. For this reason data on
L121@P2: cultivation and artificial propagation, available in horticultural books, are generally
L122@P2: not included. Likewise information on weed control is not given. Generally, the
L123@P2: categories are broadly interpreted and since the project aims to stimulate further
L124@P2: investigation, we welcome the addition of extra data on any other aspects of the
L125@P2-3: plant’s ecology.698 Cunninghamia Vol. 7(4): 2002
L126@P3: A ‘comments’ section has been deliberately avoided — for all information retrieval
L127@P3: purposes, all ecological material should be attributable to some life history aspect of
L128@P3: the plant or its habitat although even with our broad categories there have been
L129@P3: problems with ‘Vegetation‘ and ‘Substrate’ sections for specialised lifeforms such as
L130@P3: mistletoes. The main purpose of this information is to give a picture of the ecology of
L131@P3: particular species as is currently known. It is not primarily for comparative purposes
L132@P3: although it can be used in this way. One of our original aims was to demonstrate that
L133@P3: closely related species may have different ecologies and that to generalise within
L134@P3: genera for example rather than look at a particular species is an over simplification.
L135@P3: We have also included reference to individualistic information resulting from specific
L136@P3: research that will not necessarily be available for all species.
L137@P3: Similarly where we have observations on the effects of factors such as drought on
L138@P3: particular species we have included these, though we have not included a category
L139@P3: for ‘drought’ because of difficulties in definition. One of our aims is to be openended, to point out that there are many gaps in the ecological knowledge and to
L140@P3: indicate areas and species where future work may be particularly useful.

L146@P3: Life history
L147@P3: Growth form: Brief description.
L148@P3: Source: Flora of New South Wales.
L149@P3: Vegetative spread: Indication of whether localised expansion or spread is possible
L150@P3: from an individual by rhizome, stolon, rootsucker etc. Important in determining
L151@P3: ability of species to colonise immediate local area. Does not include vegetative distancedispersal which is included under ‘Dispersal, establishment & growth’ (diaspore).
L152@P3: Source: Flora descriptions, Herbarium specimens, field observations.
L153@P3: Longevity: Average potential life-span under natural conditions — range in years
L154@P3: where possible, indefinite, where death is not a result of inherent growth e.g.
L155@P3: continued rhizomatous growth. Longevity may be shorter in cultivation. There are
L156@P3: very few references in the literature to the longevity of species or records of
L157@P3: individual plants, particularly of the longer-lived species, yet this is an important
L158@P3: factor in assessing importance of recruitment and changes in plant communities.
L159@P3: Most of the estimates given are based on the authors’ judgement, based on the shape,
L160@P3: size and growth rates of individuals, and the authors’ experience in the field over 25
L161@P3: years, together with any historical data available. The figures given are conservative
L162@P3: and represent minimal average ages. The authors would not be surprised if much
L163@P3-4: greater ages are achieved in many cases.Benson & McDougall, Ecology of Sydney plant species 9 699
L164@P4: Source: literature, authors’ assessment from field observations.
L165@P4: Primary juvenile period: time taken from germination to produce first fruits or spores.
L166@P4: Gill (1975) uses the term for the period from germination to flowering but the period
L167@P4: to fruit maturity is more significant ecologically. Secondary juvenile period is time
L168@P4: taken for an individual to recover to produce fruit after major damage e.g. from fire,
L169@P4: but applicable only if species regularly resprouts. The time required to accumulate a
L170@P4: seedbank sufficient to ensure reasonable recruitment in the event of mass mortality is
L171@P4: also an important consideration. Primary and secondary juvenile periods should not
L172@P4: be considered as absolute and they may vary spatially as a function of habitat factors
L173@P4: (moisture availability, nutrients etc.) and temporally (e.g. climatic variation).
L174@P4: Source: literature, field observations.
L175@P4: Reproduction: for Angiosperms, reproduction is separated into:
L176@P4: Flowers: predominant flower colour, flowering period — range of months and, where
L177@P4: possible, peak month based on frequency of herbarium collections. Pollination vectors.
L178@P4: Fruit: fruit shape and size, particularly with respect to potential dispersal agents.
L179@P4: Maturation period. Seed size and number.
L180@P4: Source: Herbarium specimens, field observations, Flora of New South Wales, literature.
L181@P4: Dispersal, establishment and growth: diaspore type (including vegetative), dispersal
L182@P4: agent. Germination requirements: seedbank presence, dormancy, growth rates,
L183@P4: seasonality, deciduousness etc. [notes on propagation in cultivation may be given
L184@P4: where these imply similar behaviour under natural field conditions e.g. insights into
L185@P4: seed germination inhibition].
L186@P4: Source: literature, field observations.
L187@P4: Fire response: general response of mature plant to fire, in particular whether it
L188@P4: generally resprouts or is killed (see also Gill 1981, Gill & Bradstock 1992). Seedling
L189@P4: recruitment associated with fire is included under establishment and growth. Entries
L190@P4: such as ‘probably killed’ mean that a species has no obvious morphological
L191@P4: characteristics likely to assist in surviving and resprouting following fire. While
L192@P4: species that resprout after fire may be readily noted, the death of individuals may not
L193@P4: be observed unless the occurrence of the species in the area was well known prior to
L194@P4: the fire.
L195@P4: For a given species some populations may be capable of resprouting while other
L196@P4: populations may be killed. This may explain cases where contradictory evidence has
L197@P4: been given though response to fire in some species may depend on fire intensity.
L198@P4: In view of the limited information available for many species, the categories of Gill
L199@P4: and Bradstock (1992) have not been applied at this stage.
L200@P4: Source: field observations, literature.
L201@P4: Interaction with other organisms: symbiosis, predators, diseases etc.
L202@P4-5: Source: mainly literature.700 Cunninghamia Vol. 7(4): 2002
