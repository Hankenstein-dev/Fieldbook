L11@P0: The occurrence of Ceramium tenuicorne in the SW Gulf of Finland seems to be determined by physical factors, species competition and adaptation to the seasonal environment of the northern Baltic Sea. Due to the strong competition, in the upper sublittoral
L12@P0: C. tenuicorne grew mainly epiphytically. Asexual plants were most common around
L13@P0: the year. In July–August tiny C. tenuicorne was found also in the upper sublittoral. At
L14@P0: that time plants with cystocarps, tetrasporangia and propagules were observed. In October, after the decline of Cladophora glomerata, a nap-like belt of C. tenuicorne grew
L15@P0: at depths of less than one metre and a short-lived female generation with cystocarps
L16@P0: was observed. In late autumn, most of the filaments close to the shore break down due
L17@P0: to wave action, and in winter due to the ice scraping while deeper growing individuals
L18@P0: are perennial. Ceramium tenuicorne is physiologically adapted to the extreme environment of the Baltic Sea. Photosynthetic pigment content of C. tenuicorne exhibit seasonal changes. The higher winter values of pigments can be considered an adaptation
L19@P0: to an extreme environment, caused by low light. In summer, pale individuals with red
L20@P0: basal parts grew at 1–2 m depth, having grown from old, overwintered basal parts. The
L21@P0: pale colour is due to low concentrations of phycoerythrin. The fastest growth occurs in
L22@P0: late spring, and in summer months the individuals are tall, the cells are long, and the
L23@P0: number of secondary branches is exceptionally high. The cortical cells of many individuals grew upwards in the older parts of the thallus. The form of the cells was often
L24@P0: conical, but some individuals with oval cells were observed.
L25@P0: Key words: Ceramium, morphology, pigments, production, reproduction
L26@P0: Introduction
L27@P0: The taxonomic status of Baltic Ceramium was
L28@P0: discussed earlier (Wallentinus 1979). Waern
L29@P0: (1952) classified northern Baltic Ceramium species as Ceramium tenuicorne, which he considered to be widespread in the entire Baltic Sea.
L30@P0: This name was used until Waern (1992) himself
L31@P0: proposed that there were actually two species
L32@P0: in the Baltic Sea, C. tenuicorne being confined
L33@P0: to the southern Baltic and C. gobii occurring in
L34@P0-1: the low-salinity northern Baltic. Nielsen et al. 96 Bäck & Likolammi • ANN. BOT. FENNICI Vol. 40
L35@P1: (1995) used the latter name with the remark that
L36@P1: “further investigations are necessary to clarify
L37@P1: the taxonomic status”. However, Gabrielsen et
L38@P1: al. (2002) showed that the parasporangia that
L39@P1: Waern (1992) proposed to be the “gobii” type
L40@P1: are actually propagules. In her molecular study,
L41@P1: she demonstrated that C. gobii is a form of C.
L42@P1: tenuicorne.
L43@P1: The morphological characteristics that were
L44@P1: used to segregate Ceramium tenuicorne were as
L45@P1: follows: the propagules are unilocular, terminal
L46@P1: monosporangium-like structures, and the branching is regular and (pseudo)dichotomous, and also
L47@P1: secondary branches are produced.
L48@P1: In the Baltic Sea, Ceramium tenuicorne is
L49@P1: reported to be a common and abundant species.
L50@P1: Its distribution covers an area with salinity varying between 3‰ and 8‰ (Nielsen et al. 1995).
L51@P1: Ceramium tenuicorne colonises rapidly and
L52@P1: withstands wave action. It can occur in lower
L53@P1: parts of the hydrolittoral zone (Waern 1952,
L54@P1: Leskinen et al. 1992). Ceramium tenuicorne
L55@P1: occurs commonly on other macroalgae, higher
L56@P1: plants, and Mytilus trossulus. It may extend even
L57@P1: down to 9–15 m depth (Waern 1952, Ravanko
L58@P1: 1968, Wallentinus 1979). Ceramium tenuicorne
L59@P1: thrives in the upper sublittoral where it grows
L60@P1: on rocky bottoms, stones and pieces of wood
L61@P1: (Waern 1952, Ravanko 1968, Kiirikki & Lehvo
L62@P1: 1997).
L63@P1: The aim of this work was to evaluate the
L64@P1: influence of northern Baltic winter conditions on
L65@P1: the survival of Ceramium tenuicorne by determining its phenology, e.g. its seasonal occurrence and changes in morphology and photosynthetic pigments.
L66@P1: Material and methods
L67@P1: The study site was at Furuskär, which is located in
L68@P1: SW Finland in the archipelago of Tvärminne, off
L69@P1: the Hanko peninsula (59°50´0´´N, 23°16´3´´E).
L70@P1: The gently sloping rocky shore of Furuskär
L71@P1: represents a typical habitat for Ceramium tenuicorne. The species was collected monthly from
L72@P1: August 1993 until August 1994 from 3-m depth
L73@P1: by SCUBA diving. Observations and collections
L74@P1: were made simultaneously in the hydrolittoral
L75@P1: zone and at 6-m depth. During the study period,
L76@P1: the sea water temperature was close to zero in
L77@P1: November–March; during the summer months it
L78@P1: could rise up to 16.5 °C (Fig. 1a).
L79@P1: The nutrient concentrations of the seawater
L80@P1: were measured twice a month during the whole
L81@P1: sampling period. All the analytical methods are
L82@P1: adopted from Grasshoff (1976). Nutrient measurements were based on a single sample and
L83@P1: were carried out in the same week as sampling.
L84@P1: From August to October, the inorganic nitrogen
L85@P1: and phosphorus concentrations were low (NO3-N
L86@P1: < 1 µg l–1, NH4-N and PO4-P < 5 µg l–1; Fig 2b).
L87@P1: From October till the beginning of February, the
L88@P1: concentrations of ammonium-, and especially
L89@P1: nitrate-nitrogen, increased evenly. At the beginning of February, the concentration of NO3
L90@P1: -N
L91@P1: was high, about 100 µg l–1. The concentration of
L92@P1: phosphate-phosphorus increased in November
L93@P1: to 16 µg l–1. After formation of the ice cover in
L94@P1: the beginning of February, nitrate and ammonium concentrations continued to increase but
L95@P1: they started to decrease already in March 1994
L96@P1: (Fig. 1). Ice melted at the end of March and inorganic nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations
L97@P1: decreased rapidly close to the detection limit of 1
L98@P1: µg l–1 (Särkelä 1995).

L136@P2: transported back to the laboratory in seawaterfilled containers. For the study of the reproductive stage, production and morphological studies, the algal samples were stored at +10 °C in

L138@P2: out 24 hours after sampling.
L139@P2: The material was sorted and thalli were
L140@P2: microscopically studied to check their reproductive status. Seasonal changes in the morphology of Ceramium tenuicorne were studied by
L141@P2: measuring the length of the individuals and the
L142@P2: axial cells. Also the main branches of the thalli
L143@P2: were counted. The length of the cells was taken
L144@P2: as an average of ten cells in the middle part of
L145@P2: the thalli and length of the individual was taken
L146@P2: as an average of five thalli. The cells were measured with Wild M20 microscope with an ocular
L147@P2: at 100¥ magnification.
L148@P2: Chlorophyll a and phycobiliproteins (phycoerythrin and phycocyanin) were determined
L149@P2: spectrophotometrically (in triplicates: 0.1–0.5 g
L150@P2: algae) from the frozen apical material. Phycoerythrin and phycocyanin were determined
L151@P2: according to Beer and Eshel (1985). The concentration of chl a was measured with a modification of Kuosa (1991). The samples were homogenised in 5 ml of 98% ethanol and were left in
L152@P2: the dark at room temperature for 12–24 h. After
L153@P2: extraction, the samples were centrifuged once
L154@P2: with a table centrifuge (Heinz Janetzki Type
L155@P2: T12) at 3500 rpm for 15–21 min. The absorbance was measured at 0.5 nm intervals at wave
L156@P2: lengths of 664–666 nm. The chlorophyll concentrations were calculated as mg chl a (g fw)–1
L157@P2: according to the formula
L158@P2: C = vD664–666 /83.4lm
L159@P2: where C = chl a (mg g–1 fw), v = volume of ethanol (ml), D = max absorbance at wavelengths of
L160@P2: 664–666 nm, l = the length of the cuvette (cm),
L161@P2: m = fresh weight of the sample (g fw).
L162@P2: Statistical analyses were performed to reveal
L163@P2: the differences in plant length, cell length and
L164@P2: concentrations of photosynthetic pigments (chl
L165@P2: a, phycoerythrin and phycocyanin) between the
L166@P2: summer and winter months. Winter and summer
L167@P2: averages of plant length, cell lenth, phycocyanin
L168@P2: and phycoerythrin were compared using a contrast analysis after analysis of variance (error
L169@P2: d.f. = 48, 48, 17, 17 respectively). The tests were
L170@P2: performed with STATISTIX ver. 2.
L171@P2: Results
L172@P2: In summer Ceramium tenuicorne grew between
L173@P2: 1- and 6-m depths, mainly being epiphytic on
L174@P2: Fucus vesiculosus and Furcellaria lumbricalis.
L175@P2: At 1 m depth, the dominant species was F. vesiculosus, and from 3 m downwards F. lumbricalis.
L176@P2: Some C. tenuicorne individuals were also found
L177@P2: growing on rocks. At greater depths C. tenuicorne also grew on Phyllophora sp. and Mytilus
L178@P2: trossulus. From 9 m downwards, the substratum
L179@P2: is sandy. At the beginning of the summer, the
L180@P2: population of C. tenuicorne was abundant at
L181@P2: 1- and 3-m depths. In the middle of the summer,
L182@P2: at 1-m depth Cladophora glomerata and Ectocarpus sp. apparently competed for space with
L183@P2: C. tenuicorne. Ceramium tenuicorne was especially abundant in autumn at 3-m depth.
L184@P2: During the winter, the species was most
L185@P2: abundant and grew most strongly at 6 meters. In
L186@P2: February and March, ice cover prevented sampling near the shore. Ice was not likely to remove
L187@P2: all the algae at a depth of 1 metre, because as
L188@P2: early as in April, right after the ice break, there
L189@P2: was a vibrant population of Ceramium tenuicorne growing at that depth.
L190@P2: Most of the time Ceramium tenuicorne was
L191@P2: in asexual stage. Even though the sampling was
L192@P2: performed twice a month at different depths, not
L193@P2: earlier than in July were female gametophytes
L194@P2: with cystocarps found to grow only as an epiphyte on Fucus vesiculosus receptacle (Fig. 2).
L195@P2: A gametophyte generation with cystocarps grew
L196@P2: in upper part of the shore also in late September and early October. Propagules and plants
L197@P2: with tetrasporangia were observed also in July.
L198@P2: Despite intensive sampling, it could be possible
L199@P2: that the male gametophyte is so short-lived that
L200@P2: it was not found during this study.
L201@P2: The length of the thalli of Ceramium tenuicorne varied from 2 to 8 cm (Fig. 3). The
L202@P2: significantly longest individuals were sampled

L204@P3: ( p < 0.001). During the other seasons, the
L205@P3: length of the thalli varied between 2 and 5 cm.
L206@P3: It is difficult to measure the overall length of
L207@P3: the individuals, since the thalli were growing
L208@P3: in tufts, which are attached to the substrata by
L209@P3: rhizoids. Part of the individuals was branched
L210@P3: only dichotomously. Most individuals had also
L211@P3: secondary branches. These secondary branches
L212@P3: were especially abundant in the spring and
L213@P3: summer, when the mean number of branches

L216@P3: winter and spring (130–300 µm) ( p < 0.001)

L338@P5: Discussion
L339@P5: In the archipelago of Tvärminne, the zonal
L340@P5: occurrence of Ceramium tenuicorne (0.5–6 m)
L341@P5: is very similar to that reported from other areas
L342@P5: (Waern 1952, Ravanko 1968). In Furuskär, a
L343@P5: tiny and nap-like cover of C. tenuicorne grew as
L344@P5: little as 20 cm below the water line in the autumn
L345@P5: (Kiirikki & Lehvo 1997). Hence, under favourable conditions, C. tenuicorne survives also in
L346@P5: the upper part of the hydrolittoral. Waern (1952)
L347@P5: observed C. tenuicorne to grow mainly as an
L348@P5: epiphyte of Fucus vesiculosus in low water, and
L349@P5: deeper as an epiphyte of Furcellaria lumbricalis.
L350@P5: Bäck et al. (1993) observed C. tenuicorne often
L351@P5: to be widespread, and usually green in colour
L352@P5: close to the water line in the autumn.
L353@P5: In spite of the ice cover that strongly reduces
L354@P5: light penetration, Ceramium tenuicorne occurred
L355@P5: also over winter. During winter 1993–1994, the
L356@P5: long-lasting ice cover sheltered C. tenuicorne
L357@P5: from winter storms (Kiirikki & Lehvo 1997).
L358@P5: Ceramium tenuicorne may also have overwintered as short basal parts. According to Dixon
L359@P5: (1960), many species in the genus Ceramium
L360@P5: may decline into a fragment consisting of only
L361@P5: a few cells. In spring, new individuals develop
L362@P5: from these fragments. Kiirikki and Lehvo (1997)
L363@P5: reported that epiphytic C. tenuicorne was most
L364@P5: abundant in July at 6 m as an epiphyte of Fucus.
L365@P5: Old individuals of C. tenuicorne grew on the
L366@P5: lower parts of the host, while the young individuals were found on the apical parts.
L367@P5: In Furuskär, the individuals at 3-m depth
L368@P5: were as tall in the winter as those in the autumn.
L369@P5: The tallest individuals were found in the beginning and middle of the summer. The larger size
L370@P5: of the individuals was apparently due to an
L371@P5: increase in cell numbers, as well as an increase
L372@P5: in cell size. In favourable growth period in
L373@P5: summer the temperature of the seawater was
L374@P5: 10–15 °C, and the intensity of light was high; the
L375@P5: nutrient concentrations, however, were low. The
L376@P5: tall individuals in the summer grew epilithic and
L377@P5: had many secondary branches. These branches
L378@P5: are formed during the time of strong growth.
L379@P5: The observations by Waern (1952) that the
L380@P5: asexual plants of Ceramium form a bulk of the
L381@P5: population in all seasons are confirmed in this
L382@P5: study. In Furuskär small and delicate female
L383@P5: plants were found only in July and most of
L384@P5: the time C. tenuicorne was in vegetative stage.
L385@P5: Sampling was frequent and there is not any particular reason that male plants were not found,
L386@P5: even though they are smaller than female plants
L387@P5: (Waern 1952).
L388@P5: In Furuskär (salinity of 5.5–6 PSU) the cell
L389@P5: length was only 100–700 µm. These values
L390@P5: are lower than those reported in Sjöstedt’s
L391@P5: (1928) study, in which the length of the cells of
L392@P5: Ceramium tenuicorne varied between 300 and
L393@P5: 1000 µm in late summer in upper sublittoral
L394@P5: at the eastern coast of southern Sweden (salinity 7 and 8 PSU). It seems that with decreasing
L395@P5: salinity the size of C. tenuicorne diminishes, as
L396@P5: observed by Waern (1952).
L397@P5: Phycoerythrin is the most abundant (70%)
L398@P5: pigment in the northern Baltic Ceramium tenuicorne; other pigments are: chlorophyll a (about
L399@P5: 20%), and phycocyanin (about 10%). The result
L400@P5: is very similar to those of Cunningham et al.
L401@P5: (1990), who found that phycoerythrin accounts
L402@P5: for about 60%, and phycocyanin about 10% of
L403@P5: the photosynthetic pigments in Porphyridium
L404@P5: cruentum. Wallentinus (1978) showed that the
L405@P5: concentration of chlorophyll a is one of the most
L406@P5: important factors regulating the production of
L407@P5: macroalgae, though she did not mark any relation of the production to the chlorophyll a content in Ceramium tenuicorne. When comparing
L408@P5: the concentration of chlorophyll a and production in natural white light, Moon and Dawes
