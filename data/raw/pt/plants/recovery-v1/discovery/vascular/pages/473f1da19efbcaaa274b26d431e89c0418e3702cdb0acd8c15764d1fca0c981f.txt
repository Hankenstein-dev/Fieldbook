L14@P0: Abstract – The alien fl ora of Montenegro is enlarged by 5 new species: Coreopsis tinctoria, Ipomoea indica,
L15@P0: Lupinus × regalis, Physalis angulata, and Solidago canadensis. All species are grown as ornamentals, and
L16@P0: thus horticulture is considered the most possible pathway for their introduction. Nevertheless, in the case of
L17@P0: Physalis angulata, the occurrence might have originated in the remains of picnic meals. In order to defi ne the
L18@P0: alien status of these species, monitoring is needed, and thus we propose including the species on the National
L19@P0: List of Indicators in environmental protection. Special attention should be paid to the species with invasive
L20@P0: potentials: Solidago canadensis, Ipomoea indica and Physalis angulata.
L21@P0: Keywords: alien plants, Coreopsis, Ipomoea, Lupinus, Montenegro, Physalis, Solidago.

L23@P0: Introduction
L24@P0: The interest in alien fl ora in Montenegro has grown rapidly in the last decade, with many new alien species having
L25@P0: been reported. Still, information on alien plants is incomplete and frequently updated with new records. According
L26@P0: to recent research, 51 alien species are considered invasive
L27@P0: in Montenegro (Stešević and Petrović 2010, Stešević and
L28@P0: Caković 2013). Taking into account the rather small size of
L29@P0: the country (13,812 km2
L30@P0: ), this number can be considered as
L31@P0: troublesome, while in Croatia, with a state area of 57,000
L32@P0: km2
L33@P0: , the number of invasive alien species (IAS) is 74
L34@P0: (Nikolić 2015), in Serbia with a state area of 88 361 km2 the
L35@P0: number of IAS is 68 (Lazarević et al. 2012), in Vojvodina
L36@P0: with an area of 21, 506 km2 the number of IAS is 152 (Anačkov et al. 2013), in Slovenia with a state area of 20,151 km2
L37@P0: the number of IAS is 32+71 naturalized and potentially invasive (Jogan et al. 2012) etc. Nevertheless, those numbers
L38@P0: are not fully comparable because the lists of IAS in different countries have been assembled in different ways. Out of
L39@P0: 51 invasive species in Montenegro, only one, Ambrosia artemisiifolia is monitored and partly managed (Ste šević et al.
L40@P0: 2014). Nevertheless, improvements in this fi eld are expected soon, due to the recently adopted Regulation on the National List of Indicators in environmental protection. According to this document, alien (A) and invasive species
L41@P0: (IS) are considered an indicator (B05) of pressure in the
L42@P0: driving forces – pressures – state – impact – response (DPSIR) model and they will be monitored with a 10 year dynamic (Offi cial Gazette No 29/2013).
L43@P0: This paper presents a supplement to the national list of
L44@P0: alien plant species in Montenegro as well as to the general
L45@P0: distribution of the mentioned aliens in SE Europe.

L60@P1: Coreopsis tinctoria Nutt.
L61@P1: During the fl oristic survey conducted in June 2014 in
L62@P1: the hinterland of Long Beach in Ulcinj (N41°53’51”,
L63@P1: E19°17’52”) we recorded a population of Coreopsis tinctoria (Fig 1a). There were ca. 20 individuals scattered over an
L64@P1: area of 16 m2
L65@P1: . Besides the typical form with yellow ligules
L66@P1: tinged reddish brown towards the center of the fl ower
L67@P1: heads, also the form Coreopsis atropurpurea coexisted (2
L68@P1: individuals). It is characterized by completely reddish
L69@P1: brown heads (Fig 1b) (Smith and Parker 1971). The population had grown on trampled vegetation along the road, accompanied by Plantago major L., Plantago lanceolata L.,
L70@P1: Cichorium intybus L., Crepis sancta (L.) Bornm., Knautia
L71@P1: integrifolia (L.) Bertol, Poa annua L., Lolium perenne L.,
L72@P1: Vicia villosa Roth. subsp. varia (Host) Corb, Erigeron annuus (L.) Desf. etc. Since both forms of C. tinctoria are grown
L73@P1: as ornamentals in the Štoj area, we suppose that horticulture
L74@P1: is the pathway of introduction.
L75@P1: Up to the date the only Coreopsis species reported in the
L76@P1: fl ora of Montenegro was the alien North American species
L77@P1: C. lanceolata L. (Stešević et al. 2008). C. tinctoria is also a
L78@P1: North American species introduced to Europe in 1835,
L79@P1: when it has become a common garden plant (Gajić 1975).
L80@P1: In the native range it inhabits low wet areas along the coast
L81@P1: (Smith and Parker 1971), while in non-native areas it grows
L82@P1: on disturbed places up to 1000m (Strother 2006). Nowadays it is spread over almost all of Europe as largely cultivated in Spain, Portugal, Poland, Serbia; as casual, in Austria, Belgium (Euro+Med 2006), the British Isles (Stace
L83@P1: 1997), Bulgaria (V. Vladimirov 2014, personal communication), the Czech Republic (Pyšek et al. 2002), Croatia (Ni kolić 2015), Denmark (Alanen et al. 2004), France, Germany,
L84@P1: Hungary, Italy, Norway (Euro+Med 2006), Romania (Anastasiu et al. 2011), Ukraine (Volutsa 2010); or with a unclear status: in Belarus, Finland and Sweden (Euro+Med
L85@P1: 2006). The oldest record of this species in Europe dates
L86@P1: from 1883 (Czech Republic, Pyšek et al. 2002).

L103@P1: the fl ora of Montenegro was the tropical American Ipomoea
L104@P1: purpurea (L.) Roth (Stešević et al. 2008). The origin of I.

L106@P1: probably from South America (Weber 2003), and to another
L107@P1: from tropical Asia (Swarbrick and Skarratt 1994). It was introduced to Europe (Maltese Islands) in the last 500 years

L109@P1: and established individuals dates from the beginning of the
L110@P1: 1900s in Spain. The minimum residence time is estimated
L111@P1: as being 104 years (Gassó et al. 2012). In Spain (Gassó et
L112@P1: al. 2012), Portugal (Invasoras 2012), Macronesia (Silva et
L113@P1: al. 2008), and Italy (Acta Plantarum 2007) the species is
L114@P1: considered invasive, and in Greece, Kriti (Yannitsaros 1998),
L115@P1: France (Fried 2012) and Maltese Islands (Mufsud 2002–
L116@P1: 2013) is held to be naturalized. It usually inhabits coastal

L130@P2: Lupinus × regalis Bergmans = L. arboreus Sims ×
L131@P2: L. polyphyllus Lind I.
L132@P2: Several individuals of Lupinus × regalis (Fig. 1e) were
L133@P2: recorded in the urban area of Kolašin, in one old ruin frequently used for dumping unwanted vegetative material
L134@P2: and waste (N42°49’24”, E19°31’40”). On the same site
L135@P2: several ornamental plants grown in the city emerged: Cosmos bipinnatus Cav., Reynoutria × bohemica Chrtek & Chrtková and Symphoricarpos albus (L.) S. F. Blake. Thus, we
L136@P2: consider horticulture the pathway of introduction. Lupinus
L137@P2: × regalis is a garden hybrid (or hybrid complex) with the
L138@P2: parents being L. polyphyllus Lindl. and L. arboreus Sims.
L139@P2: (both native to North America), and possibly involving some
L140@P2: annual species (Huxley 1999). It was introduced to the European market in 1935 as an ornamental (Fremstad 2010)
L141@P2: and is frequently cultivated in Central and North Europe; it
L142@P2: often occurs as a casual and may be locally naturalized
L143@P2: (Franco and Silva 1968). In the UK it has gradually started
L144@P2: to replace L. polyphyllus (Fremstad 2010). Due to the similarity of these species it is probable that many modern records refer to Lupinus × regalis, or backcrosses with L. arboreus (Clement and Foster 1994). The study conducted in
L145@P2: Norway (Lid and Lid 2005) showed that L. × regalis seems
L146@P2: to be less hardy than L. polyphyllus and has not established
L147@P2: itself outside gardens so far. In France the species is sporadically established in some northern and central part of
L148@P2: the country, but still not in Corsica (Julve 2014). With respect to ecology, the species grows on rough ground, motorway verges, riverside shingle (Preston et al. 2002), along
L149@P2: railway lines (Preston et al. 2002), meadows (Groom 2007),
L150@P2: forest edges (Rich et al. 1996), urban habitats (Lawley 2010).
L151@P2: Voucher specimen: Montenegro, Kolašin N42°49’24”,
L152@P2: E19°31’40”, 960 m.a.s.l., old ruin (D. Stešević, 08.07.2014.,
L153@P2: TGU-560414).
L154@P2: Physalis angulata L., syn. P. pendula Rydb.
L155@P2: The autumn fl oristic survey in the hinterland of Long
L156@P2: Beach in Ulcinj resulted in another new record for the alien
L157@P2: fl ora of Montenegro: Physalis angulate (Fig. 1f). It inhabited moist waysides in an open coastal Pinus halepensis Mill.
L158@P2: forest on sandy dunes in the hinterland of Long Beach
L159@P2: (N41°54’33”, E19°15’12”). Population counted ca. 15 plants,
L160@P2: surrounded by numerous individuals of Ephedra campylopoda C. A. Mey., Digitaria ischaemum (Scherb) Muhl.,
L161@P2: Solanum nigrum L., Vicia villosa subsp. varia. In the area
L162@P2: of Štoj the plant is grown as an ornamental, and thus horticulture is considered to have been one introduction pathway. The species is not used as an edible plant in Montenegro, but due to the fact that the Long Beach in Ulcinj is a
L163@P2: very attractive touristic destination for foreign visitors,
L164@P2: there is possibility that its occurrence also originates in the
L165@P2: remains of picnic meals, as is the case in Greece (Travlos
L166@P2: 2012).
L167@P2: Up to this record Physalis alkekengi L. was the only
L168@P2: species out of the genus reported for our fl ora (Rohlena
L169@P2: 1942). It is native in Europe, unlike P. angulata, which is
L170@P2: considered to be native in tropical America (Hawkes 1972).
L171@P2: Nowadays the species has a pantropical distribution as a
L172@P2: weed of crops, gardens and plantations (Mairura 2008). It
L173@P2: was introduced into warm areas of the world in post-Columbian times, with the voyages of exploration, discovery
L174@P2: and commercial exploitation that began in the 16th century
L175@P2: (Sturtevant 1919). In many regions of the world it is naturalized, while in Europe is cultivated locally for edible
L176@P2: fruits (Hawkes 1972). It has the status of casual species in
L177@P2: Austria (Essl and Rabitsch 2002), Belgium (Valdés 2012),
L178@P2: the Czech Republic (Pyšek et al. 2002), Great Britain (Clement and Foster, 1994), and Slovenia (Lešnik 2009); and of
L179@P2: naturalized species in Croatia (Milović et al. 2010), while it
L180@P2: is considered invasive in Greece (Travlos 2012). The species is also present in Albania (Tan and Mullaj 2000) and
L181@P2: Denmark (Nobanis 2005), but the status of naturalization is
L182@P2: unclear. The means of introduction differs from country to
L183@P2: country: escape from cultivation (Albania, Austria, Slovenia), soya bean importation (Belgium, Verloove 2011, Croatia, Milović et al. 2010, Slovenia, Lešnik 2014, personal
L184@P2: communication), cereals (Croatia, Milović et al. 2010), oil
L185@P2: seed and wool (Britain, Clement and Foster 1994), remains
L186@P2: of picnic meals (Greece, Bőhling 2001). The oldest record
L187@P2: of this species in Europe dates from 1961 (Belgium, Verloove 2011).With respect to ecology the species grows best
L188@P2: in moist, fertile soils, it is tolerant to partial shade and occurs widely as a weed of crops and pastures, and in waste
L189@P2: areas. It can be found up to 3000 m altitude. It can tolerate
L190@P2: light frost (Mairura 2008).

L209@P3: The species (Figs. 1g–i) usually forms extensive colonies with high shoot density covering large areas, eliminating almost all other species. Individual clones expand rapidly by vegetative lateral growth. The annual stem (Fig. 1h)

L211@P3: spring. Once established the plant remains dominant for a
L212@P3: long period of time. Seeds are abundantly produced and
L213@P3: dispersed by wind (Weber 2003). It is native to North
L214@P3: America and was introduced to Europe as an ornamental
L215@P3: plant (Weber 1998). Its earliest record dates from 1645 in
L216@P3: England (Kowarik 2003). Because the species is attractive
L217@P3: and easy to grow, it was widely used by gardeners. The fi rst
L218@P3: observations of wild populations in Europe dates to around
L219@P3: 1850 (Wagenitz, 1964), and the plant spread rapidly until
L220@P3: 1950 (Weber 1998). The species soon extended its range in
L221@P3: Europe (Weber 2000). Nowadays it is present over most of
L222@P3: Europe with the status of invasive alien plant (Parker 2008).
L223@P3: In its native range the species is often a weedy component
L224@P3: of vegetation in abandoned pastures and roadsides, in abandoned fi elds, grasslands, forest edges and human disturbed
L225@P3: habitats in urban areas and settlements (Walck et al. 1999).
