L36: ## Overview
L37: Pyrenean hawkbit (Scorzoneroides pyrenaica) is a compact, low-growing perennial herb in the daisy family (Asteraceae) native to the Pyrenees, the mountains of northern Spain, Andorra and southern France. The species was formally placed in the genus Scorzoneroides by Holub in 1977, based on earlier work by Gouan, though it was long classified as Leontodon pyrenaicus.
L38: Despite being relatively unknown in British and Irish garden circles, it deserves a place in any wildflower meadow planting, rock garden or naturalistic border where the conditions are suitably open and free-draining.
L39: The plant produces bright yellow, dandelion-like flower heads that are genuinely attractive close-up, and its open structure makes it a reliable source of nectar for bees, hoverflies and small butterflies throughout the summer months. The fluffy seed heads that follow add a secondary ornamental period well into autumn.

L41: ## Appearance and bloom cycle
L42: 
L43: Scorzoneroides pyrenaica forms a low, ground-hugging rosette of narrow, lance-shaped leaves that are slightly to moderately toothed along the margins. The leaf surface tends to be softly hairy or nearly hairless depending on the individual plant. From the rosette arise slender, erect flowering stems typically reaching 15 to 30 cm in height, each topped by a single flower head.
L44: The flower heads measure roughly 2 to 4 cm across and are made up entirely of ray florets - there are no disc florets, which is a feature shared across the Scorzoneroides and Leontodon genera. The colour is a clean, pure yellow that shows well against grey rock or green turf. Blooming generally runs from May or June through August; in the original high-altitude Pyrenean habitat flowering is pushed later into July and August, but at lower garden elevations it starts earlier.
L45: After flowering, the plants produce fluffy white pappus clocks like a miniature dandelion, spreading seed readily if not deadheaded. The overall growth habit is neat and undemanding, making it suitable for the front of a border or between paving stones.
L46: ## Ideal location
L47: 
L48: This plant thrives in full sun and will only perform adequately in partial shade. A south or south-west facing position is ideal; the more direct sunlight the plant receives, the more freely and persistently it will flower. In its native Pyrenean habitat it grows on open, sun-baked hillsides, rocky outcrops, dry meadows and mountain pastures, all of which share full exposure and excellent air circulation.
L49: Good air movement is beneficial - stagnant, humid conditions encourage leaf diseases. Raised beds, rock gardens and sloping sites suit this plant particularly well because they also assist drainage, which is as important as light. A position where rainwater runs away promptly rather than pooling is always preferable.
L50: In cooler, wetter parts of the country a south-facing wall or raised container can recreate the warm, dry conditions the plant prefers. Containers work well provided they have generous drainage holes.
L51: ## Soil
L52: 
L53: The most critical requirement for Scorzoneroides pyrenaica is an acid, nutrient-poor, free-draining soil. The data indicate an optimal pH range of 4.5 to 5.0 and a very low nutrient score of 2 out of 10. This is significantly more acidic than most garden soils, and considerably poorer in nutrients than typical border compost.
L54: In practice this means the plant performs best in a gritty, sandy or stony substrate with little organic matter. A standard alpine mix of one part loam, one part coarse grit and one part horticultural sand works well. Adding a small amount of ericaceous compost will help achieve the necessary pH. Avoid any lime-rich material or standard multipurpose compost, which is too rich and too alkaline.
L55: Heavy clay soils are unsuitable without substantial amendment. Work in large quantities of coarse grit or pea gravel to a depth of at least 30 cm. The plant cannot tolerate waterlogged conditions, especially in winter, as this quickly leads to crown and root rot. Good drainage is non-negotiable.

L62: 
L63: Once established, Pyrenean hawkbit is notably drought-tolerant and rarely needs supplementary watering in a typical garden setting. Its root system, though relatively shallow, is efficient at extracting moisture from a porous, well-structured substrate during dry spells.
L64: During the first growing season after planting, water moderately about once a week during dry weather, allowing the top layer of soil to dry between applications. This encourages the roots to spread downwards rather than staying close to the surface. Avoid wetting the centre of the rosette when watering, as water pooling in the leaf axils can trigger fungal problems.
L65: In subsequent years, established plants need watering only during prolonged droughts of three weeks or more. Even then, a single thorough soaking is more effective than repeated light sprinklings. Container-grown plants need more attention; check the top centimetre of compost weekly and water when it is dry.
L66: ## Pruning
L67: 
L68: Minimal intervention is required. The main decision is whether to remove spent flower heads before seed is set. Deadheading prevents unwanted self-seeding and can extend the flowering season by redirecting the plant's energy. If, on the other hand, you welcome natural spread - and in a wildflower meadow this is often desirable - simply leave the heads to ripen and disperse.
L69: In early spring, once the worst frosts are past, remove any dead or mushy outer leaves from the rosette, cutting them off cleanly at the base. Do not cut into the green, living heart of the rosette. This light tidying is all the pruning the plant typically needs.
L70: 
L71: Cutting the flowering stems off at the base after the seed has dispersed in autumn keeps the plant looking neat. A second modest flush of flowers sometimes appears in early autumn if the summer has not been too hot and dry.
L72: ## Maintenance calendar
L73: 
L74: January and February: Leave undisturbed. The rosette provides some winter interest and self-insulation.
L75: 
L76: March: As temperatures rise, gently remove dead leaf litter from around the plant. Check for any winter damage.
L77: 
L78: April: Plant new specimens into prepared, gritty, acid soil. Ideal planting time before active growth begins in earnest.
L79: 
L80: May to June: First flowers open. Enjoy the display and let pollinators access freely.
L81: July to August: Peak flowering period. Deadhead selectively if self-seeding needs to be controlled.
L82: 
L83: September: Seed heads disperse. Allow some seed to fall for natural spread or collect it for sowing.
L84: 
L85: October to November: Foliage dies back partially. No action needed; the rosette remains semi-green.
L86: 
L87: December: Dormancy. No maintenance required.
L88: ## Winter hardiness
L89: 
L90: Scorzoneroides pyrenaica is reliably hardy to USDA zone 5, tolerating temperatures down to approximately -15 degrees Celsius. This reflects its Pyrenean mountain origins, where winters are harsh and prolonged, though the plant is usually protected under a deep blanket of snow during the coldest months.
L91: In garden conditions, winter survival depends more on drainage than on temperature. A well-drained, gritty soil allows surplus moisture to escape before freezing conditions set in; a waterlogged soil holds ice around the crown and roots, causing rot regardless of how cold-hardy the plant is genetically.
L92: A top dressing of coarse gravel around the rosette collar helps channel water away and slightly insulates the soil. In particularly harsh winters with little or no snow cover, a light covering of dry conifer branches or a single layer of dry straw laid loosely over the plant offers meaningful protection.
L93: ## Companion plants
L94: 
L95: Pyrenean hawkbit works best when combined with other plants that share its preference for acid, nutrient-poor, free-draining conditions. Natural companions include mountain sandwort (Arenaria montana), limestone stonecrop (Sedum album), wild thyme (Thymus serpyllum) and sun roses (Helianthemum nummularium). All of these enjoy similar open, sunny habitats.
L96: For a fuller mountain-meadow planting, consider adding low-growing fescues such as sheep's fescue (Festuca ovina) or fine-leaved fescue (Festuca filiformis), which will knit the planting together without smothering the smaller perennials. Small rock-loving dianthus species like maiden pink (Dianthus deltoides) make excellent neighbours, flowering in a complementary season.

L98: ## Closing
L99: Scorzoneroides pyrenaica may not feature prominently in mainstream garden centre catalogues, but it is a rewarding and ecologically valuable perennial for anyone who enjoys growing something a little out of the ordinary. Its cheerful yellow flowers, modest size and genuine low-maintenance character make it an honest addition to rock gardens, alpine troughs, dry wildflower meadows and any naturalistic planting scheme that prioritises plants over pavement.
L100: Give it the right soil and light, and it will return reliably every year.
