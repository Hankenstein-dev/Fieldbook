L212:   * Platycarpos (S.Wats) cite138†Kurl. L213: Lupinus, commonly known as lupin, lupine,^{cite139†[note 1] } or regionally bluebonnet, is a cite140†genus of plants in the legume family cite132†Fabaceae . The genus includes over 199 cite141†species , with cite142†centres of diversity in North and South America.^{cite143†[1] } Smaller centres occur in North Africa and the cite144†Mediterranean .^{cite143†[1] }^{cite145†[2] } They are widely cultivated both as a food source and as ornamental plants, but are invasive to some areas.
L214: For instance, lupine has become an ecological problem in cite146†Iceland since the beginning of the 21st century.^{cite147†[3] }
L215: The part of the plant which is eaten is the leguminous seed. It is nutritious, sustainable, and affordable.^{cite148†[4] } Because they are bitter and contain strong alkaloids, they must be pickled prior to consumption, to avoid toxicity.^{cite149†[5] } They can also be made into a flour and used as fertilizer.^{cite150†[6] } They were historically eaten in the Mediterranean, North Africa, and Latin America, but have now gained popularity in other parts of the world, such as Denmark and Belarus.
L216: In spite of lupins not being native to Australia, in the past decade it has produced 85 percent of commercially grown lupins.^{cite151†[7] }^{cite152†[8] }
L217: There are ancient texts which refer to the utilization of lupine seeds, such as the Mishnah, and the Roman writer Pliny the Elder.^{cite153†[9] }^{cite154†[10] } Evidence of their consumption have been found in Egyptian Pharonic tombs and in the Western Galilee, Israel, with the latter dating back to hunter and gatherer Natufian cave residents.^{cite155†[11] }^{cite156†[12] }
L218: The species are mostly cite157†herbaceous cite158†perennial plants 0.3–1.5 metres (1–5 feet) tall, but some are cite159†annual plants and a few are cite160†shrubs up to 3 m (10 ft) tall. An exception is the chamis de monte (cite161†Lupinus jaimehintonianus ) of cite162†Oaxaca in Mexico, which is a tree up to 8 m (26 ft) tall.^{cite163†[13] }
L219: Lupins have soft green to grey-green leaves which may be coated in silvery hairs, often densely so. The leaf blades are usually cite164†palmately divided into five to 28 leaflets, or reduced to a single leaflet in a few species of the southeastern United States and eastern South America.^{cite165†[14] }
L220: The flowers are produced in dense or open whorls on an erect spike, each flower 1–2 centimetres (3⁄8–3⁄4 inch) long. The pea-like flowers have an upper standard, or banner, two lateral wings, and two lower cite166†petals fused into a keel. The flower shape has inspired common names such as cite167†bluebonnets and cite168†quaker bonnets .
L221: 
L222: The fruit is a cite169†pod containing several seeds. The seeds contain cite170†alkaloids which lend them a bitter taste.
L223: cite171†Extrafloral nectaries have been found on a species in Arizona.^{cite172†[15] }
L224: ## Taxonomy
L225: 
L226: [cite173†edit ]
L227: 
L228: Main article: cite174†List of Lupinus species L229: 
L230: cite175†Lupinus aschenbornii , cite176†Nevado de Toluca , Mexico
L231: The genus Lupinus L. and, in particular, its North American species were divided by cite177†Sereno Watson (1873) into three sections: Lupinus, Platycarpos, and Lupinnelus. Differences in habitat and in the number of cite178†ovules were the basis for this classification. A majority of the perennial and annual species from the American continent described by Watson were referred to Lupinus.
L232: Some annual species with two ovules in the ovary and two seeds in the pod (L. densiflorus, L. microcarpus, etc.) were attributed to the Platycarpos section. Section Lupinnelus consisted of one species (L. uncialis), with axillary and solitary flowers, scarcely reflexed banner, and also with two ovules in the ovary.
L233: While Watson's work was predominantly based on study of North American species, the later research of Ascherson and Graebner (1907) extended his principle of classification to cover all lupins from the Eastern and Western Hemispheres, also using number of ovules (seedbuds) in the ovary (and thus of seeds in the pod) as the criterion for this division. They described two subgenera, Eulupinus and Platycarpos. Most of the described species were referred to subgen. A. Eulupinus. Subgen. B.
L234: Platycarpos included several annual species from the Eastern Hemisphere with two seedbuds and seeds in the bean (the same species, as the one specified by S. Watson).
L235: A current schema retains this distinction, but uses the nomenclature for the subgenera of Platycarpos and Lupinus. In this schema, subgenus cite179†Platycarpos (S.Wats.) cite138†Kurl. contains perennial and annual species from the Western Hemisphere, with a minimum two or more ovules or seedbuds. Subgenus Lupinus consists of 12 species from Africa and the Mediterranean, with a minimum of four ovules or seedbuds.^{cite180†[16] }
L236: The taxonomy of Lupinus has always been confusing. How many distinct species exist or how they might be organized within the genus is not clear. The plants are variable and the taxa are not always distinct from one another.
L237: Some American taxa have been described as cite181†complexes rather than separate species.^{cite182†[17] } Estimates of the number of lupine species generally fall between 200 and 500.^{cite145†[2] } One authority places the estimate at approximately 267 species worldwide.^{cite143†[1] } Currently, two cite183†subgenera are recognized.
L238: ### Subgenus Platycarpos
L239: 
L240: [cite184†edit ]
L241: 
L242: Main article: cite185†Lupinus subg. Platycarpos L243: The ovary contains two and more ovules or seedbuds. The seed are predominantly small-sized, with an underdeveloped embryo and small amount of endosperm. Cotyledons are small-sized, with long caulicles. The first pair of true leaves is alternate. The stem is predominantly naked with cite186†waxen coating . Dominating is the monopodial type of branching. Leaflets are smooth, with waxen coating or slight cite187†pubescence , predominantly narrow. Pods are flat or orbicular, with two or more seeds.
L244: Represented by frutcuilose, fruticose and herbaceous perennial forms, or less often annual ones. Plants are cross-pollinated. cite188†Chromosome number 2n is either 36, 48, or 96.^{cite189†[18] } This subgenus is distributed throughout North, Central and South America, predominantly in the mining systems of the Andes and Cordillera. Some species are cultivated (L. mutabilis, L. polyphyllus). This subgenus includes several hundred species, requiring further analysis of their authenticity.
