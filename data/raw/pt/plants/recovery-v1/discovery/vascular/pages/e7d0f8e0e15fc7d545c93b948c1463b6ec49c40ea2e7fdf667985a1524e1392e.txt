L5@P0: aureus (Sm.) Pimenov & Ostr. (Apiaceae)

L9@P0: Abstract – The pollen grains of Leiotulus aureus (syn. Malabaila aurea (Sm.) Boiss.) were examined by light and scanning
L10@P0: electron microscopy in order to contribute to the taxonomical and melissopalynological studies of the species. Flower
L11@P0: visitors have also been observed and analyzed aiming at the clarification of some pollination aspects including the species
L12@P0: contribution to bee pasture. The pollen grains of L. aureus are isopolar, radially symmetrical, medium to large in size, tricolporate and perprolate. They are slightly equatorially constricted with obtuse polar caps and triangular in polar view.
L13@P0: The sculpturing pattern is rugulate–microperforate. With regard to flower visitors, the following pollination types occurred: melittophily, myophily and sapromyophily and cantharophily. Some insects attracted by L. aureus cannot be considered pollinators but casual visitors. The flowers were the most frequently visited by honey bees during midday.
L14@P0: Keywords: Malabaila aurea, light microscopy – LM, palynomorphology, scanning electron microscopy –
L15@P0: SEM, insect pollinators
L16@P0: Introduction
L17@P0: According to the latest taxonomic revision, genus
L18@P0: Leiotulus Ehrenb., belonging to the Apiaceae tribe Tordylieae
L19@P0: Koch., contains 10 species and subspecies, previously mainly
L20@P0: assigned to the genera: Malabaila and Pastinaca (POWO
L21@P0: 2022). Species Leiotulus aureus is known under the following homotypic synonims: Heracleum aureum Sm. (basionym), Lophotaenia aurea (Sm.) Griseb., Malabaila aurea
L22@P0: (Sm.) Boiss., and Pastinaca aurea (Sm.) Calest., while the
L23@P0: accepted name is proposed as a new name combination by
L24@P0: Pimenov and Ostroumova (1994), based on carpological investigations. The genus included species characterized as
L25@P0: intermediate between Pastinaca L. (incl. Malabaila s.str.)
L26@P0: and Zosima Hoffm. (e.g. Malabaila involucrata Boiss. &
L27@P0: Spruner, M. pastinacifolia Boiss. & Balansa etc.).
L28@P0: L. aureus is a herbaceous biennial, a plant of the native
L29@P0: range from SE Europe to NW Turkey (POWO 2022), prefering warm and sunny places along with open rocky habitats.
L30@P0: It is caracterised by a semirosette growth form, erect, hollow, striate and somewhat viscid stem up to 50 cm tall, pinnately divided leaves with ovate leaflets near the ground and
L31@P0: linear-lanceolate in upper stem ones. Bright yellow flowers
L32@P0: are grouped in terminal and subterminal, regularly compound umbels (Tutin et al. 1981). The mature fruit is obcordate – suborbicular, flattened, surrounded by a wide somewhat thickened margin, cordate at the apex, with persistent
L33@P0: style (Fig. 1). Although the plant is slightly aromatic, a literature search did not reveal any reference to its local use in
L34@P0: traditional medicine or cooking.
L35@P0: Investigations regarding apiaceaen pollen morphology
L36@P0: have been reported by a number of authors, including
L37@P0: Erdtman (1971), Van Zeist and Bottema (1977) and CerceauLarrival (1981). However, although pollination biology within
L38@P0: the Apiaceae has not received much attention, some aspects
L39@P0: were studied in a few taxa including Chaerophyllum,
L40@P0: Heracleum, Seseli, Thaspium, Zizia and Daucus (Lindsey
L41@P0: 1984, Lindsey and Bell 1985, Pimenov and Ostroumova,
L42@P0: 1994, Lamborn and Ollerton 2000, Langenberger and Davis
L43@P0: 2002a,b, Rovira et al. 2002, Wróblewska 1993, MačukanovićJocić et al. 2016). Besides phylogenetic studies (Pimenov and
L44@P0: Ostroumova 1994, Ajani et al. 2008, Downie et al. 2010),
L45@P0: scientific papers relating to other reproductive aspects of the
L46@P0: Leiotulus species are rather rare. Although some palynological studies within the genus Leiotulus are modest, except
L47@P0: on L. secacul (Mill.) Pimenov & Ostr., L. kotschyi (Boiss.)
L48@P0: Pimenov & Ostr. (Van Zeist and Bottema 1977), the pollen
L49@P0: features of L. aureus have been unexplored.
L50@P0: The present study aimed to provide palynomorphological features of the species that can be used for the taxon
L51@P0: identification and clarification of higher level relationships
L52@P0: within the family, as well as to contribute to melissopalyno-

L78@P1: Sampling and analysis of pollen
L79@P1: For scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and light microscopy (LM) analysis, the umbels (flowers) at full flowering stage were collected from 10 plants of wild populations.
L80@P1: For SEM study, the pollen grains from fully open flowers were mounted directly on the stub. Aiming to avoid any
L81@P1: deformation of the pollen grains or any swelling attributed
L82@P1: to solvents, preparation was carried out without the previous acetolysis method (Dustmann and von Der Ohe 1993).
L83@P1: Samples were coated with gold (in BAL-TEC SCD 005 Sputter Coater, 100 seconds in 30 mA) and observed using a
L84@P1: JEOL JSM – 6390 LV electron microscope at an acceleration
L85@P1: voltage of 20 kV. Pollen grains were photographed in polar
L86@P1: and equatorial view, and measurements were done on a
L87@P1: sample of 50 or more grains for each morphological character. The following features describing pollen grains were
L88@P1: examined: size, shape, ornamentation, apertures, polarity,
L89@P1: symmetry, length of polar (P) and equatorial axis (E) (in
L90@P1: SEM) and exine thickness (in LM). Description of pollen
L91@P1: morphology was performed according to Punt et al. (2007)
L92@P1: and Erdtman (1971).
L93@P1: For LM, the pollen grains were mounted on slides in a
L94@P1: drop of saturated solution of fructose in water, observed
L95@P1: without additional staining with a Leica DM2000 microscope equipped with a digital camera (Leica DFC320) and
L96@P1: Leica IM1000 software.
L97@P1: Field monitoring and identification of insect visitors
L98@P1: Field observations were carried out during the peak
L99@P1: flowering period of L. aureus, from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., due to
L100@P1: the absence of insect activity outside during the rest of the
L101@P1: day. Furthermore, nocturnal insects or those active in the
L102@P1: evening (such as sphingid moths) were not recorded. In addition to recording honey bee visits, imaging and sampling
L103@P1: of all other flower visitors were performed. The insects were
L104@P1: photographed with a Nikon Colorpix P500 digital camera.
L105@P1: For the purpose of insect sampling, some insects were identified on sight, but for most species an accurate identification was made only after a specimen had been captured.

L181@P4: Discussion
L182@P4: Pollen grains within the Apiaceae are usually stenopalynous, radially symmetrical, isopolar and prolate to perprolate in shape (Erdtman 1971). Grains are commonly tricolporate with slit-like ectocolpi and very distinctive and
L183@P4: broadband-like costae (Punt 1984, Perveen and Qaiser
L184@P4: 2006). The tectum is commonly striate-rugulate or simple
L185@P4: striate (Punt 1984), psilate to granulate (Perveen and Qaiser
L186@P4: 2006). There are a few different criteria accepted for classification of the pollen grains of the Apiaceae. Various characters have been used, such as polar/equatorial ratio (P/E),
L187@P4: exine pattern, etc. For example, Cerceau-Larrival (1962)
L188@P4: proposed 5 pollen types occurring within the family based
L189@P4: on shape index: sub-rhomboidal (type 1, P/E:1-1.5), sub-circular (type 2, P/E:1-1.5), oval (type 3, P/E:1.5-2), sub-rectangular (type 4, P/E:2), and equatorially constricted (type 5,
L190@P4: P/E:over 2). According to this classification, pollen grains of
L191@P4: L. aureus in the current study should fit into type 5. In addition, according to Van Zeist and Bottema (1977) the pollen grains of the Apiaceae could be divided into 9 pollen
L192@P4: types: Anisosciadium, Bunium, Bupleurum, Eryngium,
L193@P4: Ferula, Malabaila, Pimpinella, Sium erectum and Turgenia
L194@P4: types. The pollen features characterizing the Malabaila
L195@P4: type are as follows: subrectangular to slightly oval shape
L196@P4: with rounded poles, P/E is 2, fairly long colpi, transversal
L197@P4: furrow oval-rectangular, distinct columellae 0.5 to 0.8 μm
L198@P4: in diameter, slight variations in the wall thickness (exine up
L199@P4: to 2 μm thick), grain size ranging from 35 to 40 μm. This
L200@P4: type comprises Malabaila secacul, M. kotschyi, Orlaya
L201@P4: grandiflora, Heracleum maximum, H. lasiopetalum, H.
L202@P4: persicum, Ormosciadium aucheri, Scandix iberica,
L203@P4: Stenotaenia nudicaulis and Turgeniopsis foeniculacea.
L204@P4: Recently, Orlaya has already been described as having its
L205@P4: own pollen type (Beug 2015), while Malabaila secacul and
L206@P4: M. kotschyi were transferred to genus Leiotulus (as L. secacul
L207@P4: (Mill.) Pimenov & Ostr and L. kotschyi (Boiss.) Pimenov &
L208@P4: Ostr.)) (Pimenov and Ostroumova 1994, POWO 2022). The
L209@P4: results of palynomorphplogical research of L. aureus (which
L210@P4: previously also belonged to Malabaila genus) are not completely in accordance with characters described for the

L216@P5: Malabaila pollen type, such as P/E ratio and grain size, provided by Van Zeist and Bottema (1977). The pollen grains,
L217@P5: described in the current paper, are slightly larger. It is
L218@P5: known that the size may vary depending on the liquid medium used to mount samples on microslides for light microscopy (Faegri and Iversen 1989, Pospiech et al. 2021).
L219@P5: However, the measurements of pollen size in the present
L220@P5: paper are based on micrographs obtained by SEM, without
L221@P5: using any mounting media which may cause enlargement
L222@P5: of pollen grains. Extensive palynological research provided
L223@P5: by Punt (1984), who recognized 50 types of pollen, as well
L224@P5: as by Perveen and Qaiser (2006) who distinguished three
L225@P5: pollen types based on tectum features within 27 Apiaceae
L226@P5: genera, did not cover Malabaila/Leiotulus species.
L227@P5: The Apiaceae species have a uniform umbel and flower
L228@P5: structure, however within the family diverse pollination
L229@P5: systems occur varying from completely self-pollination to
L230@P5: obligate cross-pollination (Koul et al. 1993). Flowers of
L231@P5: umbellifers attract numerous insect taxa from several
L232@P5: taxonomic orders supplying them with pollen and nectar
L233@P5: (Niemirski and Zych 2011). Hence, umbellifers are often
L234@P5: connected with generalized pollination system, indicating
L235@P5: that their floral nectar is easily accessible to flower-visiting
L236@P5: insects of different pollination efficiency due to the various
L237@P5: degree of mouthpart modification for feeding on nectar,
L238@P5: pollen or petals (Proctor et al. 1996, Olesen et al. 2007).
L239@P5: These include mostly flies, but also hymenopterans and coleopterans (Proctor et al. 1996, Lamborn and Ollerton 2000,
L240@P5: Zych 2007, Davila and Wardle 2008, Carvalheiro et al. 2008,
L241@P5: Mačukanović-Jocić et al. 2016). In the current study, flowers of L. aureus, representing an open dish-shaped blossom
L242@P5: type, were visited by twelve insect species from four taxonomic orders. With regard to the observed flower visitors,
L243@P5: the following pollination types occurred: melittophily,
L244@P5: myophily, sapromyophily and cantharophily. Dipterans
L245@P5: were the most frequent group of visitors. Most of the recorded dipterans were of the “hoverfly type” (i.e. members of the
L246@P5: family Syrphidae) and the “muscoid type” (i.e. members of
L247@P5: the families Sarcophagidae (flesh flies) and Calliphoridae
L248@P5: (blow flies)). Although not highly specialized in pollination,
L249@P5: flower-pollinating species have been reported in the Bibionidae in previous research (Goldblatt et al. 2005). Adults
L250@P5: of march flies (Bibionidae) are known as pollen-collecting
L251@P5: and nectar feeding flower visitors pollinating fruit trees and
L252@P5: some other crops (Freeman and Lane 1985, Woodcock et al.
L253@P5: 2014). In the present study carrion flies were found to be less
L254@P5: important from the pollination point of view, since Leiotulus
L255@P5: is an aromatic plant more attractive to Apidae and Syrphidae.
L256@P5: Despite a few Coleopteran species observed in the current
L257@P5: and similar studies on umbellifers (Zych 2006), they are of
L258@P5: minor importance as they are not considered to be efficient
L259@P5: pollinators (Mačukanović-Jocić 2010). Hemipterans observed in this study could not be considered as pollinators,
L260@P5: since their pollination activity is negligible, except in rare
L261@P5: cases (Ishida et al. 2009). Unlike previous research on some
L262@P5: other Apiaceae species (Zych 2006, Mačukanović-Jocić et
L263@P5: al. 2016), in the present study neither moths nor butterflies
L264@P5: were observed visiting L. aureus umbels. Low visitation rate
L265@P5: of lepidopterans can be explained by the length of their proboscis, which is more adapted to tubular corollas, or by
L266@P5: weaker attractiveness of flowers which can be attributed to
L267@P5: the scent or floral nectar components. L. aureus could be
L268@P5: considered an ecologically generalized species in terms of
L269@P5: the need for specialized pollinators. There are many reasons
L270@P5: for this. Although L. aureus like many other umbellifers
L271@P5: lack any visible or invisible floral signs such as honey guides
L272@P5: leading to nectar, the flowers are slightly aromatic and could
L273@P5: emit some chemical signals that attract specific groups of
L274@P5: pollinators, as previously reported for this family (Tollsten
L275@P5: et al. 1994, Tollsten and Øvstedal 1994, Niemirski and Zych
L276@P5: 2011). Regardless of their floral uniformity, some umbellifers are suggested to exhibit cryptic flower specialization
L277@P5: enabling oligolectic relationships with bee pollinators
L278@P5: (Lindsey 1984, Lindsey and Bell 1985, Niemirski and Zych
L279@P5: 2011). Unlike Zych (2006) who did not observe any honey
L280@P5: bee on Heracleum sphondylium, L. aureus flowers were very
L281@P5: frequently visited, which is in line with the findings of other
L282@P5: authors who pointed out the importance of honey bees in
L283@P5: pollinating umbellifers (Langenberger and Davis 2002b,
L284@P5: Davila and Wardle 2002). Following the diurnal dynamics
L285@P5: of honey bee visits, plants were the most frequently visited
L286@P5: about midday. Considering the frequency of honey bee visits
L287@P5: L. aureus could be regarded as a melliferous plant contributing
L288@P5: to honey bee pasture. However, regarding its rather “unspecialized” floral morphology and its being visited by numerous species of flower visitors, this plant species is without
L289@P5: ecological specialization to particular insect species.
