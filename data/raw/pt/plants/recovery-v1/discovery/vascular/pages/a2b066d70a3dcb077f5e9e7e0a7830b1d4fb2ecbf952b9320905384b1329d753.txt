L181:   * Pyrogennema cite106†Lunell L182:   * Zauschneria cite107†C.Presl L183: Epilobium is a cite108†genus of cite92†flowering plants in the cite109†family cite96†Onagraceae , containing about 197 cite110†species .^{cite111†[1] } The genus has a worldwide distribution. It is most prevalent in the cite112†subarctic , cite113†temperate and cite114†subantarctic regions, whereas in the cite115†subtropics and cite116†tropics Epilobium species are restricted to the cool cite117†montane cite118†biomes , such as the cite119†New Guinea Highlands .
L184: The cite89†taxonomy of the genus has varied between different cite120†botanists , but the modern trend is to include the previously recognised genera Boisduvalia, cite121†Chamaenerion (previously Chamerion), Pyrogennema and Zauschneria within Epilobium according to cite122†Peter H. Raven , who has extensively studied the willowherbs and merges the other cite123†segregate genera into Epilobium.
L185: Fringed willowherb (cite124†Epilobium ciliatum ) is likely a cite125†cryptic species complex ; apparently these plants also commonly cite126†hybridize with their congeners.^{cite127†[2] }
L186: Most species are known by the common name willowherbs for their willow-like leaves.^{cite128†[3] } Those that were once separated in Boisduvalia are called spike-primroses or boisduvalias. Those Epilobium species previously placed in the Chamaenerion group are known as cite129†fireweeds .
L187: ## Description
L188: 
L189: [cite130†edit ]
L190: 
L191: Ripe cite131†capsule of cite132†Epilobium canum (zauschneria) releasing seed
L192: Epilobiums are mostly cite133†herbaceous plants, either cite134†annual or cite135†perennial ; a few are cite136†subshrubs . The cite137†leaves are opposite or rarely whorled,^{cite138†[4] }^{: 354 } simple and ovate to lanceolate in shape. The cite139†flowers are cite140†actinomorphic (radially symmetrical) with four petals that may be notched.^{cite138†[4] }^{cite141†[5] } These are usually smallish and pink in most species, but red, orange or yellow in a few.
L193: The cite142†fruit is a slender cylindrical cite131†capsule containing numerous cite143†seeds embedded in fine, soft silky fluff which disperses the seeds very effectively in the wind.
L194: The genus name derives from the Greek words "epi" meaning "upon" and "lobos" meaning "lobe", with reference to position of the petals above the ovary.^{cite144†[6] }
L195: 
L196: A number of Epilobium species with slightly asymmetrical (cite145†zygomorphic ) magenta flowers and alternate leaves are placed in the genus Chamaenerion, which may be further separated by some authorities into a genus Chamerion. However, Chamerion is not universally accepted.^{cite146†[7] }^{cite147†[8] }
L197: ## Habitat and ecology
L198: 
L199: [cite148†edit ]
L200: Willowherbs sensu lato are typically very quick to carpet large swathes of ground and may become key or dominant species of local cite149†ecosystems .
L201: In the cite150†United Kingdom , for example, rosebay willowherb (cite151†Chamaenerion angustifolium ) is widely found on cite152†mesotrophic soils dominated by false oat-grass (cite153†Arrhenatherum elatius ), cock's-foot grass (cite154†Dactylis glomerata ), and red fescue (cite155†Festuca rubra ), while great willowherb (cite88†Epilobium hirsutum ) is found in mesotrophic grassland with stinging nettle (cite156†Urtica dioica ).
L202: These two willowherb species also dominate open habitat early in cite157†ecological succession , to the virtual exclusion of other plant life. Broad-leaved willowherb (cite158†Epilobium montanum ) is found characteristically, though not abundantly, in the mesotrophic grasslands with meadowsweet (cite159†Filipendula ulmaria ) and sometimes the uncommon Greek valerian (cite160†Polemonium caeruleum ).
L203: Most willowherbs will not tolerate shade trees and thus are limited to more recently disturbed patches, yielding to other plants over time. Consequently, though the genus contains many cite161†pioneer plants , rather few of them are cite162†invasive weeds of major importance.

L219: ## Use by humans
L220: 
L221: [cite175†edit ]
L222: 
L223:  | This section needs cite176†more citations . Please help cite177†improve this section by cite178†adding citations to reliable sources . Unsourced material may be challenged and cite179†removed . (October 2025) (cite180†Learn how and when to remove this message )
L224: The main use of Epilobium by humans is as a herbal supplement in the treatment of cite181†prostate , cite182†bladder (cite183†incontinence ) and cite184†hormone disorders. Many of the small willowherb species are nuisance cite185†weeds in cite186†gardens . Though few are regularly used as cite187†ornamental plants , the larger willowherbs may be attractive in cite188†ruderal locales. One of the most frequently recognized members of the genus is the cite189†circumboreal fireweed (E.
L225: angustifolium), known as rosebay willowherb in the United Kingdom. It rapidly colonizes burnt ground; during the cite190†bombing of London in World War II many of the derelict bomb sites were soon covered with these plants, bringing a splash of colour to what was otherwise a very grim scene. It is the cite191†floral emblem of cite192†Yukon in Canada, cite193†Hedmark in Norway and cite194†Southern Ostrobothnia in Finland.
L226: Fireweed is used as a sweetener in north-western North America. It is put in cite195†candy , cite196†jellies , cite197†ice cream , cite198†syrup , and cite199†sxusem ("Indian ice cream"). In the late summer its flowers yield cite200†pollen and copious cite201†nectar which give a rich spicy cite202†honey . Its young leaves, roots, and shoots are edible (if somewhat bitter), and rich in cite203†provitamin A and cite204†vitamin C .
L227: The cite205†Dena’ina found them also useful as cite206†food supplement for dogs and applied sap from the stem to wounds, believing it to have cite207†antiinflammatory properties.
L228: Several researchers have studied this taxon. cite208†Heinrich Carl Haussknecht in the late 19th century and Peter H. Raven about a century later researched the cite209†phylogeny , cite210†systematics , and cite89†taxonomy of willowherbs. cite211†Peter Michaelis ' studies of this genus paved the way for understanding of cite212†extranuclear inheritance in plants.
