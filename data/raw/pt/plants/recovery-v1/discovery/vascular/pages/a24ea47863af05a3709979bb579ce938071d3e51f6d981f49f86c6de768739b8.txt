L2@P0: This was the most difficult part of the key to write. The intention is to divide the plants broadly, if
L3@P0: vaguely, into more or less taxonomic groups, with the idea that budding bryologists should get to
L4@P0: know what these broad groups look like, as they all have their own distinctive ‘jiz’. However, this has
L5@P0: meant that some of the couplets here are rather long, complex and inelegant. In particular I was in
L6@P0: two minds about whether, like Watson, to place all mosses with hyaline hairpoints into their own
L7@P0: Section. In the end I decided against it, because hyaline hairpoints have evolved in so many different
L8@P0: unrelated groups, but I understand if others disagree with this decision. ‘Road testing’ the key has
L9@P0: also shown that couplet 11 is rather unsatisfactory. However, once one gets to know what the
L10@P0: genera Orthotrichum, Ulota and Zygodon look like, the difficulties largely disappear. I have included
L11@P0: potentially problematic species, which might key out to either Section 10 or Section 11, in the keys
L12@P0: for both Sections.

L57@P1: 1 The acrocarp-pleurocarp distinction can be difficult for beginners, so I make no apology for here reproducing
L58@P1: Watson’s excellent ‘small print’ (p.40 of the 3rd edition of British Mosses & Liverworts), which is very helpful:
L59@P1: “...it is impossible to give a single clear-cut criterion on which to base a decision in barren material. If,
L60@P1: however, attention be paid to the following three classes of characters a wrong decision should very rarely be
L61@P1: made at this point:
L62@P1: (i) General habit and mode of branching. Acrocarpous mosses are usually unbranched or sparingly
L63@P1: branched, and of erect or ascending habit; they are never regularly pinnately branched and the
L64@P1: leaves are rarely glossy and ‘chaff-like’. The majority form cushions or turfs (like the pile of a
L65@P1: carpet). Almost all pleurocarpous mosses are freely branched, often pinnately so, various in habit
L66@P1: but frequently forming dense intricate mats (or looser wefts) of the prostrate or ascending
L67@P1: secondary stems; and the leaves are most often glossy and ‘chaff-like’ in character.
L68@P1: (ii) Cell structure and nerves. Almost all doubtful examples can be settled immediately if the leaf is
L69@P1: examined under the compound microscope, for the range of cell structure is totally different in
L70@P1: the two groups. Thus very few acrocarpous mosses have long narrow cells throughout the leaf,
L71@P1: whereas this is the cell structure that is most prevalent among the pleurocarps. Again, extremely
L72@P1: few pleurocarpous mosses have short (isodiametric) cells in the upper part of the leaf and
L73@P1: elongated rectangular cells in the leaf base; but in many acrocarpous families this is the usual
L74@P1: type of cell structure. A further useful point: nerveless leaves are very rare among acrocarps;
L75@P1: leaves with excurrent nerves are almost equally rare in the pleurocarpous series.
L76@P1: (iii) Position of archegonia and capsules. In almost all the acrocarpous series the archegonia – and
L77@P1: hence the capsules – arise terminally, i.e. at the tip of a stem or branch, while in the
L78@P1: pleurocarpous mosses they arise, surrounded only by the perichaetial leaves, on the side of a
L79@P1: stem or branch. Acrocarpous mosses that are particularly likely to be mistaken for pleurocarps
L80@P1: are Cinclidotus fontinaloides, Breutelia chrysocoma and Plagiomnium affine.”

L118@P3: The best approach to demystifying Sphagnum is to divide it into smaller bite-sized chunks,

L121@P3: drill down to the species.
