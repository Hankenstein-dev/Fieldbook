L72@P2-4: Image Information .................................................................................................................................80Introduction
L73@P4: Pteridaceae is a large family that is almost cosmopolitan in distribution, but has its greatest diversity in
L74@P4: the tropics and in arid regions. It is represented in New Zealand by five indigenous genera (Adiantum,
L75@P4: Anogramma, Cheilanthes, Pellaea, Pteris) and 16 indigenous species, five of which are endemic, and
L76@P4: another ten that are fully naturalised or casual. In addition there is a wholly naturalised genus,
L77@P4: Myriopteris with a single species.
L78@P4: Members of Pteridaceae in New Zealand are terrestrial species with erect to long-creeping rhizomes
L79@P4: and 1–5-pinnate fronds. The sori are either completely unprotected and extend along the veins, or are
L80@P4: marginal and protected by the modified inrolled lamina margin. All species have trilete spores.
L81@P4: Adiantum includes six indigenous species, two of them endemic, and another two naturalised, all
L82@P4: characterised by their polished dark brown stipes, flabellate or oblong ultimate lamina segments, and
L83@P4: sori protected by reniform or oblong reflexed lamina flaps. Anogramma leptophylla is the sole species
L84@P4: of the genus in New Zealand, distinguished by its small delicate laminae, and unprotected sori
L85@P4: extending along the veins. Cheilanthes is represented by two indigenous and one casual species,
L86@P4: having small to medium-sized, 2–3-pinnate fronds, and elongated marginal sori protected by inrolled
L87@P4: pinna margins. Pellaea has two non-endemic indigenous species, with long-creeping rhizomes,
L88@P4: pinnate fronds, scaly stipes and rachises, and scarcely protected sori almost continuous around the
L89@P4: pinna margins. Pteris comprises five indigenous species, three of them endemic, and seven
L90@P4: naturalised or casual species, with medium to large, 1–4-pinnate fronds, and sori continuous along the
L91@P4: pinna edges, protected by membranous inrolled pinna margins.
L92@P4: Most species of Pteridaceae in New Zealand occur more frequently in northern or eastern parts of the
L93@P4: country. Adiantum cunninghamii and Pellaea rotundifolia are unusual in being widespread species.
L94@P4: Adiantum aethiopicum, A. diaphanum, A. fulvum, A. hispidulum, P. macilenta, P. saxatilis and P.
L95@P4: tremula have a mainly northern distribution extending only to northern parts of the South Island, and P.
L96@P4: carsei is confined to the North Island. Anogramma leptophylla, Cheilanthes distans, C. sieberi and
L97@P4: Pellaea calidirupium all occur most commonly in eastern parts of the country, especially in the South
L98@P4: Island. Two species have very restricted distributions – Adiantum formosum currently known only from
L99@P4: the vicinity of the Manawatū Gorge, and Pteris epaleata from Fiordland.
L100@P4: The naturalised genus Myriopteris is represented by a single species, M. lendigera, which is
L101@P4: distinguished by its 3-pinnate fronds, long pale orange hairs on the abaxial surfaces, and sori
L102@P4: produced on small rounded tertiary pinnae.
L103@P4-5: 1Pteridaceae E.D.M.Kirchn., Schul-Bot. 109 (1831)
L104@P5: Type taxon: Pteris L.
L105@P5: Terrestrial, rupestral or rarely (not NZ) aquatic ferns. Rhizome erect to long-creeping, bearing scales or
L106@P5: hairs; roots rarely bearing small tubers. Fronds usually monomorphic or rarely dimorphic, not
L107@P5: articulated to rhizome. Laminae 1–5-pinnate (NZ) or entire to 6-pinnate (not NZ), sometimes pedate or
L108@P5: helicoid (NZ) or palmate (not NZ); herbaceous or coriaceous, glabrous or scaly and/or hairy, rarely
L109@P5: farinose abaxially (not NZ). Pinnae or pinna segments sometimes articulated to rachis or costae. Veins
L110@P5: free or reticulate, the areolae without included veinlets. Sori extending along the veins, or extending
L111@P5: along the margins, or borne on the underside of modified, reflexed, marginal lamina flaps; paraphyses
L112@P5: present or absent; sori exindusiate or protected by the unmodified inrolled lamina margin, or by a
L113@P5: membranous, reflexed lamina margin; sporangial maturation mixed. Sporangia with vertical or rarely
L114@P5: oblique annulus, 16–64 spores per sporangium. Homosporous; spores trilete, lacking chlorophyll;
L115@P5: perispores either with coarse ridges and a prominent equatorial flange, or prominently cristate or
L116@P5: echinate without an equatorial flange, or plain to tuberculate without an equatorial flange.
L117@P5: Taxonomy: A family of five subfamilies, 53 genera and over 1200 species (PPG 1 2016).
L118@P5: Five subfamilies were recognised in this large family by PPG 1 (2016): Parkerioideae,
L119@P5: Cryptogammoideae, Pteridoideae, Vittarioideae and Cheilanthoideae. All are monophyletic
L120@P5: (Schuettpelz et al. 2007; Zhang et al. 2015), but only the latter three occur in New Zealand, with
L121@P5: Anogramma and Pteris in Pteridoideae, Adiantum in Vittarioideae, and Cheilanthes, Myriopteris and
L122@P5: Pellaea in Cheilanthoideae.
L123@P5: Allan (1961) included Adiantum, Anogramma, Cheilanthes and Pellaea in Adiantaceae, and placed
L124@P5: only Pteris in Pteridaceae, along with Histiopteris, Paesia and Pteridium, which are now included in
L125@P5: Dennstaedtiaceae (PPG 1 2016). Myriopteris has only recently been recognised as a separate genus
L126@P5: (Grusz & Windham 2013; PPG 1 2016), and the sole New Zealand representative was previously
L127@P5: included in Cheilanthes.

L157@P6: diversity in the tropics and in arid regions (Tryon 1990). Six non-endemic genera with 27 species in
L158@P6: New Zealand; five species endemic.

L167@P6: Recognition: Pteridaceae is a large and diverse family comprising terrestrial or rupestral species with
L168@P6: erect to long-creeping rhizomes. Fronds are monomorphic or dimorphic, entire to 5-pinnate, and scaly
L169@P6: or sometimes hairy. The sori are either exindusiate and extend along the veins, or are marginal and
L170@P6: protected by the modified lamina margin, producing trilete spores.
L171@P6: Adiantum L., Sp. Pl. 1094 (1753)
L172@P6: Type taxon: Adiantum capillus-veneris L.
L173@P6: Etymology: From the Greek adiantos (unwettable), a reference to the water-shedding properties of
L174@P6: the lamina.
L175@P6: Vernacular names: huruhuru tapairu; maidenhair fern; tawatawa
L176@P6: Terrestrial or rupestral ferns. Rhizomes erect to long-creeping, scaly; roots rarely bearing small tubers.
L177@P6: Rhizome scales non-clathrate, acicular to narrowly ovate or narrowly triangular, attached at base,
L178@P6: concolorous, yellow-brown to red-brown or dark brown. Fronds monomorphic. Stipes and rachises
L179@P6: red- or dark brown, highly polished. Laminae 1–5-pinnate or rarely helicoid, herbaceous or coriaceous,
L180@P6: glabrous or hairy or rarely scaly (not NZ), rarely glaucous or farinose (not NZ) abaxially. Pinnae not
L181@P6: articulated to rachis; ultimate segments rarely articulated to costae and deciduous (not NZ). Veins free
L182@P6: or rarely reticulate (not NZ). Sori borne mainly on acroscopic margins of lamina segments, on the
L183@P6: underside of orbicular to elongate, modified, reflexed, marginal, lamina flaps (“indusia”); paraphyses
L184@P6: absent or rarely present (not NZ). Spores trilete, lacking chlorophyll; perispores scabrate, rugose or
L185@P6: tuberculate, without an equatorial flange.
L186@P6: Taxonomy: A genus of c. 225 species, included in the subfamily Vittarioideae (PPG 1 2016).
L187@P6: Adiantum is now considered to be monophyletic and sister to the vittarioid ferns (Pryer et al. 2016),
L188@P6: although this relationship was regarded as uncertain (e.g. Bouma et al. 2010) until adequate taxon
L189@P6: sampling had been achieved. Even now, there is insufficient sampling across the whole genus to
L190@P6: determine how many clades are present. Bouma et al. (2010) were able to identify three major clades,
L191@P6: with all of the indigenous New Zealand species present in clade C along with other species from
L192@P6: different southern hemisphere continents. However, their sampling outside New Zealand was very
L193@P6: limited. Lu et al. (2011) sampled 86 taxa from a wider geographical range using five plastid markers
L194@P6: and found nine major clades, six of them within Chinese Adiantum. Nevertheless, the phylogeny of the
L195@P6: genus as a whole still requires further investigation. Tryon & Tryon (1982) recognised eight major
L196@P6: groups on morphological grounds, but there has been no modern monograph of the genus.
L197@P6: Within New Zealand, Allan (1961) recognised six indigenous species – A. aethiopicum, A.
L198@P6: cunninghamii, A. diaphanum, A. formosum, A. fulvum and A. hispidulum. Parris & Croxall (1974)
L199@P6: subsequently reinstated A. viridescens, Parris (1980) suggested that A. pubescens should be
L200@P6: recognised in addition to A. hispidulum, and Brownsey (in Webb et al. 1988) added two naturalised
L201@P6: species to the New Zealand flora. However, Large & Braggins (1993) found that A. hispidulum and
L202@P6: A. pubescens could only be distinguished on the basis of their hair characters, and even then with a
L203@P6: high degree of variability. They treated A. pubescens as a variety of A. hispidulum. Brownsey et al.
L204@P6: (2019) investigated the presence or absence of hairs on the abaxial lamina surfaces of all indigenous
L205@P6: New Zealand Adiantum species, and concluded that most are dimorphic in this regard, and that such
L206@P6: variation should be treated as variation within the bounds of individual species distinguished on the
L207@P6: basis of other morphological characters, a conclusion also reached for Australian and Malesian plants
L208@P6: by Bostock (1992). As a consequence, Brownsey et al. (2019) reduced A. viridescens to synonymy
L209@P6: with A. fulvum.

L239@P7: Distribution: Adiantum is pantropical in distribution, with a few species also extending to both the
L240@P7: north and south temperate regions. Many species have become naturalised in different parts of the
L241@P7: world. The greatest diversity is in the Neotropics, where more than half the species are found (Mickel
L242@P7: & Smith 2004); six indigenous and one naturalised species in southern Africa (Crouch et al. 2011),
L243@P7: eight in Australia (Bostock 1998), ten indigenous and two naturalised in the south-west Pacific
L244@P7: (Nakamura 2008), and one indigenous and four naturalised in Hawai‘i (Palmer 2003). Eight species in
L245@P7: New Zealand; two endemic, four indigenous, and two naturalised.
