L78@P2: Abstract
L79@P2: The census of the loci classici of 1,400 Italian endemic vascular plants (i.e. not thriving elsewhere with the exception of Corsica
L80@P2: and Malta) is here presented and described. The effective place of publication of accepted names, basionyms and homotypic
L81@P2: synonyms were identified and critically verified. This often resulted in some change in authorship attribution and, in seven
L82@P2: cases, in validation problems (Asperula cynanchica var. lactea var. nov., A. lactea comb. nov., Biscutella laevigata subsp.
L83@P2: raffaelliana subsp. nov., Ferulago nodosa subsp. geniculata comb. & stat. nov., Limonium tineoi comb. nov., L. usticanum sp.
L84@P2: nov., Noccaea torreana comb. nov.). The geographic information on the loci classici was excerpted from the protologues, as
L85@P2: well as information on typification for the taxa described before 1 January 1958. The names without holotype are 796. For 347
L86@P2: names a lecto- or neo-typification is available in literature, while 449 currently accepted taxa still need of type designation.
L87@P2: Key words: Alps, Apennines, Mediterranean, nomenclature, phytogeography, systematics, taxonomy
L88@P2: Introduction
L89@P2: The idea of an inventory of the loci classici of the plant taxa described in Italy was launched by Livio Poldini in the
L90@P2: 1970s, within the activities of the Floristic Group of the Italian Botanical Society. Although the inventory started in
L91@P2: 1980s, it lasted only for some time, under the coordination of Pier Virgilio Arrigoni, and as a result only a few
L92@P2: papers were produced (Mariotti 1987–1988, Dal Vesco et al. 1987–1988). In 2010, notably the International Year
L93@P2: of Biodiversity, the Floristic Group of the Italian Botanical Society decided to refuel the project.
L94@P2: Currently, several ongoing worldwide projects aim at tracing the original materials used to describe vascular
L95@P2: plant taxa and to identify, among them, the onomatophore types (Jarvis 2007, the most relevant).
L96@P2: Italy has a well-known deep floristic knowledge, and a plethora of national and local floras have been
L97@P2: published (i.e. Pignatti 1982a and literature cited therein). It hosts 76 herbaria, two thirds of which belong to
L98@P2: academic institutions (Thiers 2014). Nevertheless, the nomenclatural types of most of the Italian taxa are still
L99@P2: unknown, even for the endemic ones. This lack of knowledge places all activities involving the use of Italian
L100@P2: plants—and consequently their names—on shaky foundations. Nomenclature and taxonomy are often neglected or
L101@P2: avoided, due to the wrong belief that these branches of biological sciences are obsolete. On the contrary, scientific
L102@P2: research becomes pointless, missing a correct taxonomic identification and use of names, and a mistaken
L103@P2: application in everyday life can cause serious consequences both for economy and the human health (Kholia &
L104@P2: Fraser-Jenkins 2011). This is also a fundamental pre-requisite for nature conservation and legislation.
L105@P2: The Italian flora consists of 7,953 specific and subspecific taxa (Conti et al. 2005, updated by Peruzzi 2010),
L106@P2: including units with European, Mediterranean or wider range (or even allochtonous taxa). Therefore, the project
L107@P2: working group agreed to start working with the Italian endemics. Endemics are the most sensitive component of
L108@P2: any country’s flora. Moreover, about 5% of the Italian endemics are of great potential economical interest being
L109@P2: wild crop relatives (Domina et al. 2012).
L110@P2: As a first step, a checklist of Italian endemics (including those taxa whose range overlays Corsica and/or
L111@P2: Malta) was produced (Peruzzi et al. 2014, continuously updated at https://docs.google.com/spreadsheet/
L112@P2: ccc?key=0AiJpWnsqul0MdG1hSkk1azBjN3BoLXB4VTAzc3l1Smc#gid=0). This resulted in a list of 1,400
L113@P2: specific and subspecific taxa (as of 31 December 2014).
L114@P2: The census aims to improve the floristic knowledge of the Italian territory and the biogeographical knowledge.
L115@P2: It is harmonised with the project “Flora Critica d’Italia”, i.e. the set of actions dealing with the redaction of a
L116@P2: modern Flora of Italy. This project is promoted by the Italian Botanical Society and supported by the Foundation
L117@P2: “Fondazione per la Flora Critica d’Italia” (Pignotti 2006, Foggi et al. 2007a–b, 2010, Blasi 2014, Cecchi & Selvi
L118@P2: 2014, Troìa & Greuter 2014). This research can be considered as a first step for further investigations that will
L119@P2: include typification of the names still lacking a type designation, study of original materials or topotypical
L120@P2: populations for morphometric analyses, karyological investigations, DNA barcoding, studies on genetic variability
L121@P2-3: and so on (Domina et al. 2012, 2014, Passalacqua et al. 2014).4 • Phytotaxa 196 (1) © 2015 Magnolia Press PERUZZI ET AL.
L122@P3: Taxonomic investigation is necessary to fill relevant gaps in the knowledge of the target taxa. For instance,
L123@P3: based upon data stored in Chrobase.it (Bedini et al. 2010 onwards), chromosome data are lacking for about half
L124@P3: (640) of the Italian endemics (Bedini & al. 2012a–b).
L125@P3: Materials and methods
L126@P3: The reference point for the inventory was the list of the 1,400 taxa of Italian endemics (Peruzzi et al. 2014,
L127@P3: continuously updated). As already explained by Peruzzi et al. (2014), for the purpose of the present study Italian
L128@P3: endemics are defined as the specific and subspecific taxa occurring only in Italy or only in Italy and in Corsica
L129@P3: (France) or only in Italy and in Malta. Moreover, given the disputed taxonomic status of the subspecies within the
L130@P3: genus Hieracium Linnaeus (1753: 799) (Asteraceae; around 220 subspecies putatively endemic to Italy) these
L131@P3: subspecies were deliberately excluded from the analysis. Endemic hybrid taxa and putatively endemic taxa of
L132@P3: doubtful (alien) origin such as those in the genus Amaranthus Linnaeus (1753: 989) and Oenothera Linnaeus
L133@P3: (1753: 346) were also excluded. Similarly, taxa recorded for Italy at variety or form rank, or taxonomically
L134@P3: doubtful and disputed units, were excluded as well.
L135@P3: We searched the scientific literature for the effective place of publication (McNeill et al. 2012, hereafter ICN;
L136@P3: Hodkinson & Lendemer 2014 concerning the clarifications of effective electronic publication) of accepted names,
L137@P3: basionyms and homotypic synonyms. The names found were uploaded into a relational database, which will be
L138@P3: soon open to public online consultation. Accepted names were defined according to Peruzzi et al. (2014, online
L139@P3: list) and in the framework of the new checklist of the Italian vascular flora (F. Conti and collaborators, in
L140@P3: preparation). The bibliographic data have been searched in the available digital sources and in the libraries of the
L141@P3: Italian institutions. The rarest publications, such as those of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, pamphlets or
L142@P3: seed indexes of botanical gardens, usually having a local or limited distribution, were searched in the most
L143@P3: important European botanical libraries.
L144@P3: Once found the references, a critical analysis has been carried out by extrapoLating information on the areas or
L145@P3: localities where endemic taxa of Italy have been described. The level of geographical detail of this information was
L146@P3: extremely variable, ranging from wide areas (but also, with wrong locations and missing data), as typically
L147@P3: reported in the eighteenth–century writings, to very precisely described locations as in the most recent papers. Such
L148@P3: kind of information often needed additional interpretation, supported by historical knowledge, being many old
L149@P3: geographical names (toponyms) definitely lost. These data were loaded using a Geographical Information System
L150@P3: (GIS). In the census, the text of the protologue indicating the locus classicus is reported in its original form,
L151@P3: accompanied by a code that refers to the Italian region as follows: Valle d’Aosta, VDA; Piemonte, PIE; Lombardia,
L152@P3: LOM; Trentino-Alto Adige, TAA; Veneto, VEN; Friuli-Venezia Giulia, FVG; Liguria, LIG; Emilia-Romagna,
L153@P3: EMR; Tuscany, TOS; Marche, MAR; Umbria, UMB; Lazio, LAZ; Abruzzo, ABR; Molise, MOL; Puglia, PUG;
L154@P3: Campania, CAM; Basilicata, BAS; Calabria, CAL; Sicily, SIC; Sardinia, SAR; Corsica, COR; Malta, MAL;
L155@P3: putatively outside Italy, Corsica and Malta, EST.
L156@P3: A second step of this work involved literature analysis concerning the possible typification of the names. In
L157@P3: some cases, during the project (2010–2014), types for some names were designated in specific publications and
L158@P3: cited in the correct position after each name. Isotypes and paratypes were not considered in this work,
L159@P3: concentrating our efforts on holotypes, lectotypes, neotypes and epitypes. Holotype designations for pre-1958
L160@P3: names were mostly re-defined to lectotypes, because it is hardly ever possible to prove that the author used only
L161@P3: one element unless this is explicitly stated in the protologue. In doing this, we carefully followed the suggestions
L162@P3: for best practice recently highlighted by McNeill (2014).
L163@P3: The list of names is arranged according to the alphabetic order of currently accepted names (often
L164@P3: corresponding to the basionym itself). The currently accepted names are highlighted in bold italics.
