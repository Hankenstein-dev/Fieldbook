L136: Lantana montevidensis
L137: (cite66†Spreng. ) cite67†Briq. L138: cite68†Synonyms L139: ---
L140: Lantana sellowiana
L141: cite69†Lippia montevidensis
L142: Lantana montevidensis is a species of cite64†lantana cite70†native to cite71†South America that often forms dense cite72†thickets and mats over the ground. It is known by many common names, such as: trailing lantana, weeping lantana, creeping lantana, small lantana, purple lantana or trailing shrubverbena.
L143: The name Lantana derives from the cite73†Latin name of the wayfaring tree cite74†Viburnum lantana , the flowers of which closely resemble Lantana.^{cite75†[1] } The name montevidensis derives from the city cite76†Montevideo , Uruguay, where the species originated.^{cite77†[2] }
L144: ## Description
L145: 
L146: [cite78†edit ]
L147: 
L148: Trailing down a wall on cite79†garden bed L149: Lantana montevidensis is a small strongly scented flowering, trailing or rambling, cite80†shrub or cite81†subshrub with oval-shaped green leaves that forms a mat. With support it has a climbing 'cite82†vine ' form, when on edge a trailing form, and on the flat a groundcover form.
L150: The plant has thin, creeping or drooping, hairy stems or branches up to 1 m long, sometimes to 4 m in propitious conditions, 1–2 mm thick, and are usually quadrangular in cross-section when young and, unlike cite83†Lantana camara , free of cite84†prickles . They may produce roots at their nodes as they come into contact with the soil.^{cite85†[3] }
L151: The bright green leaves are cite86†ovate to oblong-ovate (egg-shaped) and sometimes narrowly ovate, about 2.5 cm long, and roughly serrated, with a short cite87†petiole . Oppositely arranged and borne on short stalks, the leaves are in opposite pairs on the stem, with successive pairs borne at right angles to each other.^{cite88†[4] } The leaves' upper surfaces are coarse (i.e. scabrous), whereas their undersides are tenderly hairy (i.e. pubescent).^{cite85†[3] }
L152: ### Inflorescence
L153: 
L154: [cite89†edit ]
L155: 
L156: White-flowered variety
L157: The cite90†inflorescence is a circular head of several (usually 20) purple to lavender and white funnel-shaped flowers with lobed corollas each nearly a centimeter wide. The pinkish-lilac flowers grow in 3 cm wide clusters on long flower stalks.
L158: They are 8–12 mm long and 4–8 mm wide.^{cite88†[4] } It flowers cite91†most of the year in frost-free areas, though blooming peaks in spring and summer.^{cite92†[5] } The cite93†peduncle is 2–8 cm long; cite94†bracts are broad to ovate, 4–7 mm long and cite95†corolla is 8–12 mm long.^{cite96†[6] }
L159: Yellow-flowered montevidensis are a case of misidentification and most often relate to the "New Gold" lantana Lantana × hybrida, a hybrid between camara and montevidensis. Occasionally these yellow-flowered plants are misidentified Lantana depressa var. depressa, a Florida endemic taxon more closely related to cite83†Lantana camara with smaller, less robust flowers.^{cite97†[7] }
L160: The fleshy fruit is green when young and reddish-purple when cite98†ripe . Featuring a black seed, the fruit is spherical in shape, with poisonous drupes 5-7 mm in size. It consists of a pair of nutlets surrounded by flesh somewhat like a cite99†berry .^{cite88†[4] }
L161: ## Cultivation
L162: 
L163: [cite100†edit ]
L164: Lantana montevidensis is also cultivated as an cite101†ornamental plant for its plentiful colorful lavender to purple flowers and as a cite102†drought tolerant cite103†groundcover , woody vine, and trailing plant for containers and in the ground. In subtropical climates there are flowers most of the year, with variegated leaved cultivars also available. It is a hardy, long-lived shrub that can withstand periods of extended drought and an ability to grow and reproduce under both shade and full sun.
L165: This plant is toxic to livestock.^{cite92†[5] }
L166: ## Distribution
L167: 
L168: [cite104†edit ]
L169: Native to cite105†Uruguay , northeastern and northwestern cite106†Argentina , cite107†Bolivia , cite108†Paraguay and cite109†southern Brazil ,^{cite110†[8] } the plant is present nearly worldwide as an cite111†introduced species of garden and landscape cite57†plant , where it is naturalized in cite112†New Zealand , cite113†Hawaii , cite114†New Caledonia and cite115†southern United States (i.e. California, Texas, Louisiana, Alabama, Georgia and Florida).
L170: In some areas, such as parts of cite116†Australia and cite113†Hawaii , it is a cite117†noxious weed and cite118†invasive species . It is found typically in tropical and subtropical environments, and sporadically in temperate and semi-arid areas.^{cite85†[3] }
L171: In Australia, the plant was first introduced as an ornamental in 1851, and began cite119†escaping the garden in 1871. Today, it is found in well drained cite120†wildernesss in cite121†alluvial soil, cite122†eucalyptus forest in cite123†loamy soil and on sandstone rocks, disturbed areas near cite124†mangroves , and beside roadsides and river banks. Its seeds are spread extensively by fruit-eating animals and birds, water running over the soil surface during heavy rain and in mud sticking to hooves and footwear.
L172: It can also spread vegetatively by cite125†layering .^{cite88†[4] }^{cite126†[9] }
