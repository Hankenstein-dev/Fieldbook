L187: Cistus (from the cite105†Greek kistos) is a genus of cite97†flowering plants in the rockrose cite106†family cite101†Cistaceae , containing about 34 species and a similar number of natural hybrids.^{cite103†[1] } They are cite107†perennial cite108†shrubs found on dry or rocky soils throughout the cite109†Mediterranean region , from Morocco, Spain, Italy, Greece, through to the cite110†Middle East , and also on the cite111†Canary Islands .
L188: 
L189: Cistus, with its many hybrids and cultivars, is commonly encountered as a garden flower.
L190: The common name rock-rose or rockrose is applied to the species, a name also shared by the related genera cite112†Helianthemum and cite113†Tuberaria , all in the same family Cistaceae. The common name gum cistus is also applied to resin-bearing species, especially cite114†C. ladanifer .
L191: ## Description
L192: 
L193: [cite115†edit ]
L194: 
L195: Evergreen leaves of a Cistus species (cite93†Cistus monspeliensis )
L196: 
L197: The cite116†leaves are cite117†evergreen , opposite, simple, usually slightly rough-surfaced, 2–8 cm long. In a few species (notably C. ladanifer), the leaves are coated with a highly aromatic cite118†resin called cite119†labdanum .
L198: 
L199: They have showy 5-petaled cite120†flowers ranging from white to purple and dark pink, in a few species with a conspicuous dark red spot at the base of each petal.

L204: ### Phylogeny
L205: 
L206: [cite122†edit ]
L207: Cistus and Halimium form a cohesive and the most derived clade within cite101†Cistaceae .^{cite123†[2] } cite124†Plants of the World Online and cite125†World Flora Online treat Halimium as a cite104†synonym of Cistus.^{cite126†[3] }^{cite127†[4] } cite128†Molecular phylogenetic analyses conducted between 2005 and 2011 confirm that Cistus species divide into two well-defined cite129†clades , neither of which was fully resolved internally.
L208: The first clade consists of those with purple and pink flowers (the "purple pink clade" or PPC). The second clade consists of those with white flowers or, in the case of Cistus parviflorus, pale pink flowers (the "white or whitish pink clade" or WWPC).^{cite123†[2] }^{cite130†[5] }^{cite131†[6] }^{cite132†[7] } Although the flower colour of C. parviflorus is anomalous, it has very short cite133†styles , otherwise characteristic of WWPC species.

L246: Within the purple pink clade (PPC), C. crispus is consistently the first diverging species. C. albidus, C. creticus and C. heterophyllus form a well supported clade. Seven species endemic to the Canary Islands form a cite158†polytomy , resolved differently in different analyses, in which subtaxa of some species do not always cluster together.
L247: Within the white and whitish pink clade (WWPC), there is weak support for a clade consisting of C. clusii and C. munbyi; the other species either formed part of a polytomy or resolved differently in different analyses. Halimium and Cistus were regularly shown to be cite159†paraphyletic with respect to one another.^{cite132†[7] }

L349: ## Ecology
L350: 
L351: [cite225†edit ]
L352: They are thermophilous plants, which require open, sunny places. This plant genus is peculiar in that it has developed a range of specific adaptations to resist summer drought and frequent disturbance events, such as fire and grazing. In addition, it can form both cite226†ectomycorrhizas and cite227†arbuscular mycorrhizas .
L353: More than 200 ectomycorrhiza-forming fungal species belonging to 40 genera have been reported so far to be associated with Cistus.^{cite228†[19] } As with many other Cistaceae, the species of Cistus have the ability to form cite229†mycorrhizal associations with cite230†truffles (Tuber) and are thus able to thrive on poor sandy soils or rocks. cite114†Cistus ladanifer has been found to have mycorrhizal associations with cite231†Boletus edulis , cite232†Boletus rhodoxanthus , and cite233†Laccaria laccata .^{cite234†[20] }
L354: Cistus are the only host of cite235†Cytinus hypocistis , a small cite236†parasitic plant that lives on the roots and is noticeable only for a short period of time when in flower. The presence of the parasite does not seem to harm the host population.
L355: 
L356: Cistus species are used as food plants by the cite237†larvae of some cite238†Lepidoptera species including cite239†Coleophora confluella and cite239†Coleophora helianthemella , the latter recorded on Cistus monspeliensis.
L357: Various Cistus species are known to emit volatile oils, rendering the plants flammable. Their emission of these essential oils is a genetic adaptation for the species intended to actually promote flammability and even cause spontaneous combustion.^{cite240†[21] } Cistus plants are therefore considered cite241†pyrophytes , or plants which rely upon fire as a part of their life cycle to encourage spread within their biome.
L358: Cistus seeds are resistant to the fires allowing them to germinate once fires have subsided and allowing them to benefit from an environment with less competition from other species.^{cite242†[22] }
L359: ## Cultivation
L360: 
L361: [cite243†edit ]
L362: 
L363: Cistuses are suitable for sunny gardens with a nearly frost-free cite244†Mediterranean climate . The hardiest of the species is C. laurifolius, which survived the hard frost at cite245†Royal Botanical Gardens at Kew in 1895 that eliminated all the cistuses save this and two white-flowered natural hybrids, C. × corbariensis, already grown by cite246†John Tradescant the Elder , and C. × loretii, a 19th-century introduction.^{cite247†[23] }
