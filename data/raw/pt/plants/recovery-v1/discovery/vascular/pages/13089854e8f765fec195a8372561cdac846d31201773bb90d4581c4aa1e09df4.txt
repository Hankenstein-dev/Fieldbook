L204: Angelica is a genus of about 90 species of tall cite115†biennial and cite116†perennial cite117†herbs in the family cite109†Apiaceae , native to temperate and subarctic regions of the cite118†Northern Hemisphere , reaching as far north as Iceland, cite119†Sápmi , and cite120†Greenland .^{cite121†[2] } They grow to 1–3 m (3 ft 3 in – 9 ft 10 in) tall, with large bipinnate cite122†leaves and large compound cite123†umbels of white or greenish-white cite124†flowers .
L205: It shows variations in fruit anatomy, leaf morphology, and subterranean structures. The genes are extremely polymorphic.
L206: Some species can be found in cite125†purple moor and rush pastures .

L209: ## Characteristics
L210: 
L211: [cite126†edit ]
L212: Angelica species grow to 1–3 m (3 ft 3 in – 9 ft 10 in) tall, with large bipinnate cite122†leaves and large compound umbels of white or greenish-white cite124†flowers .
L213: Their large, sparkling, starburst flowers^{cite127†[3] } are pollinated by a great variety of insects (the generalist cite128†pollination syndrome),^{cite129†[4] } the floral scents are species-specific, and even specific to particular subspecies.^{cite130†[5] } The active ingredients of angelica are found in the roots and rhizomes^{cite131†[6] } and contains cite132†furocoumarins in its tissues, which make the skin sensitive to light.^{cite133†[7] }
L214: ## Cladistic classification
L215: 
L216: [cite134†edit ]
L217: 
L218: cite135†Molecular phylogenetic analyses have identified four major cite136†clades :^{cite137†[8] }
L219: Archangelica clade
L220:     which includes the type species cite138†Angelica archangelica and North American species such as A. atropurpurea.
L221: Eurasian Angelica clade
L222:     containing primarily cite139†Eurasian species and the cite140†Pacific Coast species A. genuflexa.
L223: North American Angelica clade
L224:     comprising approximately twenty species native to cite141†North America , including A. arguta and A. californica. North American species traditionally assigned to Angelica do not form a single evolutionary lineage, are cite142†polyphyletic and require cite143†taxonomic revision.
L225: PENA clade
L226:     acronym for the genera Perideridia, Eulophus, Neoparrya, and Angelica, and represented by A. dawsonii, which was found to be more closely related to other cite144†genera within the cite109†Apiaceae .

Crystallized strips of young angelica stems and midribs are green in colour and are sold as decorative and flavoursome cake decoration material, but may also be enjoyed on their own.^{cite260†[13] } The roots and seeds are commonly used to flavour cite261†gin .
L342: Its presence accounts for the distinct flavour of many liqueurs, such as cite262†Chartreuse . Angelica roots are used in perfumery as a plant analogue of musk.^{cite263†[14] }
L343: cite201†Seacoast angelica (A. lucida) has been eaten as a wild version of cite264†celery .
L344: 
L345: A. sylvestris and some other species are eaten by the cite265†larvae of some cite266†Lepidoptera species, including cite267†bordered pug , cite268†grey pug , cite269†lime-speck pug and the cite270†V-pug .
