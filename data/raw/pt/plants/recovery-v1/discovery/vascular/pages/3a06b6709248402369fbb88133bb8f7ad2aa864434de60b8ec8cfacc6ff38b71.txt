L26@P0: Abstract. A checklist with preliminary conservation assessments of native South American species of
L27@P0: Acalypha is presented. This work is supported by the study of ca 6500 herbarium specimens and an
L28@P0: in-depth literature review. As a result, 87 species (83 native and four introduced) and eight subspecies
L29@P0: are accepted, and a further 395 names are considered synonyms. Geographical distribution, habitat, and
L30@P0: altitudinal range for all species are also indicated. Brazil is the richest country in number of species
L31@P0: of Acalypha (40), followed by Peru (32), Bolivia (29), Colombia and Ecuador—including Galapagos
L32@P0: Islands—(24), Venezuela (18), Argentina (17), Paraguay (13), Guyana (8), Uruguay (5), French Guiana
L33@P0: (4), and Suriname (3). The presence of the genus Acalypha in Chile is reported for the fi rst time, alongside
L34@P0: new country records of A. poiretii in Peru and A. venezuelica in Guatemala. The specimens previously
L35@P0: identifi ed as A. plicata from Colombia and Venezuela, are here considered belonging to A. cuspidata.
L36@P0: The red list provided follows IUCN criteria and includes 39 species and three subspecies, 47% of total
L37@P0: native species of Acalypha in South America: 16 species and one subspecies Critically Endangered (nine
L38@P0: of them probably extinct), 15 species and two subspecies Endangered, and eight species Vulnerable.
L39@P0: Keywords. Biodiversity, checklist, conservation assessment, IUCN, threatened species.

L44@P1: Introduction
L45@P1: Acalypha L. is, with ca 500 accepted species, the third largest genus in the family Euphorbiaceae Juss.
L46@P1: after Euphorbia L. (ca 2000 species.) and Croton L. (ca 1200 species) (Riina et al. 2013; Berry et al.
L47@P1: 2005). Acalypha comprises mainly small trees, shrubs and subshrubs, and also some herbs. Species of
L48@P1: Acalypha grow in a wide variety of habitats, predominantly in lowland tropical and subtropical rainforests
L49@P1: and montane forest, but appear also in savannahs, seasonal forest, dry forest, and shrublands, ranging
L50@P1: from sea level to ca 4000 metres (Cardiel & Muñoz-Rodríguez 2012).
L51@P1: Species of Acalypha are easily recognised by their simple, alternate, stipulate leaves; small, unisexual,
L52@P1: monochlamydeous fl owers with sepals, usually grouped in spicate infl orescences (sometimes racemose
L53@P1: or panicle-like); male fl owers 4-merous with pendulous anthers, vermiform at anthesis; female fl owers
L54@P1: usually sessile with calyx 3(‒5) partite, showy laciniate styles, and subtending bracts usually becoming
L55@P1: foliaceous in fruit. The pendant anthers that become twisted after dehiscence; the small, fi nely sculptured
L56@P1: pollen grains with brevicolporate apertures, and the presence and variability of epidermal crystals, appear
L57@P1: to be synapomorphies of the genus within Euphorbiaceae (Nowicke & Takahashi 2002; Sagun et al. 2006;
L58@P1: Cardiel et al. 2020; Levin et al. 2022).
L59@P1: Acalypha has traditionally been classifi ed in the Euphorbiaceae subfamily Acalyphoideae Beilschm.,
L60@P1: which includes 99 genera and 1865 species (Hayden & Hayden 2000; Wurdack et al. 2005). Studies using
L61@P1: DNA barcodes strongly support the monophyly of Acalyphoideae (e.g., Wurdack et al. 2005), but the
L62@P1: relationship between groups within the subfamily is not well understood and further studies are needed.
L63@P1: Nevertheless, all studies to date retrieve a strongly supported monophyletic Acalypha (Tokuoka 2007;
L64@P1: Wurdack & Davis 2009; Levin et al. 2022). The most recent proposal for an infrageneric classifi cation of
L65@P1: Acalypha (Levin et al. 2022) includes four subgenera: three subgenera already recognised in the previous
L66@P1: classifi cation by Pax & Hoff mann (1924)—Acalypha, Androcephala Pax & K.Hoff m., and Linostachys
L67@P1: (Klotzsch ex Schltdl.) Pax & K.Hoff m.—and a fourth one, Hypandrae (Müll.Arg.) Hurus, proposed by
L68@P1: Levin and colleagues based on molecular data. Only the subgenera Acalypha and Linostachys are present
L69@P1: in the American continent, whereas Androcephala and Hypandrae are restricted to Africa.
L70@P1: Acalypha has a predominantly pantropical distribution, with about 16 herbaceous species reaching cooler
L71@P1: temperate regions in the northern hemisphere. The greatest morphological diversity is found in Africa,
L72@P1: where the genus most likely originated (Levin et al. 2005)—only on this continent are the four currently
L73@P1: recognized subgenera present—whereas the largest number of species is found in the Americas with more
L74@P1: than half of the species in the genus. The Americas are home to ca 254 species of Acalypha (Ulloa-Ulloa
L75@P1: et al. 2017), compared to only 65 species in mainland Africa (Cardiel & Montero-Muñoz 2018), 49 in
L76@P1: the West Indian Ocean Region—including Madagascar—(Montero-Muñoz 2021; Montero-Muñoz et al.
L77@P1: 2022), 28 in the Malesian Region (Sagun et al. 2010), 18 in China (Huaxing & Gilbert 2008), eight in
L78@P1: Australia (Foster 1994), and 16 in the Pacifi c Ocean islands (Cardiel et al. 2022a).
L79@P1: The last global taxonomic study of Acalypha dates back a century (Pax & Hoff mann 1924) and included
L80@P1: 390 species. Since then, 160 species and 36 subspecies or varieties new to science have been described
L81@P1: (Cardiel et al. 2022a), and Pax and Hoff mann’s work is now outdated. More recent studies have focussed
L82@P1: on Acalypha at a regional or national scale (see below), and many species and geographical regions still
L83@P1: need a comprehensive taxonomic study.
L84@P1: Although some species are well known, many species remain poorly known, especially in continental
L85@P1: Africa, Western and Southern Asia, and in the Pacifi c islands. In the American continent, comprehensive
L86@P1: revisions are still pending for Mexico and the Caribbean Region, where recent global checklists of
L87@P1: vascular plants record ca 110 and 40 species of Acalypha respectively (Acevedo-Rodríguez & Strong
L88@P1-2: 2012; Villaseñor 2016). In contrast, the genus is better known in the United States, several Central CARDIEL J.M. et al., Catalogue and red list of Acalypha

L90@P2: American countries, and South America. In the United States, 18 mainly herbaceous species have been
L91@P2: recorded, with one of them, Acalypha rhomboidea Raf., also present in south-eastern Canada (Levin

L95@P2: (Balick et al. 2000), El Salvador (Calderon & Standley 1941) and Honduras (Nelson 2008).
L96@P2: In South America, Acalypha knowledge has increased steadily during the last 25 years through national
L97@P2: and regional fl oristic treatments and checklists, and through taxonomic revisions and synopses of the
L98@P2: genus, including recent studies by the authors of this paper. Relevant works have been published for
L99@P2: Argentina (Bacigalupo & Mulgura 1999; Cardiel & Muñoz-Rodríguez 2015), Bolivia (Cardiel et al.
L100@P2: 2013b; Cardiel 2014), Brazil (Cardiel 2010; Cardiel et al. 2022b), Colombia (Cardiel 1995a; Murillo
L101@P2: 2004), continental Ecuador (Webster 1999; Cardiel & Muñoz-Rodríguez 2012), Galapagos Islands
L102@P2: (Seberg 1984), Guianas (Gillespie 1993, 1997), Paraguay (Cardiel & Muñoz-Rodríguez 2015), Peru
L103@P2: (Cardiel et al. 2013b), the South Cone (Berry 2007; Zuloaga et al. 2019), Uruguay (Cardiel & MuñozRodríguez 2015), and Venezuela (Cardiel 1999b; Armbruster et al. 2007; Hokche et al. 2008; Levin
L104@P2: 1999, 2008). The checklist of American vascular plants by Ulloa-Ulloa et al. (2017) listed 112 species
L105@P2: of Acalypha from South America (excluding the Galapagos Islands). Other relevant studies for South
L106@P2: American species of Acalypha are the nomenclatural revision of Acalypha sect. Communes—a group of
L107@P2: species widely distributed in the South American South Cone (Cardiel et al. 2013a)—, and the global
L108@P2: review of Acalypha subgenus Linostachys (Muñoz-Rodríguez et al. 2014).
L109@P2: Here, we present the fi rst continental-level checklist of Acalypha of South America, report preliminary
L110@P2: conservation assessments of all native species, and provide a red list of threatened species. This work is
L111@P2: the result of three decades of taxonomic work on South American species of Acalypha, started in the early
L112@P2: 1990s in Colombia and completed with the publication of a revision of the Brazilian species in 2022. We
L113@P2: present updated information of all South American species, with synonyms, general distribution, habitat
L114@P2: and habit, as well as indicate species that are known from only one or very few specimens and are in
L115@P2: urgent need of taxonomic study.

L138@P3: Structure of the catalogue
L139@P3: We list accepted species in alphabetical order, including author and original publication. For each species,
L140@P3: we provide a list of all homotypic and heterotypic synonyms, in chronological order of publication.
L141@P3: We include a complete list of names associated with American species of Acalypha in Appendix 1. We
L142@P3: also indicate: the habit of the species (tree, shrub, subshrub, or herb), and whether it is native or likely
L143@P3: introduced. We indicate the general geographical distribution, the habitat and altitudinal range, and the
L144@P3: distribution in South America (listing the countries where there are records). We provide one voucher
L145@P3: collection from each country where the species inhabits (when possible, a representative collection with
L146@P3: duplicates in several herbaria). When less than fi ve collections are known, we list all of them. Follows, for
L147@P3: each species, a list of selected references that provide more detailed and updated information. Finally, for
L148@P3: the native species, we provide a provisional conservation status. This information is based on specimens
L149@P3: we have studied and identifi ed fi rst hand. We include any other relevant information, if required, in the
L150@P3: remarks section.
L151@P3: Habitat, vegetation types and conservation assessments
L152@P3: Habitat typology and vegetation types follow the biogeographical regions defi ned by Griffi th et al. (1998),
L153@P3: and specifi cally level I and level II of their proposed ecological classifi cation. For the species on the
L154@P3: Galapagos Islands, we follow the habitat description provided by Seberg (1984).
L155@P3: Preliminary conservation assessments indicate the IUCN category and the criteria and sub-criteria
L156@P3: followed, whereas detailed information and justifi cations are included in Appendix 2. Conservation
L157@P3: assessments are based on the IUCN Red List Categories and Criteria (IUCN 2017). We calculated area of
L158@P3: occupancy (AOO) and extent of occurrence (EOO) with GeoCAT, a geospatial conservation assessment
L159@P3: tool (Bachman et al. 2011; http://geocat.kew.org/), using a 2 × 2 km grid cell size as recommended by
L160@P3: IUCN (2012, 2017).
L161@P3: Results
L162@P3: We list 87 accepted species and eight subspecies of Acalypha from South America. Of them, 83 are
L163@P3: native and four are cultivated or naturalised. We also list 395 synonyms, including 31 nomen nudum and
L164@P3: 19 invalidly published names (Appendix 1). Five other species are considered doubtful and excluded.
L165@P3: Following the subgeneric classifi cation by Levin et al. (2022), 78 species belong to subgenus Acalypha, and
L166@P3: nine species belong to subg. Linostachys (A. castroviejoi Cardiel, A. chocoana Cardiel, A. inaequilatera
L167@P3: Cardiel, A. longipetiolata Cardiel, A. muelleriana Urb., A. mutisii Cardiel, A. platyphylla Müll.Arg.,
L168@P3: A. salicifolia Müll.Arg., and A. villosa Jacq.). All species of Linostachys are native, and they represent
L169@P3: 32% of the 28 accepted species of Linostachys according to Muñoz-Rodríguez et al. (2014).
L170@P3: Acalypha is present in all the First Level Ecoregions proposed and mapped by Griffi th et al. (1998)
L171@P3: except Southern Andes and Monte-Patagonian (Table 1). The largest number of species of Acalypha is
L172@P3: in the Eastern Highlands ecoregion (31 species), followed by Northern Andes (28), and Central Andes
L173@P3: (28); the ecoregions with the lowest number of species are Gran Chaco (9) and Pampas (7). Among the
L174@P3: Second Level Ecoregions, Atlantic Forest has the greatest diversity of species of Acalypha (23), followed
L175@P3: by Northern Andean Highlands (20), Yungas (18), and Cerrado (17).
