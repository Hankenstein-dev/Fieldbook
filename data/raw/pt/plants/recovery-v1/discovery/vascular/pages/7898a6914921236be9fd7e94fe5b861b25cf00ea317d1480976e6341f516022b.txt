L191: Gagea is a large cite108†genus of cite109†spring cite110†flowers in the cite98†lily family .^{cite111†[2] } Gagea are perennially flowering plants.^{cite112†[3] }^{cite113†[4] } It is found primarily in cite114†Eurasia with a few species extending into cite115†North Africa and one species (cite116†Gagea serotina ) in cite117†North America .^{cite103†[1] }^{cite113†[4] }^{cite118†[5] }^{cite119†[6] }^{cite120†[7] }^{cite121†[8] }
L192: The genus is named after the cite122†English naturalist cite123†Sir Thomas Gage (1781–1820). They were originally described as species of cite124†Ornithogalum , which, together with the usual yellow colour of the flowers, explains the cite125†English name yellow star-of-Bethlehem for the common European species, cite126†Gagea lutea .^{cite127†[9] }^{cite103†[1] }
L193: ## Morphology
L194: 
L195: [cite128†edit ]
L196: 
L197: Gagea Lutea bulbous parts
L198: 
L199: The genus contains cite129†geophytes that use cite130†bulbous parts to survive unfavorable conditions and quickly reproduce during favorable conditions. Gagea species have persistent cite131†tepals , and are generally smaller in size than other closely related genera in the Liliaceae family.^{cite132†[10] }
L200: 
L201: The genus has varied leaf morphology. However, it is common for them to have cite133†basal leaves , some of which are cauline. ^{cite132†[10] }
L202: Some species have cite134†trichomes . These trichomes differ in their morphology. For instance, G. alberti and G. jensii have cite134†dendroid-type trichomes , but G. stepposa has cite134†glandular trichomes . It is also common for Gagea species to have cite135†amphistomatic stomata .^{cite136†[11] }
L203: ## Reproduction
L204: 
L205: [cite137†edit ]
L206: Gagea use two separate reproductive systems, cite138†insect pollination and cite139†asexual cite140†bulbil reproduction.^{cite141†[12] }^{cite142†[13] } There are two patterns of bulbil reproduction. In a more "continuous" mode, the plant "turns on" the ability to make bulbils, and continues to do so, only needing 2–10 millimeters cubed of total plant volume to begin cite143†vegetative reproduction .
L207: Once it reaches 100–300 mm^{3} (0.0061–0.0183 cu in) of volume, in some species, like cite126†G. lutea , the plant stops bulbil reproduction to begin expending energy towards a flowering cite144†phenophase . In some species, like cite145†G. spathacea , the plant never stops bulbil reproduction, and has no ability to reproduce cite146†sexually (through flowering). ^{cite141†[12] }
L208: It is common for Gagea species begin cite147†flowering early into spring.^{cite142†[13] }^{cite148†[14] } Many Gagea are cite149†spring ephemerals , and need to complete their flowering cycles before tree cover re-emerges. By mid-spring to summer, these species will store themselves in cite130†bulbous parts underground in anticipation of the next flowering period.^{cite148†[14] }
L209: ## Speciation/Adaptations
L210: 
L211: [cite150†edit ]
L212: 
L213: Gagea lutea fruit
L214: During their flowering periods, species show different environmental cite151†adaptations based on their cite152†habitats . G. sect. Platayspermum Boiss live in more cite153†arid regions, where it is common for insects to feed upon any exposed cite154†fruits or cite155†seeds from flowering plants. Therefore, the cite156†sect .
L215: uses large, tough cite157†sepals to ward off insects attempting to consume its fruit when it is in its developing phase.^{cite132†[10] } Other cite156†sects , such as G. lutea, commonly emerge immediately after snowmelt.^{cite148†[14] } Unlike in more arid regions, these species will have greener sepals after the flower is fully open.^{cite132†[10] }
L216: cite158†Polyploidy and cite159†hybridization , in the case of Gagea, have large impacts on its ability to undergo cite160†speciation , as evident in European species of the cite108†genus .^{cite142†[13] }^{cite161†[15] } Previous studies have shown that these two processes can significantly contribute to the process of speciation, while retaining cite162†fertility within a population.^{cite163†[16] } Gagea, therefore, has the ability to contain unique species such as cite145†G. spathacea , being a cite158†nonaploid that causes the plant to be limited in cite164†post-zygotic reproduction.
L217: The species has spread across cite165†Central Europe , the cite166†Caucasus , cite167†Sweden , cite168†Denmark , the cite169†Netherlands , and cite170†Belgium with little to no cite171†genetic differences due to its use of cite172†clonal reproduction. However, through polyploidy and hybridization events, it still retains the ability to undergo cite173†evolution . ^{cite161†[15] }
