L19@P0: Abstract
L20@P0: The nomenclature and taxonomy of the taxa related to S. vitellina L. are clarified. Two new
L21@P0: combinations, Salix × fragilis L. f. vitellina (L.) I.V.Belyaeva and S. × pendulina f. salamonii
L22@P0: (Carrière) I.V.Belyaeva, are made and one new form, S. × pendulina f. erythroflexuosa
L23@P0: I.V.Belyaeva, is described. Twenty-seven names are synonymised to the three accepted names
L24@P0: including seven as new synonyms. Eleven new typifications are made. Fifty-one existing
L25@P0: cultivars are assigned to the three taxa accepted by the authors and brief descriptions of them
L26@P0: are provided.
L27@P0: Keywords: botanical nomenclature, cultivars, hybrid willows, Salicaceae, Salix vitellina,
L28@P0: taxonomy, typification.
L29@P0: Introduction
L30@P0: As previously reported (Kovtonyuk and Belyaeva, 2015; Kuzovkina et al., 2016a;
L31@P0: Kuzovkina et al., 2016b), the scientific names of plants are frequently misapplied to plants in
L32@P0: cultivation. Although the application of scientific names is determined by the use of
L33@P0: nomenclatural types, as stated in Art. 7.1 of the International Code of Nomenclature for Algae,
L34@P0: Fungi, and Plants (ICN) (Turland et al., 2018), the requirement to indicate a type when
L35@P0: publishing a new taxon applies only to names published on or after January 1
L36@P0: st
L37@P0: , 1958.
L38@P0: Consequently, many names published before then were not typified, thereby creating confusion
L39@P0: for botanists and horticulturists in the identification and verification of taxa. Nevertheless,
L40@P0: many herbarium specimens and other historical collections associated with plant names
L41@P0: published before 1958 are now available via virtual herbaria, such as JSTOR, assisting research
L42@P0: on these taxa.
L43@P0: Salix vitellina L. was described by Linnaeus (1753). Its species epithet translates from
L44@P0-1: Latin as ‘egg-yolk-coloured,’ describing the colour of this willow’s one-year old branchlets. 43
L45@P1: Although the epithet indicates that the unusual colour caught the eye of Linnaeus, he did not
L46@P1: mention this characteristic in his description of the taxon and listed features found in many
L47@P1: other willows.
L48@P1: This species was typified by Belyaeva in Jarvis (2007) and is a basionym for other
L49@P1: names. The currently accepted name for this taxon is Salix alba var. vitellina (L.) Stokes, and
L50@P1: this name was used for a parent taxon of some ornamental willows, including weeping willows.
L51@P1: Examination of the lectotype, “Herb. Linn. No. 1158.13, female specimen (LINN)”
L52@P1: showed that the plant on this herbarium sheet had characteristics not only of S. alba L. but also
L53@P1: of S. euxina I.V.Belyaeva and is in fact a hybrid between these two species.
L54@P1: Skvortsov (1973) compared the characteristics of S. alba and S. fragilis auct. non L. (=
L55@P1: S. euxina) and made the following observations of S. alba: “older branches becoming rather
L56@P1: bright reddish or brownish, pigmentation more pronounced on the lighted side… Buds during
L57@P1: the fall and winter coloured same as branches or brighter, not blackening”; and of S. fragilis:
L58@P1: “branches within old crown uniformly yellowish grey or nearly ivory…buds during late fall
L59@P1: and winter coloured same as branches; apices blackening due to bud scale dieback.” Because
L60@P1: the type of S. vitellina has intermediate characteristics of S. alba and S. euxina (Table 1) we
L61@P1: are led to conclude that S. vitellina is a hybrid between the two, for which the name is S. ×
L62@P1: fragilis L. (Belyaeva, 2009).
L63@P1: Although Linnaeus had the opportunity to observe Salix vitellina in cultivation in
L64@P1: Sweden (Skvortsov, 1968; Skvortsov, 1999), he did not recognise the hybrid nature of either
L65@P1: this taxon or S. fragilis, which he also described (Belyaeva, 2009). Thus, clarification of the
L66@P1: nomenclature and taxonomy of the taxa related to S. vitellina is needed. This is of particular
L67@P1: relevance to the authors’ current projects: ‘World Checklist of Salicaceae s. str.’ (I.V.Belyaeva
L68@P1: and R.H.A.Govaerts); a compilation for the ‘Checklist for Cultivars of Salix L. (willow)’
L69@P1: (Y.A.Kuzovkina); research on the cultivation of willows in the Middle Urals
L70@P1: (O.V.Epantchintseva and I.V.Belyaeva) and verification of the taxonomic identities of
L71@P1: cultivated Salix growing at the Royal Botanic Gardens Kew (K.McGinn and I.V.Belyaeva).
L72@P1: Here, we verify the taxonomic identities of cultivated plants associated with S. vitellina in order
L73@P1: to apply the correct scientific names. We do so by studying original herbarium material and,
L74@P1-2: where possible, making typifications.44

L174@P3: Nomenclatural and taxonomic notes
L175@P3: There are numerous willow cultivars with vitellinous (egg-yolk yellow) branches, used
L176@P3: in horticulture and basketry, which exist under a variety of different names, causing great
L177@P3: confusion in their application. Indeed, it is common to find multiple names used for the same
L178@P3: willow taxon in botanic gardens and plant nurseries due to the misapplication of names or
L179@P3: misidentification. The authors of some publications interpret the names related to Salix alba,
L180@P3: S. euxina, S. × fragilis and S. vitellina differently (Rehder, 1949; Snarskis, 1954; Rechinger,
L181@P3: 1964, 1981; Jalas and Suominen, 1976; Chmelař and Meusel, 1976; Neumann, 1981; Skvortsov
L182@P3: and Edmondson, 1982; Chmelař, 1983; Krüssman, 1984; Meikle, 1984; Meikle, 1985; Clarke,
L183@P3: 1988; Santamour and McArdle, 1988; Newsholm, 1992; Blanco, 1993; Rechinger and
L184@P3: Akeroyd, 1993; Lautenschlager-Fleury and Lautenschlager, 1994; Skvortsov, 1999; Belyaeva
L185@P3: et al., 1999; Berg and Christensen, 2000; Hörandl et al., 2002; Fischer et al., 2005; Belyaeva,
L186@P3: 2009; Stace, 2010; Mabbett, 2012; Hillier and Lancaster, 2014; Stace et al., 2015: Marchenko,
L187@P3: 2017; Schulz, 2018; Sell, 2018). However, there are only three accepted scientific names
L188@P3: recognised by the authors of the current paper that apply to willows with twigs whose bark is
L189@P3: both vitellinous (egg-yolk coloured) and sanguineous (blood red coloured) and which are
L190@P3: related to S. vitellina. One is Salix × fragilis f. vitellina (L.) I.V.Belyaeva, a form of crack
L191@P3: willow (S. × fragilis = S. alba × S. euxina); the second is a hybrid weeping willow, S. ×
L192@P3-4: pendulina f. salamonii (Carrière) I.V.Belyaeva (= S. babylonica L. × S. × fragilis f. vitellina) 46
L193@P4: and the third is S. × pendulina f. erythroflexuosa I.V.Belyaeva (= S. babylonica f. tortuosa
L194@P4: Y.L.Chou × S. pendulina f. salamonii).
L195@P4: 
L196@P4: All cultivars attributable to these three names are easily propagated vegetatively. Under
L197@P4: these trees, one can usually find many fallen branchlets and branches broken by the wind that
L198@P4: may root and propagate in this way. These willows are also able to reproduce by seed, creating
L199@P4: morphologically variable seedlings similar to one parent or different from both parents.
L200@P4: Identification of these taxa is consequently challenging, as mentioned in publications reporting
L201@P4: molecular research by Beismann et al. (1997), Triest et al. (1999), Triest (2001), Baccardia et
L202@P4: al. (2003), De Cock et al. (2003), Kell et al. (2008), Trung et al. (2008) and van Puyvelde
L203@P4: (2013). However, narrowing the taxonomy of this group of willows to the three accepted
L204@P4: names, as described below, will simplify the process of identification. These willows are very
L205@P4: ornamental and widely cultivated around the world (Fig. 1–4).
