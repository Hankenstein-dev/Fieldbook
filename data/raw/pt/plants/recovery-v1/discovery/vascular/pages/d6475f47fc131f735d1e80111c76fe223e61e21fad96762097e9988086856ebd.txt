L202: Sonchus is a genus of flowering plants in the tribe cite102†Cichorieae within the family cite100†Asteraceae ^{cite107†[2] }^{cite112†[5] } and are commonly known as sow thistles (less commonly hare thistles or hare lettuces). Sowthistles are annual, biennial or perennial herbs, with or without rhizomes and a few are even woody (subgenus Dendrosonchus, restricted to the cite113†Canary Islands and cite114†Madeira ).^{cite115†[6] }^{cite116†[7] }^{cite117†[8] }^{cite118†[9] }^{cite119†[10] }
L203: ## Description
L204: 
L205: [cite120†edit ]
L206: 
L207: Sonchus hierrensis in the Canary Island of La Gomera
L208: The genus is named after the cite121†Ancient Greek for such plants, σόγχος. All are characterized by soft, somewhat irregularly lobed cite122†leaves that clasp the stem and, at least initially, form a basal rosette. The cite123†stem contains a milky cite124†latex . cite125†Flower heads are yellow and range in size from half to one inch in diameter; the cite126†florets are all of ray type.
L209: Sonchus fruits are single-seeded, dry and cite127†indehiscent .^{cite128†[11] } Sow thistles are common roadside plants, and while native to cite129†Eurasia and tropical cite130†Africa , they are found almost worldwide in cite131†temperate regions.^{cite132†[12] }
L210: Mature sow thistle stems can range from 30 cm to 2 m (1 to 6 ft) tall, depending upon species and growing conditions. Coloration ranges from green to purple in older plants. Sow thistles exude a milky cite124†latex when any part of the plant is cut or damaged, and it is from this fact that the plants obtained the common name, "sow thistle", as they were fed to lactating cite133†sows in the belief that cite134†milk production would increase.
L211: Sow thistles are known as "milk thistles" in some regions, although cite135†milk thistle more commonly refers to the genus cite136†Silybum .
L212: ### Comparison with dandelions
L213: 
L214: [cite137†edit ]
L215: 
L216: They superficially resemble and are in the same family as cite138†dandelions , and even have cite139†pappus -like structures resembling dandelion seeds. However, they are usually taller than dandelions, have multiple flowers per stem (as opposed to one flower per dandelion), and have more irregularly shaped lobes.^{cite140†[13] }

L328: ## Invasive
L329: 
L330: [cite249†edit ]
L331: In many areas sow thistles are considered cite250†noxious weeds ,^{cite251†[15] } as they grow quickly in a wide range of conditions and their wind-borne cite252†seeds allow them to spread rapidly. cite149†Sonchus arvensis , the perennial sow thistle, is considered the most economically detrimental, as it can crowd commercial cite253†crops , is a heavy consumer of cite254†nitrogen in cite255†soils , may deplete soil water of land left to cite256†fallow , and can regrow and sprout additional plants from its creeping cite257†roots .
L332: However, sow thistles are easily uprooted by hand, and their soft stems present little resistance to slashing or mowing.
L333: Most cite258†livestock will readily devour sow thistle in preference to cite259†grass , and this lettuce-relative is edible and nutritious to humans—in fact this is the meaning of the second part of the Latin name of the common sow thistle, oleraceus.^{cite260†[16] } Attempts at weed control by herbicidal use, to the neglect of other methods, may have led to a proliferation of these species in some environments.^{cite261†[17] }
L334: ## Cultivation and use as food
L335: 
L336: [cite262†edit ]
L337: Sow thistles are common host plants for cite263†aphids . Gardeners may consider this a benefit or a curse; aphids may spread from sow thistle to other plants, but alternatively the sow thistle can encourage the growth of beneficial predators such as cite264†hoverflies . In this regard sow thistles make excellent cite265†sacrificial plants .
L338: Sonchus species are used as food plants by the cite266†larvae of some cite267†Lepidoptera including cite268†Celypha rufana and the cite269†broad-barred white , cite270†grey chi , cite271†nutmeg , and cite272†shark moths. The cite273†fly cite274†Tephritis formosa is known to attack the cite125†capitula of this plant.^{cite275†[18] }
L339: Sow thistles have been used as fodder, particularly for cite276†rabbits , hence the other common names of "hare thistle" or "hare lettuce". They are also edible to cite277†humans as a cite278†leaf vegetable ; old leaves and stalks can be cite279†bitter but young leaves have a flavour similar to cite280†lettuce . Going by the name pūhā or rareke (raraki) it is a traditional food eaten in cite281†New Zealand by cite282†Māori . When cooked the flavour is reminiscent of cite283†chard .
L340: The greens were also eaten by the cite284†indigenous people of North America . Edible raw when young, the older greens can also be eaten after cooking briefly.^{cite285†[19] }
