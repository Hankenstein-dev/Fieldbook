L40@P2: INTRODUCTION
L41@P2: Ongoing bryological surveys in the Pyrénées-Orientales department (France) have already
L42@P2: yielded several remarkable species that have been published elsewhere, among them
L43@P2: Gymnomitrion adustum Nees or Bryoerythrophyllum inaequalifolium (Taylor) R.H.Zander
L44@P2: (Hugonnot 2015a, b). In the course of completing a synthesis of the Pyrénées-Orientales
L45@P2: bryoflora, we performed targeted surveys that allowed observing several additional noticeable
L46@P2: mosses. Among them three turned out to be interesting or new records for the Pyrenees since
L47@P2: none of them are cited in Casas et al. (2001, 2004) for the southern face of these mountains, nor
L48@P2: in Infante (2015) or Thouvenot (2002) for the northern face. In the present note, the new localities
L49@P2: are briefly described and comments on the ecological affinities and distribution of these mosses
L50@P2: are given.
L51@P2: All the samples have been collected by the author and are deposited in the private herbarium
L52@P2: of V. Hugonnot.

L54@P2: FRANCE. Pyrénées-Orientales, Cerdagne, Bolquère, l’Accordéon, 2000 m, 42º33’020’’N,
L55@P2: 02º01’012’’E, Hugonnot 9-10-2015 (Herbarium Hugonnot).
L56@P2: This is a rare Eurasiatic species (Pierrot, 1985), widely distributed in Eastern European
L57@P2: mountain ranges (Alps, Carpathians, Sudetes), and also occurring in Caucasus and Russia
L58@P2: (Ignatov & Milyutina, 2010; Orgaz et al., 2012). The nearest populations are in Auvergne

L62@P3: Brachythecium geheebii is characteristic of upper mountainous to subalpine belt, but its
L63@P3: ecology is insufficiently known (Orgaz et al., 2012). It has been mentioned as saxicolous,
L64@P3: growing on siliceous rocks in cool and dark ambient (Orgaz Alvarez & Blockeel, 2010), a
L65@P3: description that applies well to the situation in the Pyrenees, where the species has been seen
L66@P3: over gneiss outcrops in subalpine Abies alba woodland on steep slope. In Auvergne and Vosges,
L67@P3: Brachythecium geheebii is more frequently found growing on living trees, mostly beeches, in
L68@P3: mountain to subalpine Abies alba-Fagus sylvatica woodlands. In Pyrenées-Orientales, it was
L69@P3: mostly found growing on rocks. It is worth noting that Marstaller considers this species to be a
L70@P3: differential species of the Anomodontetum rugelii Pec. 1965, an association characterised as
L71@P3: subneutrophytic and mesophytic. Anomodon rugelii (Müll.Hall) Keissl. is frequently associated
L72@P3: with Brachythecium geheebii in Auvergne but not so in Pyrénées-Orientales. Associated species
L73@P3: in the Pyrenees are Pseudoleskea saviana (De Not.) Latzel, Sanionia uncinata (Hedw.) Loeske,
L74@P3: and Paraleucobryum longifolium (Hedw.) Loeske.
L75@P3: The sporophytes were not seen in the Pyrenees. They are occasionally produced in other regions
L76@P3: of France but reported as rare, and even absent for entire regions of Europe.
L77@P3: Pseudoleskea saviana (De Not.) Latzel
L78@P3: FRANCE. Pyrénées-Orientales, Cerdagne, Bolquère, l’Accordéon, 2000 m, 42º33’020’’N,
L79@P3: 02º01’012’’E, Hugonnot 9-10- 2015 (Herbarium Hugonnot).
L80@P3: Pseudoleskea saviana is widely spread in North America and Western Eurasia (Lawton,
L81@P3: 1957). The species is still badly known and probably has been overlooked. In France, it has been
L82@P3: recorded in Massif Central, with particular abundance in Monts Dore (Puy-de-Dôme), Monts du
L83@P3: Cantal (Cantal), Haut-Forez (Loire), Pilat (Loire), Tanargue (Ardèche), Margeride (Lozère)
L84@P3: (Meylan, 1938; pers. obs.). It has been additionally recorded in Corsica (Lawton, 1957; Pierrot,
L85@P3: 1987). It is not known from the Iberian Peninsula.
L86@P3: Pseudoleskea saviana grows on living trees, and more rarily on humiferous rocks (Dierssen,
L87@P3: 2001). It is characteristically observed at the base of old and deformed veteran Fagus sylvatica,
L88@P3: at the upper altitudinal limit for this tree species in subalpine Fagus sylvatica-woodland. In such
L89@P3: habitats, beech trees are often of much reduced stature, and tend to develop tortuous trunks that
L90@P3: accumulate huge amounts of organic material. In Pyrénées-Orientales, Pseudoleskea saviana has
L91@P3: been observed mostly over gneissic rocks, or exceptionally on Fagus sylvatica, where it forms
L92@P3: dense mats, together with Paraleucobryum longifolium and Lescuraea mutabilis (Brid.) Lindb.,
L93@P3: and occasionally, Brachythecium geheebii. This community is close to Lescuraeetum mutabilis
L94@P3: Wilmanns 1962.

L99@P4: Schistidium atrichum is widespread in mountain ranges of Western North America, from
L100@P4: North California to South-Eastern Alaska, with a particular abundance in Rocky Mountains. It
L101@P4: is of exceptional occurrence in Europe (Chavoutier & Hugonnot, 2013). It was recorded in
L102@P4: Savoie, in Vanoise Massif (Sébille, 1909), where it has been recently confirmed (Chavoutier &
L103@P4: Hugonnot, 2013). In the French Pyrenees, it has been mentioned by Culmann (1924) from
L104@P4: Ariège, where it has been a long forgotten species.
L105@P4: Schistidium atrichum is a high altitude species, growing over 2500 m in most of its localities.
L106@P4: In the Pyrenees, the altitude was slightly lower. The species is restricted to harsh alpine
L107@P4: environments. It is an acidophilic species that thrives on siliceous rocks, often in small pockets
L108@P4: where minor amounts of detritic sand may accumulate. The new Pyrenean population was made
L109@P4: of several tenths of tiny turfs, often with Schistidium dupretii (Thér.) W.A.Weber.

L165@P6: INTRODUCTION
L166@P6: In the course of bryological surveys in the eastern part of the Pyrenees, several bryophytes
L167@P6: of phytogeographical interest were found. Among them, Cinclidium stygium Sw. deserves to be
L168@P6: mentioned because it had not been previously recorded south of the Alps, which means a
L169@P6: significant southwards extension of its known range.
L170@P6: The worldwide distribution of Cinclidium stygium was reviewed by Mogensen (1973). This
L171@P6: species has a circumpolar distribution in boreal and low-arctic regions in the northern
L172@P6: hemisphere, and a remarkable disjunction in South America. In the Holarctic, its range extends
L173@P6: farther south than the other species of Cinclidium, none of which has been recorded in southern
L174@P6: Europe. Conversely, it is rare or absent in high-arctic regions. In North America, it is widespread
L175@P6: in Canada (Koponen, 1974), from Yukon to Quebec. It is locally frequent in Alaska and
L176@P6: Greenland. The species is widespread in Fennoscandia (Koponen, 1969), in Altaï Mountains
L177@P6: (Ignatov, 1994), and Kamtschatka (Czernyadjeva & Ignatova, 2008), in Switzerland
L178@P6: (http://www.nism.uzh.ch/map/map_en.php), of local occurrence in the north of the British Isles
L179@P6: and Ireland (Blockeel et al., 2014), in Denmark and Germany (Meinunger & Schröder, 2007),
L180@P6: as well as in Poland (Karczmarz, 1962). Its southwestern limit lies in French Alps, where it is
L181@P6: very rare (Sarrassat, 1939). In this country the most important populations are in the Jura (Hétier,
L182@P6: 1896; Hillier, 1954).
L183@P6: Cinclidium stygium is experiencing a gradual decline, particularly in the southern part of its
L184@P6: range. It has disappeared from several countries (Netherlands, Belgium), and is hence of
L185@P6-7: conservation concern. It is for example VU in Ireland, CR in Slovakia, EN in Romania (Hodgetts, Boletín de la Sociedad Española de Briología 46–47 (2016)
L186@P7: 6
L187@P7: 2015). In France it is protected under the law in the region of Franche-Comté (Arrêté of 22 June
L188@P7: 1992, Journal Officiel of 4 August 1992).
L189@P7: This paper intends to report the presence of an isolated population of Cinclidium stygium in
L190@P7: the Pyrenees, as well as to describe its ecology and discuss conservation issues.
L191@P7: STUDY AREA
L192@P7: Madres-Coronat massif occupies a position between the Mediterranean Sea and the first high
L193@P7: summits of the Pyrenean range. The Pic Madres culminates at 2469 m. It is settled less than 60
L194@P7: km from the Mediterranean Sea. The geologic bedrock is mostly composed of schists and granite,
L195@P7: with veins of granodiorite and other magmatic rocks very rich in Ca, Mg, K, etc. The summit
L196@P7: plateau has been carved by glaciers which formed typical U-shaped valleys, where horizontal
L197@P7: sections and drops in elevation alternate. The plateau has been fractured by erosion, and the
L198@P7: cracks are filled with detritic granitic sand that plays the role of an aquifer restituting slowly
L199@P7: accumulated large quantities of water. This may account for the particular abundance of peaty
L200@P7: habitats on the slopes of the massif.
L201@P7: The climate is marked by oceanic influence but strongly modified by altitude. The annual
L202@P7: mean precipitation is 1500-1900 mm at Madres summit. The summer may experience violent
L203@P7: storms. The mean annual temperature is low (around 3°C at 2300 m). Snow is abundant in winter
L204@P7: and may last from November to April in alpine habitats. Cold and dry winds are very frequent.

L219@P8: RESULTS
L220@P8: The studied material has been confirmed to belong to Cinclidium stygium. However, it has
L221@P8: been found rather variable as far as gametophytes are concerned. Frequently, the plants appeared
L222@P8: with the vegetative parts depauperate, with small leaves, from 1.5 to 2 mm, of ovate to elliptical
L223@P8: outline and an acute apex. The largest leaves (from 3 to 4.5 mm) were oblong to obovate-elliptic,
L224@P8: longer than wide and sharply apiculate. The best developed ones were distinctly petiol-like at
L225@P8: base and had an elongated areolation. The margin was uniformly unistratose. Old portions of
L226@P8: stem were densely tomentose. Macronemata were initiated as indistinct longitudinal lines at the
L227@P8: apex of shoots. No gametangia were observed. At 2 cm deep in the colony, the leaves were
L228@P8: mostly damaged and reduced to their thick nerves, the lamina cells being destroyed.
L229@P8: The colony was composed of an extensive red-blackish mat of densely interwoven stems,
L230@P8: packed into a dense tomentum. The mat was strikingly superficial, not deep, as it is often the
L231@P8: case in floating fens. Here, it did not exceed 2 cm deep, and grew upon a dense and black peat
L232@P8: where no fragments of plants were recognizable.
L233@P8: Cinclidium stygium grew on a very gentle slope (less than 5 deg. of inclination) in a
L234@P8: subalpine Trichophorum cespitosum (L.) Hartm. fen, attributable to Caricion davallianae Klika
L235@P8: 1934. Associated bryophytes were not abundant, with Scorpidium cossonii (Schimp.) Hedenäs,
L236@P8: Ptychostomum pseudotriquetrum (Hedw.) J.R.Spence & H.P.Ramsay (C. sp.), Imbribryum
L237@P8: muehlenbeckii (Bruch & Schimp.) N. Pedersen, Campylium stellatum (Hedw.) Lange &
L238@P8: C.E.O.Jensen, Palustriella falcata (Brid.) Hedenäs, Sphagnum teres (Schimp.) Ångstr., and
L239@P8: Calliergon richardsonii (Mitt.) Kindb.
L240@P8: Fifteen square meters were occupied by Cinclidium stygium. Three main colonies were
L241@P8: observed in distinct small similar fens. Several hundred meters of unoccupied fens were also
L242@P8: present in the surroundings.
L243@P8: DISCUSION
L244@P8: Determination of material
L245@P8: The genus Cinclidium includes 4 species (Mogensen, 1973). The distinction between them
L246@P8: is generally straighforward taking into consideration sexual condition, leaf shape and capsule
L247@P8: characters. Then, difficulties may arise when only sterile material is at hand, which is the case
L248@P8: here. In particular, the distinction of Cinclidium stygium and C. articum (Bruch & Schimp.)
L249@P8: Schimp. may raise serious problems. The areolation of Cinclidium stygium is longer (lamina
L250@P8-9: cells more than two times as long as wide) than that of C. arcticum (less than two times as long Boletín de la Sociedad Española de Briología 46–47 (2016)
L251@P9: 8
L252@P9: as wide). The leaf apiculum is long squarrose-pointed in Cinclidium stygium while being shortly
L253@P9: acuminate in C. arcticum, which is a northern species that has been reported from Germany in
L254@P9: recent times (Meinunger & Schröder, 2007).
L255@P9: Reproduction and Ecology
L256@P9: Cinclidium stygium is a synoicous species whose sporophytes are occasional. They are
L257@P9: frequent in Jura or Southern Alps (pers. obs.), where they were considered frequent in the past
L258@P9: (Hétier, 1896; Hillier, 1954). Absence of sporophytes in monoicous taxa may relate to the
L259@P9: absence of gametangia, inefficient fertilization or abortion of sporophytes at early stages of
L260@P9: development. In the case of Cinclidium stygium in the Pyrenees, it is clearly linked to the total
L261@P9: absence of gametangia. The harsh climatic conditions could be responsible for a depleted
L262@P9: fertility, which is common at the margin of plants distribution areas.
L263@P9: Cinclidium stygium is a fen indicator included in many types of “eutrophic” communities
L264@P9: (see review in Koponen, 1969), mostly Caricion lasiocarpae (Braun, 1968; Paul & Lutz, 1941).
L265@P9: There is a great deal of confusion about fen nomenclature, deriving from the phytosociological
L266@P9: approach to this vegetation type. Calcareous fens are widely considered eutrophic systems even
L267@P9: though detailed chemical characteristic of water or soil are mostly lacking. Though admittedly
L268@P9: rich in Ca++ solutes, or other minerals, they are mostly poor in biogenous elements (mostly P
L269@P9: and N compounds), and then clearly oligotrophic (Braun, 1968; Lamentowicz et al. 2013;
L270@P9: Kooijman et al., 2015). This is likely the case in the Pyrenees where no natural source of nutrient
L271@P9: enrichment is known.
L272@P9: Cinclidium stygium may extend into thinly wooded stands, which is the case in Alps or Jura,
