L504: ## Maintenance
L505: 
L506: Does well as a potted patio plant. Will tolerate reflected heat and is drought tolerant. Looks nice in the winter with the slender branches displaying shredded bark. Native habitat: high elevation, canyons, ditches, ravines, thickets.
L507: ## Description
L508: 
L509: Blooms June-September. Produces numerous long, pink, tubular flowers with re-curved linear lobes before new foliage appears. Long, semi-woody, spreading to decumbent stems. Fruit is a capsule. Larval Host: Crescentspot Butterfly.
L510:

L520: Seed  | No Treatment  | Germinates promptly from fresh untreated seeds planted in a greenhouse or outdoors in spring after soil has warmed. Seeds should be planted between .3"-.5" deep in a well draining mix. Keep moist, but not soggy. Germination will occur in 2-3 weeks.  | Collect seeds as soon as capsules begin to dry and brown, but before they split open. As Anisacanthus are intermittent bloomers, seeds can be collected over several periods during the season. Allow seeds to air dry before storage.
L521: A light dusting of insecticide is recommended for long term storage of seeds.  | 1) 1) Nokes, Jill. How to Grow Native Plants of Texas and the Southwest: Revised and Updated Edition. University of Texas Press, 2001.
L522: Cutting  | Cutting  | Cut just below a node approximately 4-6 inches long and remove leaves from the bottom half. Rooting hormone helps, but isn't necessary. Insert cuttings at least 3 inches apart in a flat or small pots to allow space for a substantial root system. Kept fairly moist, rooting will occur within 3 weeks.  | A. puberulus cuttings grow best from juvenile, new growth. Collect cuttings in early summer and again in the fall after the most recent flush of growth has begun to harden off.
L523:  | 1) 1) Nokes, Jill. How to Grow Native Plants of Texas and the Southwest: Revised and Updated Edition. University of Texas Press, 2001.
L524: Seedling  | Transplant  | Transplants should be cut back by at least a third and kept moist.  | Where topsoil is sufficient, plants often self-sow. Young plants can be dug up with as much of the root system intact and planted where desired.  | 1) 1) Nokes, Jill. How to Grow Native Plants of Texas and the Southwest: Revised and Updated Edition. L556: ## About the Region
L557: 
L558: cite151†Image: 2026 Fall Symposium Logo L559: 
L560: This low-elevations region of Texas extends inland from the barrier islands, about 60 or so miles, and stretches from Brownsville to Louisiana. In total, it covers about 9.5 million acres, with a high point of 150 feet in elevation. More than 1000 species of plants can be found in this region. On the southern end, species more common in Mexico (such as Sabal mexicana) and Central America occur.
L561: The barrier islands provide us with dune systems, and clay flats to the inland side, which have species found in these areas alone. Many plants here, such as Ipomoea pes-caprae (beach morning glory), can be found throughout tropical regions of the globe. I’ve encountered the same species on the beaches of Guam.
L562: Once inland, vast marshes and wet prairies occur. Occasionally, oak (Quercus fusiformis) groves can be found. Common grasses include species of Bothriochloa, Paspalum, and Sporobolus; eastern gamagrass (Tripsacum dactyloides); and switchgrass (Panicum species). Many rivers and creeks cut through the Gulf Prairies, and along these riparian areas various species of trees, Sabal minor, and other plants adapted to clay soils can be found.
L563: Due to overgrazing, farming, and fire suppression, woody species such as mesquite (Prosopis glandulosa) and huisache (Acacia farnesiana), and invasive species such as chinaberry (Melia azedarach), Brazilian pepper (Schinus terebinthifolius), and Chinese tallow (Sapium sebiferum) have increased and displaced our native flora.
