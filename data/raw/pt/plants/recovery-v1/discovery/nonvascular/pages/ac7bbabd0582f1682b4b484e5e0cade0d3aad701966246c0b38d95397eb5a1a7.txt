The first cite75†native Hawaiian woman to receive a cite76†PhD in cite77†science ,^{cite78†[1] } she became a leading expert on Pacific marine cite79†algae .^{cite80†[2] }
L148: ## Early life
L149: 
L150: [cite81†edit ]
L151: Abbott was born Isabella Kauakea Yau Yung Aiona in cite66†Hana , cite67†Maui , cite68†Territory of Hawaii , on June 20, 1919. Her Hawaiian name means "white rain of Hana" and she was known as "Izzy".^{cite82†[3] } Her father was ethnically Chinese while her mother was a cite75†Native Hawaiian . Her mother taught her about edible cite83†Hawaiian seaweeds ^{cite82†[3] } and the value and diversity of Hawaii's native plants. Abbott was the only girl and second youngest in a family of eight siblings.^{cite84†[4] }
L152: She grew up in cite85†Honolulu near Waikiki, and graduated from cite70†Kamehameha Schools in 1937.^{cite78†[1] }^{cite82†[3] } She received her undergraduate degree in cite86†botany at the cite71†University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa in 1941,^{cite82†[3] } a master's degree in botany from the cite72†University of Michigan in 1942, and a cite87†Doctor of Philosophy in botany from the cite73†University of California, Berkeley in 1950.^{cite80†[2] }^{cite82†[3] } She married zoologist cite88†Donald Putnam Abbott (1920–1986), who had been a fellow student at the University of Hawaiʻi as well as Berkeley.
L153: The couple moved to cite89†Pacific Grove, California where her husband taught at the cite90†Hopkins Marine Station run by cite65†Stanford University .^{cite91†[5] } Since at that time women were rarely considered for academic posts, she spent time raising her daughter Annie Abbott Foerster, while studying the algae of the California coast. She adapted recipes to use the local bull kelp (cite92†Nereocystis ) in foods such as cakes and pickles.^{cite82†[3] }
L154: ## Career
L155: 
L156: [cite93†edit ]
L157: 
L158: In 1956 she became a research associate and taught as a lecturer at Hopkins. She compiled a book on marine algae of the cite94†Monterey peninsula , which later was expanded to include all of the California coast. She was awarded the cite95†Darbaker Prize by the cite96†Botanical Society of America in 1969.^{cite97†[6] } By 1972, cite65†Stanford University promoted her directly to full professor of biology, where she was the first woman and first person of color in this position.^{cite98†[7] }
L159: In 1982 both Abbotts retired and moved back to Hawaii, where she was hired by the University of Hawaii to teach cite74†ethnobotany , the interaction of humans and plants.^{cite82†[3] }
L160: She authored eight books and over 150 publications. She was considered the world's leading expert on Hawaiian seaweeds, known in the cite99†Hawaiian language as cite100†limu . She was credited with discovering over 200 species, with several named after her, including the cite101†Rhodomelaceae family (red algae) genus of Abbottella.^{cite84†[4] } This earned her the nickname "First Lady of Limu."
L161: In 1993 she received the Charles Reed Bishop Medal and in 1997 she received the cite102†Gilbert Morgan Smith Medal from the cite103†National Academy of Sciences .^{cite104†[8] }^{cite105†[9] }
L162: She was the G. P. Wilder Professor of Botany from 1980 until her retirement in 1982, when she and her husband moved to Hawaii where she continued her research as the professor emerita of botany at the University of Hawaii.^{cite106†[10] } She served on the board of directors of the cite107†Bernice P.
L163: Bishop Museum .^{cite108†[11] } In November 1997 she co-authored an essay in the cite109†Honolulu Star-Bulletin criticizing the trustees of Kamehameha Schools, which led to its reorganization.^{cite110†[12] }^{cite111†[13] } In 1988 she was elected a fellow of the cite112†American Association for the Advancement of Science .^{cite113†[14] }
L164: In 2005, she was named a cite114†Living Treasure of Hawaiʻi by the cite115†Honpa Hongwanji Mission of Hawaii .^{cite116†[15] }^{cite117†[16] }
L165: 
L166: She was considered the foremost authority on the algae of the Pacific Ocean basin and in 2008 she received a lifetime achievement award from the cite118†Hawaii Department of Land and Natural Resources for her studies of coral reefs.^{cite119†[17] }
L167: The Board of Regents of the University of Hawaiʻi unanimously voted to rename the Life Sciences Building after Abbott in 2023.^{cite117†[16] }^{cite120†[18] }
L168: ## Death
L169: 
L170: [cite121†edit ]
L171: 
L172: Isabella Kauakea Aiona Abbott died on October 28, 2010, at the age of 91 at her home in cite85†Honolulu .^{cite122†[19] } Abbott's surviving family includes her daughter Annie Abbott Foerster, and a granddaughter, both residing in Hawaii.
L173: 
L174: To preserve Abbott's legacy and career as a botanist, the cite123†University of Hawaiʻi established a scholarship to support graduate research in Hawaiian cite74†ethnobotany and marine botany.^{cite124†[20] }
