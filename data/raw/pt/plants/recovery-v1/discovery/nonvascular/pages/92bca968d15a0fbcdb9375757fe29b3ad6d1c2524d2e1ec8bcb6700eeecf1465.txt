L6@P1:  The Hawaiian Islands are the most distant from any other land
L7@P1: mass on earth. Because of this geography, their flora and fauna
L8@P1: have evolved in isolation and are unique: about 25% of Hawaii’s
L9@P1: reef fish, coral, and algae species occur nowhere else. Their
L10@P1: isolated evolution, combined with human alteration of near-shore
L11@P1: habitats, has made Hawaii’s marine habitats, especially coral
L12@P1: reefs, particularly vulnerable to alien invasions.
L13@P1: Four species of alien algae have become invasive in Hawaii:
L14@P1: Acanthophora spicifera, Hypnea musciformis, Kappaphycus spp.,
L15@P1: and Gracilaria salicornia. Acanthophora spicifera arrived as an
L16@P1: accidental “visitor” on the bottom of a shipping barge in 1950; the
L17@P1: latter three species were intentionally introduced on Oahu and
L18@P1: Molokai in the 1970’s for experimental aquaculture for the agar
L19@P1: industry. These “weedy” species have now spread from their initial
L20@P1: sites of introduction and are competing with native marine flora
L21@P1: and fauna.
L22@P1: Most alien algae populations are currently confined to discreet
L23@P1: areas and may still be able to be controlled by removal and/or
L24@P1: enhancement of native grazer populations. Other areas, particularly coral reefs, remain at risk from the spread of aliens from
L25@P1: these sites.
L26@P1: This field guide is designed to help ReefWatchers protect Hawaii’s
L27@P1: reefs. It includes information about invasive alien algae, native
L28@P1: seaweeds (so important in the reef food web, as well as for cultural usage), and native algae that may become invasive in altered
L29@P1: habitats (e.g. from nutrient pollution or overfishing).
L30@P1: Algae are major contributors to our coral reefs. Just as plants are
L31@P1: on land, algae are the primary producers in the ocean. Algae exist
L32@P1: in many forms: as the larger, more-recognizable macroalgae in this
L33@P1: guide, as well as lesser known encrusting coralline, turf, and
L34@P1: filamentous forms. Even the corals are photosynthetic factories,
L35@P1: infused with zooxanthellae, a microscopic alga.
L36@P1: How to Use the Field Guide
L37@P1: Organization: The first section of this guide portrays an alien alga
L38@P1: positioned next to the native alga with which it is most likely to be
L39@P1: confused. Later pages alphabetically list natives species commonly
L40@P1: found in the same habitats on the Big Island.
L41@P1: Colored Bands: The algal names are located in a color band which
L42@P1: specifies whether the algae in alien, native or of unknown origin (see
L43@P1: legend below).
L44@P1: What’s in a name?: Most of our Hawaiian algae do not have common
L45@P1: names, except for the edibles that have Hawaiian names. The latin
L46@P1: name is comprised of two parts: the genus name groups related algae
L47@P1: while the species name is unique to that particular plant.
L48@P1:  Example: (genus) Halimeda opuntia (species)
L49@P1: Group Classifications: The alien and native macroaglae included in
L50@P1: this guide are from 3 major groups of marine algae. The group
L51@P1: classifications are based upon photosynthetic pigments and give the
L52@P1: algae their characteristic colors: the Rhodophyta (red algae),
L53@P1: Chlorophyta (green algae), and Phaeophyta (brown algae).
L54@P1: Features: Key features of each alga are listed next to the its picture,
L55@P1: providing a quick reference for identification in the field. Unique or
L56@P1: defining characteristics, color and habitat are noted.

L68@P1-2: 2 3Acanthophora spicifera Rhodophyta
L69@P2: Description: Solid, cylindrical branches covered with many distinctive
L70@P2: small spinelike branches, branched sparingly to repeatedly. Grows to
L71@P2: 20 cm high. Color varies from red, brown to dark green but turns
L72@P2: yellow in bright sunlight.
L73@P2: Habitat: Acanthophora spicifera is abundant on calm, shallow reef
L74@P2: flats, tidepools, and on rocky intertidal benches. This alga attaches
L75@P2: to hard substrates such as rocks, basalt ledges, or dead coral heads.
L76@P2: It may also be found free-floating, due to its brittle, easily-broken
L77@P2: nature.
L78@P2: Ecology: Acanthophora spicifera is the most widespread and successful alien alga in Hawai‘i. A. spicifera is often found in communities
L79@P2: with common native and alien intertidal species, such as Hypnea and
L80@P2: Laurencia spp. The brittle nature of the branches often results in
L81@P2: fragmentation, which contributes to frequent, large free-floating
L82@P2: populations and its widespread distribution. A. spicifera was first
L83@P2: found in Pearl Harbor and Waikiki in the early 1950’s, most likely
L84@P2: entering Oahu on a barge from Guam. The alga’s adaptability has
L85@P2: enabled it to spread throughout the state: it is found in brackish
L86@P2: water ponds, salty tidepools, on basalt ledges and in sandy bottomed
L87@P2: habitats attached to coral rubble. It is now found on all of the main
L88@P2: Hawaiian Islands and is a common component of the intertidal environment throughout the state.
L89@P2: Competition between A. spicifera and native algae and invertebrates
L90@P2: is likely, but impacts on community structure and diversity have not
L91@P2: yet been extensively quantified.

L116@P2-3: 4 5Hypnea musciformis Rhodophyta
L117@P3: Description: Rounded main branches, 1-3 mm diameter, with short,
L118@P3: pointed side branches. Tips of branches are flattened and have characteristic “hooks” or tendril-like projections. Length from 3-30 cm. Bright
L119@P3: yellow in sun to dark red in shaded areas.
L120@P3: Habitat: Hypnea musciformis is common on calm intertidal and shallow
L121@P3: subtidal reef flats, tidepools and on rocky intertidal benches. Most often
L122@P3: found low intertidal to shallow subtidal reef flats, attached to sandy flat
L123@P3: rocks. In bloom stage, may be found free-floating.
L124@P3: Ecology: H. musciformis was introduced to Kane‘ohe Bay in 1974 for
L125@P3: kappa-carageenan mariculture. The alga is often found as an epiphyte
L126@P3: on reef algae such as Sargassum spp. and Acanthophora spicifera. H.
L127@P3: musciformis forms large, odiferous unialgal mats, and during the winter
L128@P3: can represent 2/3 of the biomass of drift algae on windward and leeward
L129@P3: beaches on Maui. In peak blooms, thousands of pounds wash up on Maui
L130@P3: beaches. This species has been collected from all the main Hawaiian
L131@P3: Islands except Hawai‘i and Kaho‘olawe. Samples have also been collected from lobster traps in deep water off Maro Reef and Necker Island
L132@P3: in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands.
L133@P3: The success of this alga in Hawai‘i is likely due to a rapid growth rate,
L134@P3: ability to epiphytize other algae and easy fragmentation. Dispersal may
L135@P3: well have been enhanced via interisland travel on fouled boat hulls.
L136@P3: Soon after its introduction, it was identified as a food source for the
L137@P3: green sea turtle, Chelonia mydas. H. musciformis can make up a
L138@P3: signficant part the their diet, sometimes representing 99-100% of the
L139@P3: seaweed mass found in their stomachs.

L167@P3-4: 6 7Gracilaria salicornia Rhodophyta
L168@P4: Description: Plants brittle, cylindrical, 2-5 cm in diameter. Branching
L169@P4: irregular, often forked at tips and constricted at base of forks. Tips
L170@P4: bluntly rounded. Varies in color from a bright yellow at the tips to
L171@P4: orange or green and then even dark brown at the base.
L172@P4: Habitat: Gracilaria salicornia is found in tidepools, on reef flats, and
L173@P4: intertidal to subtidal 4 meters deep, attached to limestone and basalt
L174@P4: substrates. This alga grows in three-dimensional mats tightly adherent
L175@P4: to hard substrata up to 40 cm in thickness. In calm environments it
L176@P4: can also grow in an upright and more openly branching form.
L177@P4: Ecology: G. salicornia’s three dimensional growth form allows it to
L178@P4: grow over the top of other benthic organisms (native algae, corals and
L179@P4: other invertebrates), thus becoming particularly disruptive and
L180@P4: ecologically dominant in some habitats.
L181@P4: G. salicornia has been present for several decades inside and outside
L182@P4: the break wall and in Kapoho Bay in the Hilo area on the Big Island of
L183@P4: Hawai‘i; the origin of these populations is unknown. G. salicornia was
L184@P4: intentionally transported from Hawai‘i to Kane‘ohe Bay and Waikiki in
L185@P4: O‘ahu in the 1970’s and later to near Pukoo, Moloka‘i, where it was
L186@P4: planted in open reef cultures for experimental aquaculture and research. Evidence suggests that G. salicornia has significantly altered
L187@P4: benthic community structure and species diversity where it has spread
L188@P4: throughout much of Waikiki, and reports suggest that it is now common
L189@P4: on much of Molokai’s south shore from Kamalo to Kaunakakai.
