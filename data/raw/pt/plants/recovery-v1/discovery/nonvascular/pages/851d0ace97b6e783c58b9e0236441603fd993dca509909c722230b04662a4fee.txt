L6@P0: Abstract: The present study adds 19 species (15 Rhodophyta, one Chlorophyta and three Ochrophyta) to the
L7@P0: Azorean marine macroalgal flora, increasing the current
L8@P0: total of species recorded in this region to 521 (349 Rhodophyta, 76 Chlorophyta and 96 Ochrophyta), and showing
L9@P0: that this isolated island group supports a relatively rich
L10@P0: marine macroalgal flora. Some species fall within their
L11@P0: known overall distributional range, whereas other found
L12@P0: here represent a northern or southern extension to their
L13@P0: known distribution in the Atlantic. Three species (Antithamnionella elegans, Gymnophycus hapsiphorus and Scytosiphon dotyi) are probable introductions to the Azores,
L14@P0: whereas Melanothamnus pseudoforcipatus has an uncertain status. Six of the newly reported species were found as
L15@P0: components of the intertidal algal turf samples, which
L16@P0: justifies the need for continuing to examine turf samples as
L17@P0: new discoveries can be anticipated.
L18@P0: Keywords: algal turfs; biogeography; introduced species;
L19@P0: Macaronesia; range expansion.
L20@P0: 1 Introduction
L21@P0: The distribution pattern of species within and across ecosystems is determined by environmental factors (Clarke
L22@P0: 2003) and, in the case of marine macroalgae, their
L23@P0: biogeographical ranges are mainly determined by global
L24@P0: temperature gradients (Lüning 1990). Well-documented
L25@P0: changes in species abundance and distribution have
L26@P0: increased during recent years in response to the Earth’s
L27@P0: warming and other human-mediated shifts in ecosystem
L28@P0: structure (e.g. Barrientos et al. 2020; Lima et al. 2007;
L29@P0: Rindi et al. 2020). Regarding macroalgae, many nonindigenous species (NIS) have been found out of their
L30@P0: distributional range worldwide (Piñeiro-Corbeira et al.
L31@P0: 2020; Thomsen et al. 2016; Williams and Smith 2007) and
L32@P0: a tropicalization process has been described in temperate
L33@P0: regions associated with the arrival of warm-water species
L34@P0: in recent years (Osland et al. 2021; Ribeiro et al. 2019;
L35@P0: Verges et al. 2014). ´
L36@P0: The mid-Atlantic Ocean Azores archipelago (approximately −24°59′17″ to −31°17′42″ longitude and 36°53′2″ to
L37@P0: 39°44′13″ latitude, Figure 1), lying between European and
L38@P0: American coasts, has a significant role in present and past
L39@P0: maritime traffic connections (Cardigos et al. 2006). Nearly
L40@P0: 1200 km west of Europe, the nearest mainland, the archipelago is composed of nine islands, the Formigas islets and
L41@P0: nearby sublittoral banks and seamounts, which spread
L42@P0: over 500 km in a WNW–ESE direction and are organized in
L43@P0: three groups (eastern, central, and western). The tidal
L44@P0: range is small (<2 m, Hidrográfico 1981), and the coastline is
L45@P0: mainly composed of irregular compact, bedrock platforms,
L46@P0: alternating with boulder and cobble locations (Wallenstein
L47@P0: et al. 2009a). A deep-sea floor (500–1000 m depth) occurs
L48@P0: within about 5 km offshore (Neto et al. 2005).
L49@P0: The Azorean intertidal and shallow subtidal communities are primarily dominated by macroalgae. The intertidal mainly exhibits a mosaic and/or zoned distribution
L50@P0: pattern and has a predominance of algal turfs that cover
L51@P0: the rocks as a carpet (Wallenstein et al. 2009b). This type of
L52@P0: growth occurs in temperate and subtropical regions in

L86@P1: Despite the great distance of the archipelago from potential donor coastal areas in Europe, Africa and America,
L87@P1: endemism in the Azores is low, and a locally distinct algal
L88@P1: flora is absent (Neto et al. 2021a). Biogeographically, the
L89@P1: Azores marine macroalgal flora has a mixed nature, with
L90@P1: species shared with other Macaronesian archipelagos, North
L91@P1: Africa, the Mediterranean Sea, Atlantic Europe, and the
L92@P1: Americas (Tittley 2003; Tittley and Neto 1995, 2005, 2006;

L94@P1: second in species richness within the Macaronesian archipelagos after the Canary Islands and followed by Madeira and
L95@P1: Cabo Verde (Freitas et al. 2019). According to Freitas et al.
L96@P1: (2019), the Lusitanian biogeographical province should be
L97@P1: redefined in several ecoregions and the Azores should be
L98@P1: recognized as a biogeographical entity on its own, which they
L99@P1: named the Azores ecoregion, and separated from the new
L100@P1: Webbnesia ecoregion that comprises the archipelagos of
L101@P1: Madeira, the Selvages and the Canary Islands.

L103@P1: 180 years (Seubert and Hochstetter 1843). Initial taxonomic
L104@P1: and biogeographic studies in the area (e.g. Prud’homme van
L105@P1: Reine and van den Hoek 1990; Prud’homme van Reine 1998)
L106@P1: were followed by research on species biology, ecology, the
L107@P1: structure and functioning of intertidal and shallow subtidal
L108@P1: communities (Neto et al. 2021a) and the taxonomic revision
L109@P1: of herbarium material. Intensive fieldwork at locations not
L110@P1: previously studied resulted in the recent publication of a
L111@P1: series of checklists for different islands of the archipelago
L112@P1: (Neto et al. 2020a–f, 2021a–c, Table 1), which confirm that
L113@P1: the Azores host a relatively rich marine macroalgal flora.
L114@P1: Despite this richness, there remain some habitats and
L115@P1: locations that were not specifically investigated. Furthermore, the species diversity of algal turfs is still poorly understood. The present study aims to contribute to fill those
L116@P1: gaps contributing to a deeper knowledge of the Azorean
L117@P1: macroalgal flora.

L129@P1-2: 2 A.I. Neto et al.: New additions to the Azorean marine flora2 Materials and methods
L130@P2: The algae referred to in this paper were collected at the Azores archipelago (Figure 1) between 1993 and 2018 during field studies at
L131@P2: littoral and sublittoral levels on the islets of Formigas and the islands
L132@P2: of Santa Maria, São Miguel, Pico, and Graciosa. Intertidal collections
L133@P2: were made at low tide by walking along the shores. Subtidal collections
L134@P2: were made by scuba diving around the surveyed area. In each sampling
L135@P2: location, whenever an unknown or potentially unrecorded species was
L136@P2: found, it was collected manually by scraping and/or manually collecting
L137@P2: one or two specimens into previously labelled bags. Moreover, the diversity in algal turfs was studied in detail. The species found in the turfs
L138@P2: were morphologically identified and isolated using a dissecting microscope. Material collected was either fixed in 4–5% formalin seawater
L139@P2: (later transferred to Kew solution, following Bridsen and Forman 1999)
L140@P2: and/or pressed and dried. Part of the material of each species isolated
L141@P2: from algal turfs was desiccated in silica gel for molecular studies.
L142@P2: Voucher specimens were deposited at the Herbarium Ruy Telles Palhinha (AZB, herbarium of the University of the Azores), based at the Faculty of Sciences and Technology of the University of the Azores or the
L143@P2: Herbarium of the University of Santiago de Compostela (SANT).
L144@P2: For morphological and anatomical characterization, when
L145@P2: necessary, specimens on microscope slides were stained with aniline
L146@P2: blue and permanently mounted using ‘Karo’
L147@P2: ® corn syrup (Best Foods,
L148@P2: Englewood Cliffs, USA). The slides included complete specimens or
L149@P2: sections that were made by hand using a razor blade. Dried pressed
L150@P2: specimens from earlier collections were re-hydrated and prepared for
L151@P2: microscopic examination as above.
L152@P2: The whole specimens or slide preparations were observed with
L153@P2: the naked eye and using dissecting and compound microscope Zeiss
L154@P2: Axio Imager A1, to characterize the diagnosing structures described in
L155@P2: the literature and compare with descriptions from other locations.
L156@P2: Taxonomically relevant structures were measured using a calibrated
L157@P2: micrometre eyepiece. Cell and other microscopic structure measurements, and micrographs were taken using a digital camera (Olympus
L158@P2: model C5060, Japan) attached to light microscopes (Olympus model
L159@P2: BX50 F, Japan), and also the optical microscope Leica DM4 B with
L160@P2: Digital Camera Leica MC 190 HD (Leica, Germany). DNA sequences of
L161@P2: the rbcL gene of selected species were obtained and assembled
L162@P2: following methods specified in Díaz-Tapia et al. (2021) and were
L163@P2: deposited in GenBank (see Supplementary Table S1). Sequences were
L164@P2: used for molecularly assisted identification initially using BLAST in
L165@P2: GenBank (Altschul et al. 1990). Subsequently, newly determined and
L166@P2: previously published sequences of related taxa were aligned using
L167@P2: Geneious 7.0.6 and uncorrected p-distances were analysed.
