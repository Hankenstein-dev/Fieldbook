L4@P0: The physical background
L5@P0: The climate, landform and geology of Assynt have been
L6@P0: dealt with in the introduction to the vascular plant flora
L7@P0: but a short account of the features that particularly
L8@P0: affect bryophytes is necessary. Assynt has a large total
L9@P0: annual precipitation but more important for the
L10@P0: bryophytes, particularly those described as ‘oceanic’, is
L11@P0: the total number of wet days or conversely, the absence
L12@P0: of any prolonged period of drought. Residents in the
L13@P0: area will not be surprised that much of Assynt has in
L14@P0: excess of 200 wet days per year (Ratcliffe 1968), with a
L15@P0: ‘wet day’ defined as one with at least 1mm. of rain
L16@P0: falling. The ameliorating effect of the relatively warm
L17@P0: sea means that Assynt as a whole has fewer than 60
L18@P0: frosty days and fewer than 20 days with snow lying
L19@P0: (Page 1982), although these sea-level figures need to be
L20@P0: adjusted for the higher ground in the east of the parish.
L21@P0: To give an expression to this type of climate, an index
L22@P0: of ‘oceanicity’ (see Averis 1991 and Page 1982), can be
L23@P0: derived from the number of wet days per annum divided
L24@P0: by the annual temperature range (mean maximum July
L25@P0: temperature minus the mean minimum February
L26@P0: temperature). Plotting the areas with the highest index
L27@P0: gives a narrow band of ‘wet mildness’ (or ‘cool
L28@P0: wetness’) that extends down the coast of Highland
L29@P0: Scotland, including the Hebrides, and the extreme west
L30@P0: of Ireland, and which includes all of Assynt. This index
L31@P0: has proved a useful indicator of the distribution of
L32@P0: oceanic bryophyte species. This very wet climate has
L33@P0: also had a marked effect on bryophytes in another way,
L34@P0: through the formation of mire areas where mosses,
L35@P0: particularly Sphagnum, are often dominant.
L36@P0: Bryophytes are mostly small plants and do not compete
L37@P0: well with the larger flowering plants and ferns except
L38@P0: where conditions are favourable. The landforms of
L39@P0: Assynt with a preponderance of rocky slopes, crags,
L40@P0: boulders and ravines provide a wonderful assortment of
L41@P0: niches where bryophytes can become established and
L42@P0: form long-lived communities. The effects of glaciation
L43@P0: are everywhere apparent but particularly so on north and
L44@P0: east-facing slopes where the resultant crags and screes
L45@P0: provide an important habitat for the hepatic heath
L46@P0: described below. The run-off from the melting of the
L47@P0: glaciers with an increasingly mild and wet climate, over
L48@P0: a landscape largely devoid of vegetation and with
L49@P0: prodigious quantities of rock debris gave rise to the,
L50@P0: sometimes spectacular, ravines which seam the area.
L51@P0: These now form very important sites for bryophytes.
L52@P0: Another very recent landform with a distinctive
L53@P0: bryophyte community is wind-blown shell-sand which
L54@P0: has a patchy distribution from Achmelvich round to
L55@P0: Oldany.
L56@P0: The effects of the underlying rocks are as apparent for
L57@P0: the bryophytes as they are for the flowering plants,
L58@P0: though the physiological mechanism through which the
L59@P0: rock type operates to determine presence or absence is
L60@P0: even less well-understood. The large expanse of
L61@P0: limestone and the associated Fucoid Beds and the runoff from the calcareous areas have a nationally
L62@P0: important bryophyte flora with a number of species
L63@P0: limited to this zone. The sandstone is largely rather acid
L64@P0: and often has a limited flora although the gritty texture
L65@P0: of the rock is ideal, particularly for the smaller
L66@P0: liverworts.
L67@P0: As with the flowering plants, it is the complex
L68@P0: mineralogy of the gneiss that poses most of the
L69@P0: problems. Some facies of the gneiss are strongly baserich and have a number of ‘calcicole’ species while
L70@P0: others are absent (though frequent enough on the
L71@P0: limestone). Schoenus nigricans flushes on the gneiss
L72@P0: have a limited number of species, Scorpidium
L73@P0: scorpioides, Drepanocladus revolvens, Aneura pinguis
L74@P0: and Blindia acuta, but the expected Philonotis fontana,
L75@P0: Dicranella palustris, Jungermannia exertifolia ssp.
L76@P0: cordifolia and Bryum pseudotriquetrum are rarely seen.
L77@P0: Some large gneiss crags are almost completely devoid
L78@P0: of bryophytes except at the base and in some seepage
L79@P0: lines. The autecological studies necessary to provide an
L80@P0: explanation for these distribution patterns have not been
L81@P0: carried out. However it would seem likely that it is the
L82@P0: balance of a range of minerals including pyroxene,
L83@P0: hornblende and plagioclase and their breakdown
L84@P0: products that are critical, along with the depletion of
L85@P0: several trace elements in the gneiss in general
L86@P0: (Johnstone and Mykura 1989).
L87@P0: Assynt bryophytes in context
L88@P0: In order to put the bryophyte flora of Assynt into
L89@P0: context it seems sensible to discuss briefly the
L90@P0: importance of the Scottish Highlands as a whole.
L91@P0: Britain has an internationally important bryophyte flora;
L92@P0: we have approximately 70% of the European
L93@P0: bryophytes as compared to only 18% of the European
L94@P0: flowering plants. In general, the diversity of bryophytes
L95@P0: in Britain increases as you move north and west because

L97@P0: higher rainfall and less pollution. Within this diverse
L98@P0: bryophyte flora, the most important of the bryophyte

L102@P1: The climatological and geomorphological features
L103@P1: already described for Assynt are common to much of
L104@P1: western Scotland. The combination of equable
L105@P1: temperatures and consistently high humidity occurs only
L106@P1: in a very few areas of the globe, principally on
L107@P1: temperate oceanic margins and in high montane zones
L108@P1: closer to the equator. In Europe, this zone is limited to
L109@P1: the extreme western margins of the continent, where
L110@P1: very small areas occur on the coasts of France and Spain
L111@P1: and again in south-west Norway. The largest areas are
L112@P1: on the western margins of the British Isles, in a band
L113@P1: stretching from south-west Ireland to north-west
L114@P1: Scotland.
L115@P1: In addition to the climatic factors, buffering from
L116@P1: changes in humidity can also be enhanced by a
L117@P1: reasonably continuous tree canopy, deeply incised river
L118@P1: valleys and by very rocky terrain. The latter two
L119@P1: conditions are common in the west of Scotland as a
L120@P1: result of glacial and fluvio-glacial processes; the burns
L121@P1: tend to be steep and have ravine sections and there are
L122@P1: rock-falls and scree slopes, not least those associated
L123@P1: with the extensive raised-beaches around the coast.
L124@P1: Broadleaf tree cover was probably fairly continuous at
L125@P1: one time from Kintyre to Torridon and extensive in
L126@P1: favoured spots north of this, probably including much of
L127@P1: Assynt.
L128@P1: These various factors have given rise to distinctive and,
L129@P1: in global terms, very rare, moss and liverwort
L130@P1: communities. Several of the species involved have
L131@P1: their only European sites in the British Isles, while
L132@P1: many other species that are reasonably common here are
L133@P1: extremely rare elsewhere. Perhaps more important is
L134@P1: that, though there may be isolated records for most of
L135@P1: the species from elsewhere in Europe, it is principally in
L136@P1: the British Isles that there are extensive and distinct
L137@P1: communities containing these species.
L138@P1: The west coast of Scotland has the largest area of this
L139@P1: kind of habitat in Britain and has the largest number of
L140@P1: typical oceanic species as well as many of the most
L141@P1: important populations. The available habitat in Assynt,
L142@P1: as in much of Scotland, has been much reduced in the
L143@P1: last few millennia as most of the species, as well as
L144@P1: requiring an oceanic climate, require the further
L145@P1: buffering from changes in temperature and humidity
L146@P1: provided by a broadleaf tree cover. As the area under
L147@P1: permanent broadleaf tree cover has decreased, so the
L148@P1: populations of the oceanic species have become
L149@P1: increasingly fragmented. This means that any seminatural woodland with populations of oceanic bryophyte
L150@P1: species is scientifically important, both nationally and
L151@P1: internationally. A much more detailed analysis of the
L152@P1: processes giving rise to this rich woodland flora is given
L153@P1: by Averis (1991) and Hodgetts (1993) has a good
L154@P1: account of the species involved.
L155@P1: These woodland and ravine communities of bryophytes
L156@P1: have a better representation further south than Assynt,
L157@P1: with woodlands of larger size, a greater diversity of
L158@P1: species and more rarities. However, some sites in
L159@P1: Assynt are extremely rich in oceanic species and are of
L160@P1: international importance. Outside of the woodlands,
L161@P1: there is one oceanic community that has some of its best
L162@P1: Scottish sites in Assynt and in similar areas in West
L163@P1: Ross just to the south. This is the liverwort community
L164@P1: under ericaceous shrubs which has been variously
L165@P1: described as ‘oceanic’ heath, ‘oceanic-montane’ heath,
L166@P1: liverwort-rich heath or the ‘mixed Northern Atlantic
L167@P1: hepatic mat’ (Ratcliffe 1968). This community is
L168@P1: described more fully below but, essentially, it is a
L169@P1: community of rocky slopes with a north or northeasterly aspect, consisting of Calluna vulgaris or, at
L170@P1: higher altitudes, Vaccinium myrtillus, and with a
L171@P1: bryophyte layer below which includes a number of
L172@P1: large, leafy liverworts with an extremely restricted and
L173@P1: disjunct global distribution. The best sites for this
L174@P1: community, in the bigger hills in the east of Assynt, are,
L175@P1: again, of international importance.

L178@P1: The object of this section is to describe the typical
