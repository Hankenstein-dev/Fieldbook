L344: Racomitrium elongatum is a acrocarpous moss of the family Grimmiaceae, forming loose, straggling, greyish-green tufts on sandy and gravelly acidic substrates typically 3-10 cm tall in sandy heathland, gravelly soil, stabilized dunes, and thin acidic soil over rock. It is notable for elongate, straggling growth habit and short, denticulate hair-points; a pioneer on sandy acidic ground.
L345: This species plays an important ecological role in its native habitats and is of interest to bryologists for its distinctive morphology and ecology.

L383: 
L384: Tightly adhering to rock surfaces. Often forming cushions or crusts. Leaves frequently with hair points (awns). Tolerant of exposure. Key genera: Grimmia, Schistidium, Andreaea, Racomitrium.
L385: ## Cultivation Guide
L386: To cultivate Racomitrium elongatum, prepare a substrate matching its natural habitat: acidic organic substrate such as peat-sand mix or leaf mold (pH 4.0-5.5). Provide partial to full shade and consistent moisture.. Water only with rainwater or distilled water to prevent mineral buildup. Temperature range matches the species' natural distribution. Establish from divisions or fragments pressed onto prepared substrate and kept consistently moist for the first 4-8 weeks until attachment occurs.
L387: ### Group Cultivation Guide — Rock Moss
L388: 
L389: Plant on rock surfaces, stone walls, or gravel. Many tolerate drought and sun better than other mosses. Acidic to neutral substrate. Buttermilk or yoghurt slurry helps establishment on stone. Slow growing but very persistent.
L390: ## Climate & Seasons
L391: Racomitrium elongatum exhibits notable desiccation tolerance, surviving complete water loss for weeks to months and resuming metabolic activity within minutes to hours of rehydration. This poikilohydric strategy -- allowing tissue water content to equilibrate with the environment -- is a fundamental bryophyte adaptation. UV-protective compounds (flavonoids, melanins) accumulate in exposed habitats, protecting chloroplasts from photodamage.
L392: Cold tolerance extends to approximately -15 to -25°C, typical of its temperate distribution. Metabolic recovery after stress follows predictable patterns: photosynthesis recovers within minutes of optimal conditions returning, while growth resumes over days to weeks.

L395: ### Seasonal Changes
L396: 
L397: Spring brings fresh growth to Racomitrium elongatum, with new shoots and branch extensions emerging from March to May in temperate climates. Sporophytes are infrequently produced due to dioicous sexuality, with separate male and female plants requiring proximity for fertilization. Desiccation-induced dormancy occurs during dry periods, with rapid recovery upon rehydration. Leaf color may deepen from bright green to darker tones as the growing season progresses.

L405: ## Propagation
L406: Division of established colonies is the most reliable propagation method for Racomitrium elongatum. Separate portions in spring and press firmly onto prepared substrate matching the species' requirements. Stem fragments of 1-3 cm can regenerate new shoots within 4-8 weeks if kept consistently moist. Fragmentation is a natural propagation strategy for this species. Spore culture: surface-sow fresh spores on sterile agar or mineral substrate (pH 4.0-5.5) under bright indirect light at 15-20°C.
L407: Protonema develops in 2-4 weeks; gametophores emerge in 2-6 months. Maintain high humidity (>80%) throughout.
L408: ### Spore Reproduction
L409: Racomitrium elongatum is dioicous -- male and female gametophytes are separate, requiring water for sperm transport between individuals. Sporophyte frequency varies -- in some populations sporophytes are rare, with vegetative reproduction predominating. The sporophyte consists of a foot embedded in the gametophyte, a seta elevating the capsule for spore dispersal, and a capsule with arthrodontous peristome.
L410: Spores (10-14 micrometers) germinate to produce a protonema that develops into the mature gametophyte.
L411: ## Problems & Pests
L412: ### Common Problems
L413: Overwatering can paradoxically be a problem for this drought-adapted species -- ensure some drying between waterings. Alkaline water (hard tap water) causes chlorosis, browning, and death -- use rainwater or reverse-osmosis water exclusively. Algal overgrowth (green films or cyanobacteria) can smother colonies in over-lit or nutrient-enriched conditions.
