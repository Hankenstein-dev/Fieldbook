L6@P0: Abstract
L7@P0: Background: Terrestrial, freshwater and marine green algae constitute the large and morphologically diverse phylum
L8@P0: of Chlorophyta, which gave rise to the core chlorophytes. Chlorophyta are abundant and diverse in freshwater environments where sometimes they form nuisance blooms under eutrophication conditions. The phylogenetic relationships among core chlorophyte clades (Chlorodendrophyceae, Ulvophyceae, Trebouxiophyceae and Chlorophyceae),
L9@P0: are of particular interest as it is a species-rich phylum with ecological importance worldwide, but are still poorly
L10@P0: understood. In the Mediterranean ecoregion, data on molecular characterization of eukaryotic microalgae strains are
L11@P0: limited and current knowledge is based on ecological studies of natural populations. In the present study we report
L12@P0: the isolation and characterization of 11 green microalgae strains from Greece contributing more information for the
L13@P0: taxonomy of Chlorophyta. The study combined morphological and molecular data.
L14@P0: Results: Phylogenetic analysis based on 18S rRNA, internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region and the large subunit of
L15@P0: the ribulose-bisphosphate carboxylase (rbcL) gene revealed eight taxa. Eleven green algae strains were classified in
L16@P0: four orders (Sphaeropleales, Chlorellales, Chlamydomonadales and Chaetophorales) and were represented by four
L17@P0: genera; one strain was not assigned to any genus. Most strains (six) were classified to the genus Desmodesmus, two
L18@P0: strains to genus Chlorella, one to genus Spongiosarcinopsis and one filamentous strain to genus Uronema. One strain is
L19@P0: placed in a separate independent branch within the Chlamydomonadales and deserves further research.
L20@P0: Conclusions: Our study reports, for the first time, the presence of Uronema in an aquatic environment up to 40 °C
L21@P0: and reveals new diversity within the Chlamydomonadales. The results from the ITS region and the rbcL gene corroborated those obtained from 18S rRNA without providing further information or resolving the phylogenetic relationships
L22@P0: within certain genera, due to the limited number of ITS and rbcL sequences available. The comparison of molecular
L23@P0: and morphological data showed that they were congruent. Cosmopolitan genera with high worldwide distribution
L24@P0: inhabit Greek freshwaters.

L32@P0: Background
L33@P0: All green algae and embryophyte plants belong to Viridiplantae which were divided early into two evolutionary
L34@P0: discrete lineages: the Chlorophyta and Streptophyta [1].
L35@P0: Chlorophyta are an ancient, morphologically and ecologically diverse lineage that include three major classes:
L36@P0: Ulvophyceae, Trebouxiophyceae, Chlorophyceae (UTC)
L37@P0: [2] and the majority of described species of green algae
L38@P0: [1]. Approximately 8000 Chlorophyta species have been
L39@P0: described, whilst it is estimated that at least 5000 species still remain undescribed [3]. Despite the fact that the
L40@P0: diversity of Chlorophyta is being studied for a long time,
L41@P0: our knowledge for their taxonomic and phylogenetic
L42@P0: relationships is still deficient [4]. Microscopic green algae
L43@P0: are mainly identified based on specific morphological
L44@P0: traits (general shape of the cells, position of chloroplasts,
L45@P0: presence of pyrenoids, type of reproduction, colony formation, flagella, ultrastructural characteristics etc.) [1].

L52@P1: Nevertheless, morphological identification of microalgae
L53@P1: can be very tough due to the absence of obvious structural features in most species and the high degree of variability of several of the observable characteristics within
L54@P1: species [5, 6]. The morphological species concept is very
L55@P1: subjective, thus the phylogenetic species concept or diagnostic concept [7] has gained a lot of ground [8–10].
L56@P1: As summarized by Krienitz and Bock [11] higher taxonomic lineages such as divisions, classes and orders have
L57@P1: been totally revised since molecular phylogenetic methods were introduced into the taxonomy of green algae.
L58@P1: Molecular phylogenetic evidence has provided a substantially improved understanding of the relationships among
L59@P1: major lineages.
L60@P1: Molecular diversity and phylogeny of isolated members
L61@P1: of Chlorophyta derived from Mediterranean freshwaters is a relatively unexplored area; most of the existing
L62@P1: studies investigate the ecological status of freshwaters,
L63@P1: including morphological identification of microalgae
L64@P1: [12–14]. Data on isolation and combined morphological
L65@P1: and molecular characterization of Chlorophyta strains
L66@P1: are limited and mainly focused on their biotechnological potential [15, 16]. Greece is probably the most diverse
L67@P1: Mediterranean country with an excessive level of diversity and endemism of species [17] and it is not rare that
L68@P1: new taxa are discovered in animal [18] and microalgal
L69@P1: phyla [19, 20]. However, the phylogenetic relationships of
L70@P1: Chlorophyta in Greece are barely known as the available
L71@P1: information is derived almost exclusively from morphological and/or ecological studies of natural populations
L72@P1: [21, 22]. This study investigates the diversity of green
L73@P1: algae (Chlorophyta) strains isolated from freshwaters in
L74@P1: Greece using a polyphasic approach including morphology, 18S rRNA, ITS, and rbcL phylogeny.
L75@P1: Results
L76@P1: Eleven strains were isolated from surface water samples
L77@P1: collected from six lakes (Doirani, Karla, Kastoria, Koronia, Pamvotis, and Volvi) and one hot spring (Agkistro)
L78@P1: located in Greece (Table 1). Three morphotypes of Chlorophyta were identified using light microscopy; six strains
L79@P1: corresponded to Desmodesmus-like species, four strains
L80@P1: were coccoid, and one filamentous (Figs. 1, 2). According
L81@P1: to morphology (Table 2) and phylogenetic analyses of 18S
L82@P1: rRNA (Fig. 3), the 18S–28S ITS region (Fig. 4) and rbcL
L83@P1: gene (Fig. 5), the isolated strains were placed into clades
L84@P1: within the Chlorophyceae and Trebouxiophyceae.
L85@P1: Based on 18S rRNA phylogeny (Fig. 3), the strains TAUMAC 0810, 2810, 3110 and 3410 were clustered together
L86@P1: into the Sphaeropleales group, within Desmodesmus;
L87@P1: strains 2810 and 3410 were placed within D. subspicatus and D. communis, respectively, whereas the strains
L88@P1: 0810, 3110 were clearly positioned within a separate
L89@P1: Desmodesmus subclade comprised of D. abundans. In
L90@P1: the order Chaetophorales, strain TAU-MAC 0215 was
L91@P1: placed into the Uronema clade. Strains TAU-MAC 3510,
L92@P1: 3310 and their allied taxa form a separate independent
L93@P1: branch within the Chlamydomonadales; 3510 formed a
L94@P1: subcluster with a Chlorococcum strain and 3310 formed
L95@P1: a well-supported (0.99 posterior probability) clade which
L96@P1: contains a total of four OTUs: the studied strain, two
L97@P1: isolates characterized as “Chlamydomonad sp.” (≥99.9%
L98@P1: similarity) and one strain of the new genus and species
L99@P1: Spongiosarcinopsis terrestris. A direct comparison of
L100@P1: sequence similarities among the strains 1110, 3210 and
L101@P1: Chlorella vulgaris showed a 99% sequence identity. Nevertheless they formed a distinct subclade inside Chlorella
L102@P1: vulgaris clade (Fig.  3). The well-supported relationships
