L144: ## Abstract
L145: The carrageenophyte red alga Sarcothalia radula (Gigartinaceae), referred to under its earlier name Gigartina radula, is one of eight species considered a safe source of the refined hydrocolloid and food additive carrageenan and is also reported to be a source of compounds of interest in a variety of other commercial applications.
L146: While the latter name has been applied to carrageenophyte red algae from many parts of the Southern Hemisphere as well as in the northeast Pacific, the identity of the type material and the type locality have remained unclear. The location of the type specimen of Fucus radula could not be found. We discuss available information about the probable collection location and present arguments for the collection to have been made during Cook's second voyage to southern New Zealand.
L147: Both a lectotype and epitype are designated.
L148: ## INTRODUCTION
L149: The red algal species Sarcothalia radula (Esper) Edyvane & Womersley is a member of the Gigartinaceae. Referred to under the earlier name Gigartina radula (Esper) J.Agardh, it is one of eight species currently listed by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as being a safe source of the refined hydrocolloid and food additive carrageenan (C.F.R., cite21†2022 ), and is also reported to be a source of compounds of interest in a variety of other commercial applications (e.g., Ortiz-Viedma & al., cite22†2021 ).
L150: While the latter name has been applied to carrageenophyte red algae from many parts of the Southern Hemisphere as well as in the northeast Pacific, the identity of the type material and the type locality have remained unclear.
L151: Fucus radula Esper was one of the first members of the Gigartinaceae to be described from the Southern Hemisphere (Esper, cite23†1802 : 3, pl. CXIII). Accompanying his description, Esper stated “This recently discovered seaweed, departing so much from the usual in its form, has been brought from New Holland and has been supplied by Mr D. Turner under the above name” (translation, Parkinson, cite24†1981 : 21). Turner (cite25†1807–1808 : 54) placed Fucus radula into synonymy with F. bracteatus S.G.Gmel.
L152: stating “It is with great regret that, on account of its prior date, I have been forced to substitute Gmelin's unmeaning name of F. bracteatus for that of F. Radula, which is given to the present plant in the Banksian Herbarium, and is most happy both as to look and touch.” Turner provided the following distribution for F. bracteatus – “At the Cape of Good Hope. Mr. Robertson.—On the western shores of North America. Mr. Menzies.” This synonymy has not been followed by subsequent authors.
L153: The species Gigartina bracteata (S.G.Gmel.) Setch. & N.L.Gardner (Setchell & Gardner, cite26†1933 : 258, 295), is now considered to be restricted to the western Cape Province, South Africa (Hughey & al., cite27†2002 ; Guiry, cite28†2015 ). It is a large, bladed species in which tetrasporangial thalli become reticulate when mature and differs from Esper's concept of Fucus radula.
L154: After the description of Fucus radula in 1802, this species was transferred to Sphaerococcus (Agardh, cite29†1822 ), Iridaea (Bory de Saint-Vincent, cite30†1828 ), Mastocarpus (Kützing, cite31†1843 ), Gigartina (Agardh, cite32†1851 ), Mammillaria (Kuntze, cite33†1891 ), and most recently to Sarcothalia (Edyvane & Womersley, cite34†1994 ). In addition to changing perspectives on the generic placement of this species, the application of the name has also varied, as well as the understanding of its geographic distribution.
L155: Bory de Saint-Vincent's treatment (cite30†1828 ) merged a South American species (as Iridaea papillosa) with Fucus radula, thus broadening the concept of its geographic distribution. Although Esper gave the type locality of this species as Neuholland [Australia], in 1851 when J.
L156: Agardh transferred this species to Gigartina he included a range of growth forms from a wide geographic distribution (South Africa, New Zealand including the New Zealand subantarctic Auckland and Campbell islands, and the Pacific coast of North America). In 1876, J. Agardh restricted the application of Gigartina radula to South Africa (Agardh, cite35†1876 ; Hughey & al., cite27†2002 ), and described a new species, G. circumcincta, for material from New Zealand that had been previously assigned to G. radula.
L157: Womersley (cite36†1959 ) stated that the type of Gigartina radula had been collected by Robert Brown in Australia and then passed on to Esper via Dawson.
L158: However, Parkinson (cite24†1981 ) considered that the collection was probably made on Cook's first voyage to New Zealand and Australia (1768–1771), and that the locality “Neu Holland” was an error because the parts of the east coast of Australia visited by Cook (from Sydney, New South Wales and northwards), “do not include raduliform Gigartinas in their marine flora” (Parkinson, cite24†1981 : 22).
L159: Subsequently, Edyvane & Womersley (cite34†1994 : 293) transferred this species to Sarcothalia and stated the type locality to be “Australia” and the type specimen “Herb. Esper, ER?”. Although also summarising Parkinson's view that the labelling “Neuholland” was an error, they stated “the type specimen cannot be excluded from Australia”.
L160: Esper's description of Fucus radula was accompanied by an excellent illustration of a large, bladed thallus with papillae (Esper, cite23†1802 : pl. CXIII) (Fig. cite37†1 ). Esper's collections were purchased by the University of Erlangen in 1802 (Silva, cite38†1953 ). The location of the type specimen of F. radula, however, is unknown. It had not been located by Edyvane & Womersley (cite34†1994 ), and Hughey & al.
L161: (cite27†2002 ) referred to an extensive search of Esper's herbarium at Erlangen (ER) that did not locate a specimen matching Esper's description. Our recent enquiries have also not yielded any information about type material of this taxon in ER.

L167: We have not been able to find any additional evidence that early European collections of Gigartina sensu lato from southern Australia were sent by Turner to Esper. In our view, the most likely origin of the material that Esper received from Turner was from southern New Zealand. There is previous evidence of mislabelling of New Zealand material as originating from Australia, and specifically in relation to material described by Esper.
L168: The brown algal species Carpophyllum flexuosum was also described by Esper (cite23†1802 : 43, pl. CXXXI, as Fucus flexuosus) as being from Neuholland. While there is no indication of the collector or the date of collection given in Esper's account, this species is a member of a genus that is endemic to New Zealand (Silva, cite38†1953 ; Adams, cite40†1994 ). This material was also communicated by Turner to Esper (Turner, cite41†1819 ; Silva, cite38†1953 ).
L169: Based on historical accounts, specifically the diaries and records of Cook's voyages, it is known that Cook did not visit any southern New Zealand sites on his first voyage to New Zealand (1768–1771) (Beaglehole, cite42†1961 ; Begg & Begg, cite43†1975 ). Collections were made in the northeastern North Island (Bay of Islands) and also in the Marlborough Sounds (northern South Island).
L170: However, on his first voyage, Cook did discover and name part of the southwestern South Island in the Fiordland region – Dusky Bay (now Dusky Sound), and the nearby Five Fingers Peninsula. He was unable to enter the fiord or to land but recorded details of the area and returned on his second voyage in 1773 in the vessel Resolution.
L171: The Resolution made initial landfall in Chalky Inlet, and then the vessel moved north to Dusky Sound where they spent about seven weeks in the vicinity of Pickersgill Harbour (Beaglehole, cite42†1961 ; Begg & Begg, cite43†1975 ).
L172: On Cook's second voyage to New Zealand some algae were collected (Forster, cite44†1786 ) but it is not documented where these came from. Wynne (cite45†2002 ) clarified the status of Fucus cirrhosus Turner (= Plocamium cirrhosum (Turner) M.J.Wynne), a species based on material collected in “Dusky Bay” in 1773 on Cook's second voyage. The type specimen of P.
L173: cirrhosum has errors in both the year of collection and the name of the collector, suggesting that these details were added to the specimen sheet after the voyage. While there is no documentation from the voyage indicating whether either Carpophyllum flexuosum or Fucus radula were amongst the collections from Dusky Sound, both C. flexuosum and the species that we consider agree with the description of F. radula are present in Fiordland.
L174: The material illustrated by Esper (cite23†1802 : pl. CXIII) is very similar in morphology to modern material from Fiordland that is currently identified as Sarcothalia lanceata (J.Agardh) Hommers. (Agardh, cite46†1899 ; Hommersand & al., cite47†1993 ). In his account of Fucus radula, Esper commented on the application of the epithet radula, and that it was referencing the hooked and often branched papillae or proliferations found on the edges and surfaces of the blades.
L175: Such proliferations are also a feature of S. lanceata. While some S. lanceata blades are narrow and strap-like, befitting the name, surveys of material from around the South Island, Stewart Island and the New Zealand subantarctic islands have shown that this species is variable in its morphology. Esper refers to the flattened and narrow base of the thalli and the production of “narrow lancet shaped leaves” (trans. in Parkinson, cite24†1981 ), as well as the thickness of the thalli when rehydrated.
L176: These are also features found in S. lanceata. As noted by Adams (cite40†1994 ), thalli of S. lanceata are always robust and some become extremely thick when mature.
L177: In addition to Sarcothalia lanceata, there are two other species of large, bladed Gigartinaceae commonly reported from New Zealand – Sarcothalia (‘Gigartina’) atropurpurea (J.Agardh) Hommers. (Iridaea atropurpurea J.Agardh) and Sarcothalia circumcincta (J.Agardh) Hommers. (Gigartina circumcincta J.Agardh) (Agardh, cite35†1876 , cite48†1885 ; Hommersand & al., cite47†1993 ).
L178: Parkinson (cite24†1981 ) associated Gigartina radula with the later-named Sarcothalia circumcincta, a species that is now understood to be found in the southern South Island, Stewart Island and the New Zealand subantarctic. It has a more restricted ecological distribution than S. lanceata and is much less commonly found. To date no specimens of this species have been confirmed from Fiordland.
L179: The large, bladed member of the Gigartinaceae treated by Edyvane & Womersley (cite34†1994 ) as Sarcothalia radula has been shown in phylogenetic analyses to belong to the same species as Sarcothalia (‘Gigartina’) atropurpurea (Hommersand & al., cite49†1999 ). Nelson & Broom (cite50†2008 ) established that the New Zealand endemic species Gigartina grandifida J.Agardh and Sarcothalia (‘Gigartina’) atropurpurea from New Zealand (S.
L180: radula sensu Edyvane & Womersley), belong to a separate and as yet undescribed genus: they are neither Gigartina nor Sarcothalia. In making the transfer from Gigartina to Sarcothalia, Edyvane and Womersley noted that unlike the other Australian Sarcothalia species, “S. radula appears distinctive in possessing a diffuse enveloping tissue without transverse gonimoblast filaments”, a character of the yet to be described genus.
L181: In this paper we present molecular sequence data from Gigartinaceae from New Zealand and Australia, designate both a lectotype and an epitype for Sarcothalia radula and present an emended synonymy for this species.
L182: ## MATERIALS AND METHODS
L183:

Fresh material was pressed as herbarium vouchers and subsamples were dried in silica gel for molecular analyses.

L201: DNA of subsamples was extracted following a modified CTAB protocol (Zuccarello & Lokhorst, cite53†2005 ). The forward F-8 and F-765 and the reverse F-753 and R-rbcS start primers were used to amplify rbcL either in one or two parts (Freshwater & Rueness, cite54†1994 ; Wang & al., cite55†2020 ). Amplification reactions and PCR conditions with an annealing temperature of 45°C were performed as described in (Preuss & al., cite56†2022 ).
L202: Successful amplifications identified through gel electrophoresis were purified using ExoSAP-IT (USB, Cleveland, Ohio, U.S.A.) and both directions commercially sequenced (Macrogen, Seoul, Korea).
L203: Sequences were assembled and manually edited in Geneious Prime 2021.2.2 (cite57†https://www.geneious.com/†www.geneious.com ). GenBank sequences covering the diversity within the Gigartinaceae (Appendix cite52†1 ) were combined with the newly edited sequences using MAFFT (v.7) alignments (Katoh & Standley, cite58†2013 ). Mastocarpus spp. were designated as outgroup taxa.
L204: Phylogenetic tree reconstruction was performed using 1000 replicates of non-parametric bootstrap (Felsenstein, cite59†1985 ), ultrafast bootstrap (Hoang & al., cite60†2018 ) and SH-aLRT (Guindon & al., cite61†2010 ) in IQ-TREE v.1.6.12 (Nguyen & al., cite62†2015 ) on the Rāpoi High-Performance Computing Facility of Victoria University of Wellington.
L205: Best-fitting models for 1st codon (TN + F + I + G4), 2nd codon (K3Pu + F + I + G4) and 3rd codon (K3Pu + F + I + G4) positions were determined using Bayesian information criterion in ModelFinder v.1.4.2 (Kalyaanamoorthy & al., cite63†2017 ). All sequences were submitted to GenBank (OP086042–OP086055).
L206: ## RESULTS
L207: The 1392 bp alignment (suppl. Appendix cite64†S1 ) contained both full-length and partial rbcL sequences of 13 specimens sequenced in this study (Table cite51†1 ). The maximum likelihood phylogeny showed that our sequence data were resolved in three clearly distinct clades.
L208: Specimens matching morphological features of Esper's description of Fucus radula from southern New Zealand and a GenBank sequence labelled as Sarcothalia lanceata (DQ104831) were resolved in a strongly supported monophyletic clade and within Sarcothalia with strong support in all three analyses (Fig. cite65†2 ). Intraspecific variation between specimens of S. radula was between 0% and 1.0% with the exception of the two partial sequences (OP806048, OP806049) that were slightly higher with variation of 1.8%.
L209: A South African specimen identified as Gigartina ‘radula’ (U03431) is genetically distinct from specimens grouping as Sarcothalia radula from New Zealand. In addition, there is strong phylogenetic support in all three analyses for a grouping of specimens identified as S. circumcincta from southern New Zealand and S. circumcincta AF146219, and for the monophyly of sequences attributed to ‘Gigartina’ atropurpurea from Australia and New Zealand.

L214: ## DISCUSSION
L215: Clarification of the type of Fucus radula is long overdue. Our molecular analyses confirmed that a species of Sarcothalia from southern New Zealand includes specimens that conform to Esper's description of F. radula, and that the name in current use for this species is the later-described Sarcothalia lanceata. Attempts to locate the material that Esper had available at the time of the description of this species have not been successful. In accordance with Art.
L216: 9.3 and 9.11 of the ICN (Shenzhen Code, Turland & al., cite67†2018 ), we designate the illustration published by Esper as lectotype (Fig. cite37†1 ). The illustration is selected as there is no extant specimen whose status as original material is unambiguous. While the illustration provided by Esper (cite23†1802 ) provides an excellent impression of the species, this illustration is taxonomically ambiguous.

L228: ## CONCLUSION
L229: The combination Sarcothalia radula, made by Edyvane & Womersley (cite34†1994 ), was based on material that differs from the concept of Esper (cite23†1802 ). The combination is correctly applied to the species previously referred to as S. lanceata. Sarcothalia radula is found on the west and east coasts of the South, Chatham, and Stewart Islands, and on the New Zealand subantarctic islands, growing in the low intertidal and upper subtidal zones, on rock on exposed coasts.
L230: While a range of morphologies can be found, this species is characterised as having a narrow wedge-shaped base, with blades that are occasionally forked, with papillae on the blade surface and edges of female and tetrasporangial plants. Thalli are rich, dark red to purple-red in colour with a fleshy and tough texture.
L231: Cystocarps, which are large and protuberant, are produced on both surfaces of the blade (but not in the same area), male thalli are paler and thinner than female or tetrasporangial plants, and usually without papillae, and in tetrasporophytes the tetrasporangia are scattered over blades in rounded sori.
