L16:     Back from the grave: Tom Blockeel and
L17:     Sam Bosanquet report on the resurrection
L18:     of a neglected moss
L19:     edwigia striata was first recognised as
L20:     a distinct moss by William Wilson
L21:     from a collection made at Llyn Idwal,
L22:     Snowdonia, in April 1829. It was originally
L23:     published under the name Anictangium striatum
L24:     in Hooker (1833). Hooker notes that "Mr
L25:     Wilson considers the difference in the leaves of
L26:     this plant, as sufficient alone to keep it distinct
L27:     from the preceding [= A. ciliatum = Hedwigia
L28:     ciliata]". These distinguishing characters
L29:     included the obscurely striate leaves and the
L30:     margins recurved in the upper as well as the lower
L31:     part of the leaf. In Bryologia Britannica, Wilson
L32:     (1855) subsequently demoted it to the status of
L33:     variety, following Bruch & Schimper's treatment
L34:     in Bryologia Europaea (Bruch, Schimper &
L35:     Gümbel, 1846), but remained convinced that
L36:     10
L37:     FieldBryology No115 | May16
L38:     it was a good species ("the whole aspect of the
L39:     plant is in favour of it being considered a distinct
L40:     species"). However this comment is surely
L41:     coloured by the fact that H. stellata is much the
L42:     commonest Hedwigia in Britain, whereas the true
L43:     H. ciliata is rare. Most subsequent authors, up to
L44:     and including Dixon (1924) treated Hedwigia
L45:     striata as a variety of H. ciliata, but some field
L46:     bryologists remained convinced that it was a
L47:     good species. A documented example of this is
L48:     from a meeting of the Manchester Cryptogamic
L49:     Society on 11 August 1879 (Anon, 1879a):
L50:     "The mosses exhibited by the president [John
L51:     Whitehead] were Hedwigia striata, Wils, from
L52:     Grasmere, which he, along with Mr. Fergusson,
L53:     proposed to restore again to the rank of a distinct
L54:     species". This meeting report is important for
L55:     the authorship of the name Hedwigia striata and
L56:     is discussed further in the nomenclatural note
L57:     below.
L58:     Collectors continued to name specimens as
L59:     Hedwigia ciliata var. striata up to the early part of
L60:     the 20th century, and such specimens are found
L61:     in many herbaria. The variety was reported from
L62:     eight vice-counties in Britain and Ireland in the
L63:     first and second editions of the Census Catalogue
L64:     of British mosses (Ingham, 1907; Duncan 1926).
L65:     Thereafter it became neglected and was omitted
L66:     from subsequent floras and check-lists. Hedenäs
L67:     (1994) was the first botanist in recent years to
L68:     recognise greater diversity within Hedwigia
L69:     ciliata. In revising the genus for Sweden he
L70:     described H. stellata as a new species, but
L71:     included H. striata as a synonym of H. ciliata.
L72:     Crundwell (1995) revised British and Irish
L73:     Hedwigia using Hedenäs' criteria, recognising
L74:     three taxa within the H. ciliata group: H.
L75:     stellata, H. ciliata var. ciliata and H. ciliata var.
L76:     leucophaea. A more recent study by Buchbender
L77:     et al. (2014), incorporating molecular analysis,
L78:     has reinstated H. striata as a distinct species.
L79:     Hespanhol et al. (2013) and Gallego et al. (2014)
L80:     have documented its occurrence in Portugal and
L81:     Spain.
L82:     Buchbender et al. (2014) cite a number of
L83:     historical collections of H. striata from North
L84:     Wales, the Lake District and Scotland, all
L85:     collected in the 19th century. In June 2015
L86:     during the B.B.S. summer meeting an extant
L87:     population of H. striata was found in Snowdonia
L88:     at Ffynnon Lloer, Caernarvonshire (Bosanquet,
L89:     2016); in fact it had already been collected
L90:     there by R.G. Woods in 2010 but named H.
L91:     ciliata, and similarly at another site, Llyn Gafr,
L92:     Merionethshire, by SDSB in 2013. In November
L93:     2015 TLB visited the Grasmere area in Cumbria
L94:     and succeeded in refinding a small population
L95:     of H. striata at Easedale Tarn. Examination of
L96:     herbarium material has revealed a further three
L97:     vice-counties (West Ross, West Sutherland and
L98:     Co Waterford) in which H. striata has been
L99:     found since 2000. In this note we describe
L100:     the differentiating characters of H. striata and
L101:     document the known British and Irish records.
L102:     A full description of the species can be found in
L103:     the paper by Buchbender et al. (2014).
L104:     Identification
L105:     Hedwigia striata resembles H. ciliata and differs
L106:     from H. stellata in having multiple (1-4) papillae
L107:     on the upper and median leaf cells. The hairpoints are often short, and when well-developed
L108:     they tend to remain erect when dry, rather than
L109:     spreading or reflexed as in H. stellata. The epithet
L110:     striata refers to the leaves; sulcate would perhaps
L111:     be a better word to describe them. The leaves
L112:     are furrowed, in the manner of a corrugated
L113:     asbestos or tin roofing sheet (the furrows are
L114:     oriented from base to apex) (Fig. 1). The furrows
L115:     are typically broad and shallow, and sometimes
L116:     indistinct. They constitute a useful field character
L117:     for H. striata, but are not absolutely diagnostic.
L118:     We have seen material of H. ciliata in which
L119:     a few weak furrows are visible on some leaves,
L120:     although they are absent from the majority of
L121:     H. ciliata specimens that we have studied. Other
L122:     characters, therefore, must be used to confirm
L123:     the identification.
L124:     The most reliable diagnostic character is
L125:     provided by the cilia of mature perichaetial
L126:     leaves. In H. stellata and H. ciliata, the cilia are
L127:     numerous, long and contorted, often abruptly
L128:     bent or curled and sometimes bear lateral
L129:     projections (Fig. 2). In H. striata the cilia are
L130:     fewer, shorter and more or less straight (Fig. 3).
L131:     This does not mean that they are particularly

L150:     Other characters are less constant.
L151:     The leaf margins of H. striata are usually
L152:     recurved along the length of the leaf to
L153:     the base of the hair-point, or almost so,
L154:     but occasionally only to around 3/4 of
L155:     the leaf length. In H. stellata the margins
L156:     are typically recurved to around midleaf or a little above. H. ciliata is much
L157:     more variable and it shows some overlap
L158:     with H. striata, but we have seen many
L159:     specimens with almost plane margins.
L160:     This character is therefore inconclusive as
L161:     a diagnostic aid.
L162:     Gallego et al. (2014) pointed out that
L163:     in H. striata the marginal cells at the base
L164:     of the leaf just above the insertion are
L165:     often ornamented by prominent papillae
L166:     and/or double teeth. The same cells are
L167:     smooth in H. stellata and H. ciliata. Figure
L168:     4 shows a leaf base with exceptionally
L169:     well-developed papillae and teeth but in
L170:     fact they are often absent, and it may be
L171:     necessary to search a number of leaves/
L172:     shoots to demonstrate their presence.
L173:     Thus it is their presence that is a useful
L174:     confirmatory character. Their absence is
L175:     inconclusive. The extreme base of the leaf
L176:     should be examined. The base is normally
L177:     decurrent as a short wing along the stem.
L178:     Buchbender et al. (2014) give two
L179:     further differentiating characters. (1) The
L180:     spores of H. striata are ornamented on the
L181:     distal surface with elongate vermiform
L182:     (worm-like) papillae. Those of H. ciliata
L183:     have mostly simple papillae, occasionally
L184:     mixed with vermiform ones. This is
L185:     consistent with our observations, but we
L186:     have seen very little material of H. ciliata
L187:     with mature capsules. The vermiform
L188:     papillae of H. striata are visible with the
L189:     light microscope as lines on the spore
L190:     surface. (2) The mid-leaf cells of H. striata are
L191:     shorter than those of H. ciliata (7.5−15.0 μm
L192:     vs. 12–33 μm). This appears to be a less reliable
L193:     character. Although the cells of H. striata are
L194:     indeed short, we have observed similarly short
L195:     cells in (=< 15 μm) in some material of H. ciliata
L196:     (measured at about ⅓ from the leaf apex).
L197:     Habitat of Hedwigia striata
L198:     At its two extant Welsh localities, H. striata grows
L199:     on basic igneous boulders in mountain llynnau
L200:     (tarns). It is rare at both sites, with records from
L201:     four boulders at Ffynnon Lloer (Fig. 5) and
L202:     one at Llyn Gafr. Ffynnon Lloer is at 655 m
L203:     altitude in the Carneddau mountains, whilst
L204:     Llyn Gafr is at 415 m altitude. The boulders that
L205:     support H. striata are set a metre or two into the
L206:     lake from the shore, but are large enough that
L207:     substantial areas are never submerged, although
L208:     they are subject to regular splash. Associates
L209:     recorded at Ffynnon Lloer include Grimmia
L210:     ramondii, Marsupella emarginata var. aquatica,
L211:     Racomitrium aciculare and Sciuro-hypnum
L212:     plumosum, with Pterigynandrum filiforme
L213:     sharing one boulder with H. striata and Bryum
L214:     muehlenbeckii on rocks nearby. Hedwigia stellata
L215:     is present on several boulders but did not share
L216:     any with H. striata. Associates at Llyn Gafr were
L217:     not recorded in detail.
L218:     Most of the historic Welsh records of H. striata
L219:     that mention a habitat come from boulders by
L220:     lakes, and there are numerous collections from
L221:     Llyn Idwal, which is ecologically similar to
L222:     Ffynnon Lloer but at a lower altitude. SDSB
L223:     searched boulders on ¾ of its margin in 2013
L224:     without locating H. striata or H. ciliata, but there
L225:     is a chance that it might still be present. Other
L226:     mountain llynnau were searched specifically for
L227:     H. ciliata during the 2015 BBS meeting without
L228:     success. There are also numerous early 20th
L229:     century collections from a very different habitat

L240:     At Easedale Tarn in the Lake District, H.
L241:     striata was found only on a single boulder, which
L242:     (as at Ffynnon Lloer) is large enough never to be
L243:     submerged. The tarn is situated at slightly over
L244:     280 m altitude and the bedrock is of Ordovician
L245:     strata. The lakeside boulders support a calcifuge
L246:     flora that includes Andreaea rothii, Campylopus
L247:     atrovirens, Grimmia ramondii, Racomitrium
L248:     aciculare, R. fasciculare, R. aquaticum, R.
L249:     sudeticum and R. lanuginosum. Other species on
L250:     boulders and outcrops on the banks near the tarn
L251:     include Gymnomitrion obtusum, G. crenulatum,
L252:     Grimmia donniana and Schistidium strictum.
L253:     There are scattered tufts and patches of Hedwigia
L254:     stellata (noted on 11 lakeside boulders). There
L255:     was nothing bryologically distinctive to mark
L256:     out the boulder supporting H. striata. The
L257:     bryophyte cover on this boulder consisted largely
L258:     of Hypnum cupressiforme, Racomitrium aciculare
L259:     and a little H. stellata.

L265:     Less is known in detail about the habitat of the
L266:     Scottish and Irish specimens, but as in England
L267:     and Wales most of them are from lakeside
L268:     habitats; others are from a burn in a ravine and a
L269:     rock slab by a waterfall. Of the many specimens
L270:     from New Galloway, one is labelled "on a rock
L271:     in a field, ½ a mile from the River Ken". The
L272:     old records from the Ochil Hills stand apart in
L273:     that they appear to have come from less humid
L274:     habitats, one specimen being labelled "summit
L275:     of Alva", which may refer to Wood Hill, an
L276:     exposed summit above Alva at 525 m.
L277:     The status of Hedwigia ciliata var. leucophaea
L278:     Buchbender et al. (2014) found that the characters
L279:     of var. leucophaea can be developed independently
L280:     in different taxa of the Hedwigia ciliata complex.
L281:     Two of the British records of the variety are
L282:     from localities that have produced numerous
L283:     specimens of H. striata (New Galloway, VC 73,
L284:     and Glas Bheinn, VC 108). Unfortunately the
L285:     vouchers of var. leucophaea, deposited in the
L286:     GL herbarium according to Crundwell (1995),
L287:     can no longer be traced, but it is very likely that
L288:     these particular records were based on plants of
L289:     H. striata. This leaves the occurrences on stone14
L290:     FieldBryology No115 | May16
L291:     tiled roofs in Monmouth and Herefordshire
L292:     (Callaghan, 2006) and a recent record from
L293:     Traprain Law in Scotland as the only confirmed
L294:     sites for the variety in Britain. Although its status
L295:     as a good variety remains uncertain, we propose
L296:     to retain it pending further study.
L297:     Distribution of Hedwigia striata and H. ciliata
L298:     Hedwigia striata has an oceanic distribution
L299:     in Britain and Ireland. In Britain the known
L300:     sites are scattered from North Wales through
L301:     the Lake District and SW Scotland north to
L302:     Sutherland. The old records from the Ochil
L303:     Hills depart slightly from this pattern, but the
L304:     oceanic tendency of H. striata is clear, and is
L305:     consistent with its wider distribution in Europe.
L306:     Buchbender et al. (2014) confirm records from
L307:     SW Norway and S Sweden through Belgium to
L308:     Spain, Portugal and Morocco.
L309:     After the transfer of many of its records to
L310:     Hedwigia striata and the exclusion of some misidentified or ambiguous vouchers, H. ciliata s.str.
L311:     is seen to have a much rarer presence in Britain
L312:     than previously thought, and it is no longer
L313:     known from Ireland. The sole remaining records
L314:     from Scotland are in the south-east, var. ciliata
L315:     at Sandyknowe Craigs, Smailholm (VC 80) and
L316:     var. leucophaea at Traprain Law (VC 82), both
L317:     from natural rock outcrops and made within
L318:     the last five years. The recent vouchers from
L319:     Earncraig Hill (VC 72) and Ben Lawers (VC 88)
L320:     are sterile and have some weakly plicate leaves,
L321:     and may belong to H. striata. Records from Skye
L322:     (VC 104) belong to H. stellata. In England and
L323:     Wales most of the confirmed records are from
L324:     roof tiles and walls, or as epiphytes (the latter
L325:     presumably being transitory occurrences), with a
L326:     single record from a tarmac path. However there
L327:     are a few occurrences on natural rock outcrops
L328:     and sarsen stones, in widely scattered localities
L329:     from Wiltshire north to Leicestershire and
L330:     Merionethshire. The Merionethshire specimen,
L331:     an old one mixed with Syntrichia ruralis from
L332:     Barmouth, is of interest as the only confirmed
L333:     record from the oceanic west of Britain.
L334:     Otherwise H. ciliata has a more southern and
L335:     eastern distribution than H. striata.
