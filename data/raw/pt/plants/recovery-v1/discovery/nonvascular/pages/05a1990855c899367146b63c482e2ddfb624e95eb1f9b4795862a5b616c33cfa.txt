L9@P0: ABSTRACT
L10@P0: The biotope approach developed originally for use in Northern Europe has been used to describe and classify
L11@P0: benthic marine assemblages in various parts o f the world, including the Laguna San Rafael National Park (LSRNP)
L12@P0: in southern Chile and nearby protected areas. Given is a brief description o f how to identify, describe, classify
L13@P0: and map marine biotopes. Some o f the more widely distributed rocky shore biotopes in the LSRN P area are
L14@P0: briefly described. Full biotope descriptions are given for the most distinctive biotope within the fjords (Macrocystis
L15@P0: pyrifera biotope) and two on the very wave-exposed open Pacific coast (Durvillaea antarctica, Lessonia
L16@P0: nigrescent). All the biotopes recognised are regarded as provisional and require further testing and refinement.
L17@P0: Once refined biotopes can be taken into account when making management and conservation decisions on
L18@P0: seashore and seabed habitats within the LSRNP and surrounding nature reserves.
L19@P0: Key words: Marine Biotope, Laguna San Rafael National Park, Aisén, Chile.

L33@P0: INTRODUCTION
L34@P0: To the south of the island of Chiloé the coast of Chile consists of a series of offshore islands
L35@P0: between which lie deep channels that sometimes lead into Qords and a glaciated mountainous hinterland.
L36@P0: Associated with this largely unspoilt wilderness is a unique assemblage of ecosystems whose habitat
L37@P0: diversification is greater than that to be found elsewhere in Chile. In this area lies the Laguna San Rafael
L38@P0: National Park (LSRNP) and other protected areas that harbour a wide variety of aquatic ecosystems,
L39@P0: including fully marine wave-exposed shores, brackish water estuaries, Qords, tidal lagoons, freshwater
L40@P0: lakes and rivers. Until the launch in 1996 of the LSRNP Darwin Initiative-funded biodiversity research
L41@P0-1: programme, the Corporación Nacional Forestal (CONAF) had a problem making meaningful and effective160 BOLETIN DEL MUSEO NACIONAL DE HISTORIA NATURAL. CHILE
L42@P1: management decisions in the absence of scientifically sound knowledge. In response to the urgent need
L43@P1: for baseline information on marine biodiversity and the conservation status of the coastal sector of the
L44@P1: Park and neighbouring protected areas, several collaborative research projects were undertaken involving
L45@P1: British and Chilean scientists. These projects were aimed primarily at the provision of information in a
L46@P1: form that CONAF staff could use for monitoring or poses and on which to base future management
L47@P1: decisions. Additionally, it was recognised that there was a priority to provide CONAF with tools necessary
L48@P1: for identifying and mapping marine animal and plant assemblages.
L49@P1: An approach developed in northern Europe for describing and classifying the seashore and seabed
L50@P1: habitats and their associated communities has been used in the LSRNP and its environs. It is designed to
L51@P1: provide a sound scientific framework for setting conservation priorities by enabling an assessment to be
L52@P1: made of the conservation value of sites and coastal sectors. The approach, termed biotope assessment, is
L53@P1: discussed here in the context of Chile and briefly described are the methods used to identify, describe
L54@P1: and to classifying benthic marine assemblages. Some preliminary findings concerning the more distinctive
L55@P1: rocky shore biotopes within the boundary of the LSRNP and protected areas to the north are presented.
L56@P1: BIOTOPES IN THE COASTAL ZONE
L57@P1: Until the advent of the biotope approach marine conservation management planning and the
L58@P1: selection of protected areas in Europe was based largely on subjective criteria or focused on a few highly
L59@P1: visible organism. The biotope approach was developed in order to provide more objective criteria and
L60@P1: has proven very successful when applied to assemblages of marine macroalgae and sessile invertebrate
L61@P1: fauna associated with rock and sediment shores. It was developed by the Joint Nature Conservation
L62@P1: Committee of the UK as part of an assessment and classification of marine habitats in Europe and became
L63@P1: known as ‘The Marine Nature Conservation Review’. Later the approach was expanded as part of the
L64@P1: European Union Biomar project co-funded by the European Commission Life Programme (Hiscock,
L65@P1: 1996; Connors et al., 1997). To date it has been used in Thailand in South-East Asia, Ghana in West
L66@P1: Africa, Abu Dhabi (UAE) in the Arabian Gulf, Helgoland in the North Sea and in the Azores archipelago
L67@P1: (Tittley & Neto, 2000); John et al. (1999) and Paterson et al. (2001) have reported on its application in
L68@P1: southern Chile.
L69@P1: ¿What are the biotopes?
L70@P1: A biotope is very broadly defined as ‘the biota interacting with the physical habitat: plants and
L71@P1: animals found at a location together with their immediate physical’. Defining a biotope is a convenient
L72@P1: means of summarising field conditions for the purpose of describing general ecological patterns. A
L73@P1: biotope should be compared to a ‘habitat’ which is defined as a physical entity (e.g. rock, sediment)
L74@P1: together with particular conditions of wave exposure, salinity, etc. Biotopes are artificial constructs used
L75@P1: simply for convenience with the boundary line between them often somewhat subjective. In drawing this
L76@P1: imaginary boundary, it is necessary to ensure that biotopes are easily recognisable ‘in the field’ by having
L77@P1: distinctive features and species and recur relatively frequently. They are best regarded as ‘nodes’ along
L78@P1: an environmental continuum and if carefully selected provide a useful tool to assist in the identification
L79@P1: of important ecological patterns and stimulate further ecological study;
L80@P1: The species assemblages used to define biotope are thought of as circumscribed units as opposed
L81@P1: to physical environmental features which cover a range of gradients and scales. The biotopes integrate
L82@P1: the species and abiotic components and can be used to described the larger landscape. The relationship

L94@P2: Every effort should be made when defining biotopes to ensure that they are conspicuous arid their
L95@P2: characteristics are easily recognisable. They should be described in terms of the following: (a) the most
L96@P2: conspicuous and predominant species; (b) if taxonomically difficult then life form is used, e.g. sponges,
L97@P2: sea squirts; (c) groups of species which form a mixed-species life form with a list of some of the more
L98@P2: important component species, e.g. turf forming algae, crustose coralline red algae; (d) key species in the
L99@P2: ecosystem, e.g. grazers such as sea urchins and molluscs; (e) species or group of less common species
L100@P2: unique to a biotope. Features of the physical habitat are used and include details of the substratum
L101@P2: (amount of physical relief; rock, coral, sand, etc.), position on shore and wave exposure. Scale is important
L102@P2: and for most shore work a biotope should cover an area of at least 5-10 square metres or more. Often this
L103@P2: area takes the form of a long linear strip reflecting the fact that many shore biotopes form horizonatl

L105@P2: biotope unit. Scale has to be used with caution since many biotopes are mixed or form a mosaic resulting

L107@P2: In summary, our biotopes should have the following features:-
L108@P2: • There should be a presumption to limit their number and not proliferate them.
L109@P2: • They should be based on conspicuous features.
L110@P2: • They should be identified readily (at least to a workable level in the hierarchy).
L111@P2: • They should be arranged into broader categories.
L112@P2: • Biotopes should be based on descriptions of areas ideally of about 25m x 25m for mapping.
L113@P2: What are advantages and disadvantages?
L114@P2: Apart from aiding rapid identification, the conspicuous species or life forms used to characterise
L115@P2: biotopes, are likely to be biologically important since they structure the habitat for less conspicuous
L116@P2: species, generally have a greater biomass and structure trophic interactions within the biotope (or larger
L117@P2-3: ecosystem). The more important biotopes so defined can be mapped using remote sensing data from162 BOLETIN DEL MUSEO NACIONAL DE HISTORIA NATURAL, CHILE
L118@P3: aerial photography and satellite imagery. However, small species (particularly grazers and carnivores)
L119@P3: may also exert considerable influence on the composition and biology of a biotope and therefore need to
L120@P3: be noted. For sediments, the use of the predominant species is especially problematic since these are
L121@P3: often buried.
L122@P3: Some of the main reasons for adopting the biotope approach are:
L123@P3: * To provide a short-hand and rapid way of describing complicated assemblages of species and habitat
L124@P3: features.
L125@P3: * To provide structure for describing small areas by predetermining the important attributes that should
L126@P3: be observed and noted.
L127@P3: * To provide a ‘yardstick’ description against which field records can be assessed.
L128@P3: * To describe the biological ‘landscape’ of small areas in a holistic way.
L129@P3: * To detect broad scale ecological patterns (especially by using mapping).
L130@P3: * To help with the interpretation of the ecology of the area.
L131@P3: * To focus attention on issues worthy of further study.
L132@P3: * To stratify ecological sampling and ensure that an area is surveyed in a representative way.
L133@P3: * To provide the basic units for mapping biological resources.
L134@P3: Limitations to the biotope approach are:
L135@P3: * They are human constructs and do not exist as real entities.
L136@P3: * They should be used to help ecologists develop new insights and must not be used to restrict vision.
L137@P3: * In particular, they must be used flexibly to help description, not rigidly to constrain description.
L138@P3: * They should be used to describe small areas in the field based on observed attributes. In other words,
L139@P3: they should not be the only information recorded in the field but be supported by other recorded
L140@P3: observations.
L141@P3: * It is unlikely that they will be adequate in themselves (without recording their attributes) to describe
L142@P3: fine scale diversity.
L143@P3: * They are not a complete description of the ecology of an area - just an introduction to the ecology.
L144@P3: How are biotopes defined?
L145@P3: In practise, biotopes are recognised based on prior knowledge of the major habitat types of a
L146@P3: region and field recording. It is also likely that the framework for biotopes will be similar to that derived
L147@P3: for other regions. Local knowledge might be useful in drawing up a basic framework for the biotope
L148@P3: system and suggesting what might be some of the higher levels present. Examination of maps and
L149@P3: charts, aerial photography as well as consulting local knowledge might assist in predicting what biotopes
L150@P3: might be found in an area.
L151@P3: All sources of information should be consulted at the pre-planning stage in order to construct a
L152@P3: list of habitat types that might be expected to support different biotopes. The next step is to carry out field
L153@P3: survey at as many representative sites as possible. These field records are used to compile the biotope
L154@P3: descriptions. Some survey site selection is need to ensure that well developed and typical examples of
L155@P3: different biotope types are covered rather than focusing too much attention on a mosaics of biotopes.
L156@P3: Such mixtures of biotopes are often difficult to interpret, at least initially, and often reflect substrate
L157@P3: heterogeneity. Inevitably continua will be recognised and it will be necessary to decide where to draw
L158@P3: the line between the ‘nodes’ or biotopes.
L159@P3: The format for recording data for deriving and refining biotope classifications is the same as that
L160@P3: used for field recording for biotope mapping. Marine and coastal intertidal biotopes should be grouped
L161@P3: into very coarse categories that form the basis for a very broad classification. For example, the broad
L162@P3: categories found in the LSRNP include rocky marine shores, rocky brackish shores, sand/shingle shores

L164@P4: How are biotopes described?
L165@P4: A formalise scheme has been adopted for laying out the description of biotopes and this is shown
L166@P4: for the provisionally recognised Macrocystis, Durvillaea and Lessonia biotopes described in southern
L167@P4: Chile (see below). The biotopes in Chile are still inadequately described and for that reason not all the
L168@P4: categories of information that are required for standard descriptions are available.
L169@P4: All biotopes should be given a unique code letter based on biotope features some combination of
L170@P4: key species, life form and physical habitat characteristics. The descriptive title is often long since included
L171@P4: are the key biological characteristics of the biotope and features of the physical environment, with emphasis
L172@P4: placed on those that distinguish it from closely related biotopes. The physical component in the title
L173@P4: normally includes position on the shore, substratum and other key habitat features such as wave-exposure.
L174@P4: To ensure the title is bot too long and clumsy only key habitat characteristics or characterizing species
L175@P4: are mentioned.
L176@P4: The “habitat classification” is placed before or after the habitat description. The following are the
L177@P4: habitat characteristics commonly considered: salinity, wave exposure (very sheltered to very/extremely
L178@P4: exposed), tidal streams (weak to strong), substratum (sand, gravel, mud, bedrock, boulders, etc.), zone
L179@P4: (supralittoral downward to sublittoral fringe; sublittoral zone), height/depth in relation to chart datum,
L180@P4: and any other important features (e.g. also on sheltered vertical rock).
L181@P4: The biotope description is an account of the general nature of the habitat and community
L182@P4: characteristics, its variability (including any known temporal changes), any microhabitat features (e.g.,
L183@P4: crevices, fissures, beneath boulders, shaded, Macrocystis stipes), and its relationship to other biotopes
L184@P4: (i.e. along gradients of zonation on the shore, substrate type, wave exposure, salinity, etc.).
L185@P4: Distribution in the region is important and sometimes a map is included indicating its site or
L186@P4: general distribution in a coastal sector. Where there is a considerable body of information in existence
L187@P4: then it might be possible to indicate its likely frequency of occurrence.
L188@P4: Photographs to illustrate the main features of a biotope are important and should show it in broad
L189@P4: view and/or close up. In some cases it might be necessary to photograph it at different times in the season
L190@P4: if it changes in the course of time.
L191@P4: Other features are recommended for inclusion in biotope description. Unfortunately, unless a
L192@P4: region has been thoroughly surveyed then it is not always possible to include information on regional
L193@P4: variation in habitat or species characteristics compared to elsewhere. Similarly without having a large
L194@P4: body of information available it is often difficult to comment on features of conservation interest Often
L195@P4: it is desirable to include a list of species that characterise the biotope and the typical abundance at which
L196@P4: they occur. The list should include species listed in perhaps over 60% o f the records o f the biotope
L197@P4: (“constant species”) compared to those in less than 60% of the records ('faithful species’). In addition,
L198@P4: normally listed would be those that occur in a high proportion of the records but which are not particularly
L199@P4: indicative of the biotope.

L237@P5: How are biotopes mapped?
L238@P5: The mapping of robust, easily recognised and well defined biotopes provides information that
L239@P5: can be combined with maps of land use, geology, climate and coastal morphology to study the dynamic
L240@P5: patterns of landscape ecology and their underlying causes. These maps also provide valuable information for the identification of sites of conservation or heritage importance, sites which are sensitive to
L241@P5: various types of disturbance (e.g. tourist impact, fish farming) and will also help identify areas for which
L242@P5: the sustainable use of resources may be achievable. They can also be used by those involved with the
L243@P5: scientific study and teaching of ecology as well as by planners and economists.
L244@P5: It is useful if biotopes are based on conspicuous and highly visible characteristics so that they
L245@P5: may be used in conjunction with remote sensing. Traditional cartography and remote sensing impose
L246@P5: size limits on biotopes. Often is difficult to map units less than about 25m x 25m and this size is
L247@P5: probably also appropriate for use in the interpretation of remotely sensed data. Unfortunately, on the
L248@P5: steep sided rocky shores of the fjord systems in Chile the biotopes frequently are in the form of narrow
L249@P5: linear strips less than a metre in width. It is often impractical to have too many classes shown on a map
L250@P5-6: and likewise it is difficult (if not impossible) to interpret a large number of classes from remotely sensedP.M. JOHN et al. I A “biotope" approach to marine benthic biological assemblages 165
L251@P6: data. The upper limit imposed by these practical considerations means that the large number of maps
L252@P6: might be needed to cover a large region. However, it would be reasonable to arrange the biotopes into
L253@P6: some form of hierarchy of smaller numbers of higher levels and possibly even subdividing the basic
L254@P6: mapping biotopes into smaller units as the location demands (or to be used for specific purposes).
L255@P6: The mapping of habitats and biotopes should be designed to give an overview of the broad but
L256@P6: ecologically important properties of an ecosystem. It is possible from the observed biotope distribution
L257@P6: patterns to formulate hypotheses about very broad ecological patterns (e.g. importance of salinity, sea
L258@P6: urchin grazing), and to provide a context for the interpretation of more detailed data. The approach is
L259@P6: not designed to provide a definitive description of the shore ecosystem, but rather to give a broad description
L260@P6: intended to be the starting point for further investigations that might include monitoring the biological
L261@P6: impact of future development including the impact of tourism in an area.

L288@P7: Many of the rocky shore biotopes described below form bands and the position they occupy is
L289@P7: described using the scheme and terminology proposed by Stephenson and Stephenson (1947,1972) that
L290@P7: was modified by Lewis (1964). According to Lewis, the shore can be divided into a littoral zone and an
