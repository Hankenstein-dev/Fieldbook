L32@P1: ABSTRACT. We characterized the morphological variation and algal species
L33@P1: associations of the filamentous green alga, Acrosiphonia arcta (distinguishing it
L34@P1: from its closely related sister genus Spongomorpha), in two contrasting
L35@P1: environments in Nova Scotia, Canada. The habitats differed in seawater
L36@P1: salinity and temperature (and fluctuations thereof), substratum, vertical
L37@P1: position on the shore, wave exposure, turbidity, and diversity of macroalgae
L38@P1: and macrofauna. Acrosiphonia arcta thalli exhibited distinct morphologies in
L39@P1: the different environments. Thalli from the open-ocean Whitehead site (Atlantic
L40@P1: coast) exhibited the typical A. arcta morphology of profuse, short, curved
L41@P1: branches and rhizoids binding the filaments together to form hemispherical to
L42@P1: spherical tufts. Thalli from the sheltered Pomquet Harbour Estuary (Gulf of St.
L43@P1: Lawrence coast) consisted of matted clumps with extensive rhizoidal growth at
L44@P1: the base but little branching otherwise. Acrosiphonia arcta was primarily
L45@P1: associated with thick leathery and crustose (coralline) algae at Whitehead. In
L46@P1: the Pomquet Harbour Estuary, a less stable habitat than Whitehead, sheet and
L47@P1: filamentous algae were in greater abundance with A. arcta than at Whitehead.
L48@P1: Damaged apical cells from thalli of both Pomquet and Whitehead A. arcta were
L49@P1: able to regenerate new filamentous tips or to give rise to rhizoids, phenomena
L50@P1: not previously documented for Acrosiphonia in North America. Morphological
L51@P1: variation of A. arcta in different habitats points to its ability to respond to and
L52@P1: persist in a wide range of abiotic (and possibly biotic) conditions, and helps to
L53@P1: explain its seasonal dominance on the east and west coasts of North America.

L56@P1: Acrosiphonia is a filamentous green alga composed of branched
L57@P1: uniseriate filaments that tend to be bound together by hooked
L58@P1: branchlets or by rhizoids to form ropey masses or spherical tufts. Its
L59@P1: RHODORA, Vol. 113, No. 953, pp. 87–105, 2011
L60@P1: E Copyright 2011 by the New England Botanical Club
L61@P1-2: 87thalli are seasonally abundant (spring and summer) on rocky
L62@P2: substrata in the low to mid-intertidal zone of the exposed open
L63@P2: coast and of relatively sheltered inlets in the northeastern Pacific
L64@P2: (Hudson 1974; Sussmann and DeWreede 2001). In the northwestern and northeastern Atlantic, Acrosiphonia thalli are present in the
L65@P2: low intertidal and upper subtidal zones of the exposed open coast
L66@P2: and in estuarine habitats (Kornmann 1970b; Mathieson et al. 2010;
L67@P2: Sears 1998; Taylor 1962; Villalard-Bohnsack 2003).
L68@P2: In southwestern British Columbia, field and molecular work has
L69@P2: demonstrated that this seasonal alga (a gametophyte) alternates
L70@P2: with a unicell (the sporophyte) that colonizes and overwinters in the
L71@P2: foliose red alga Mazzaella splendens (Setch. & Gardner) Fredericq
L72@P2: and in the crustose phase of Mastocarpus papillatus (C. Agardh)
L73@P2: Ku¨tz. (Sussmann and DeWreede 2001; Sussmann et al. 1999). We
L74@P2: have little understanding of the life history of Acrosiphonia on the
L75@P2: east coast of North America; alternating phases have not been
L76@P2: elucidated in nature.
L77@P2: Acrosiphonia has long been difficult to separate from Spongomorpha (Collins 1909; Jo´nsson 1991; Kornmann 1965, 1970b;
L78@P2: Scagel 1966; Wille 1900), its sister genus in phylogenetic reconstructions (Sussmann et al. 1999). The two genera were originally
L79@P2: separated on the basis of cell length-to-width ratios (Agardh 1846),
L80@P2: but this and other vegetative/morphological characters (e.g., cell
L81@P2: diameters, branching patterns, and shapes of tip cells) have often
L82@P2: been unreliable. Spongomorpha is absent on the northwestern coast
L83@P2: of North America, but present on the northeastern coast
L84@P2: (Mathieson et al. 2010; Sears 1998; Taylor 1962; VillalardBohnsack 2003), and has been reported from New Jersey, U.S.A.,
L85@P2: north to Baffin Island, Canada (Hillson 1977). The first objective of
L86@P2: this study was to differentiate Acrosiphonia thalli in the northwestern Atlantic from Spongomorpha thalli.
L87@P2: There is also a lack of agreement on species delimitation of
L88@P2: Acrosiphonia, primarily due to variability in characteristics such as
L89@P2: cell diameters, cell length-to-width ratios, presence or absence of
L90@P2: hooked branchlets, and branching patterns of thalli (Hudson 1974;
L91@P2: Kornmann 1962, 1965, 1970b; Scagel 1966; Setchell and Gardner
L92@P2: 1920; Taylor 1962). Hudson (1974) and Sussmann and DeWreede
L93@P2: (2001) recognized two Acrosiphonia species, A. arcta (Dillwyn) Gain
L94@P2: and A. mertensii Yendo, from the northeastern Pacific. Acrosiphonia mertensii is present only in the northeastern Pacific, whereas A.
L95@P2: arcta has been documented in both the northern and southern
L96@P2-3: 88 Rhodora [Vol. 113hemispheres, including the northwestern and northeastern Atlantic
L97@P3: (Hudson 1974; Kjellman 1883; Kornmann 1970b; Mathieson et al.
L98@P3: 2010; Sears 1998; Taylor 1962; van Oppen et al. 1994; van Patten
L99@P3: 2006; Villalard-Bohnsack 2003).
L100@P3: Acrosiphonia arcta has been viewed both as a single, quite
L101@P3: variable species (Burrows 1991; Hudson 1974; Kornmann 1962;
L102@P3: Sussmann and DeWreede 2001) and conversely as a complex of
L103@P3: closely related species (Scagel 1966; Setchell and Gardner 1920).
L104@P3: Burrows (1991), Hudson (1974), and Kornmann (1962) united A.
L105@P3: spinescens (Ku¨tz.) Kjellm. and A. arcta in A. arcta on the basis of
L106@P3: morphological and microscopic characters. At least for the
L107@P3: northeastern Pacific, Hudson (1974) found the two taxa to
L108@P3: intergrade completely for all the characteristics she observed, and
L109@P3: culture studies demonstrated the plasticity of the alga in response to
L110@P3: varying physical factors. Sussmann et al. (1999) were unable to
L111@P3: separate northeastern Pacific A. arcta and A. spinescens based on
L112@P3: rDNA ITS sequences. On the other hand, A. C. Mathieson and C.
L113@P3: Dawes (Univ. New Hampshire, Durham, NH; pers. comm.)
L114@P3: consider A. arcta and A. spinescens to be distinct species based on
L115@P3: unpublished morphological and rDNA ITS sequence data from
L116@P3: thalli from Maine, U.S.A.
L117@P3: According to Sears (1998), two Acrosiphonia species comprise the
L118@P3: green filamentous alga in the northwestern Atlantic. In addition to
L119@P3: A. arcta, Sears (1998) recognized A. sonderi (Ku¨tz.) Kornmann,
L120@P3: similar in appearance to A. arcta, and described by Kornmann
L121@P3: (1962) in Helgoland, Germany. Villalard-Bohnsack (2003), on the
L122@P3: other hand, maintained that only one well-defined entity, A. arcta,
L123@P3: is represented on the east coast of North America. South and
L124@P3: Hooper (1980) stated that they were not able to distinguish between
L125@P3: A. sonderi and A. arcta thalli in Newfoundland, Canada. Other
L126@P3: Acrosiphonia species, not reported for North America, have been
L127@P3: identified in Japan, Germany, France, and Scandinavia (Kornmann
L128@P3: 1962, 1970a; Miyaji 1984, 1996; Miyaji and Kurogi 1976).
L129@P3: Further molecular studies, involving selection of a more variable
L130@P3: DNA sequence than ITS sequences, and applied to both Pacific and
L131@P3: Atlantic specimens, are needed to resolve the infrageneric relationships within Acrosiphonia. Regardless of taxonomic uncertainties,
L132@P3: in this study we recognize a single species, A. arcta (based on
L133@P3: intergradations of all thalli for morphological and microscopic
L134@P3: characters), occurring in considerably different environments of the
L135@P3: northwestern Atlantic. Acrosiphonia arcta has several traits that are
L136@P3-4: 2011] Sussmann and Scrosati—Variation in Acrosiphonia 89indicative of good colonizers of unstable environments. The ability
L137@P4: of A. arcta to exhibit plasticity in response to varying environmental factors, as well as its high productivity (Sussmann 2000;
L138@P4: Sussmann and DeWreede 2005, 2007), suggest an array of strategies
L139@P4: for the alga’s survival in a wide range of environments. For this
L140@P4: study, we hypothesized that the filamentous phase of A. arcta
L141@P4: would display gross differences in morphology and algal species
L142@P4: associations in environmentally different habitats of the northwestern Atlantic.
L143@P4: MATERIALS AND METHODS
L144@P4: We collected hemispherical to spherical, tufted green macroalgae
L145@P4: from the low intertidal and subtidal zones in June 2007 at the
L146@P4: sheltered estuarine system of Pomquet Harbour (45u399N,
L147@P4: 61u509W) and the exposed, open coastal sites of Whitehead
L148@P4: (45u139N, 61u119W) and Tor Bay Provincial Park (45u119N,
L149@P4: 61u219W) in Nova Scotia, Canada. Delimitation between Acrosiphonia and Spongomorpha thalli was based on a number of
L150@P4: dependable morphological and cytological microscopic characteristics such as the number of nuclei per cell, cell diameter, and thallus
L151@P4: length. To determine whether cells were uninucleate or multinucleate, live specimens from the above sites were immersed in DAPI
L152@P4: stain [0.5 mg DAPI in 100 ml distilled water; working solution of
L153@P4: 1:9 parts stock to buffer and pH 4.1, as described by Garbary and
L154@P4: Clarke (2001)], microwaved for 5–10 sec and viewed with an
L155@P4: epifluorescence microscope. Acrosiphonia and Spongomorpha specimens were deposited at the herbarium of St. Francis Xavier
L156@P4: University (STFX), Nova Scotia.
L157@P4: Acrosiphonia was only identified at Pomquet Harbour (between
L158@P4: 20.1 m and +0.3 m relative to chart datum) and at Whitehead
L159@P4: (between 0 m and +0.7 m relative to chart datum). Pomquet
L160@P4: Harbour is a shallow lagoon system with a mean depth of about
L161@P4: three meters. Our study site was located on a channel where the
L162@P4: water was well mixed from tidal currents (average tidal amplitude
L163@P4: of 0.5 m) and remained free of ice during the winter. Mean water
L164@P4: temperature varies annually from 0 to 24uC, whereas mean salinity
L165@P4: varies from 18% to about 27% (Bird et al. 1976; Kim et al. 2004).
L166@P4: The lower salinity value is due to dilution by spring rain and runoff
L167@P4: from melting snow. In the exposed Whitehead region, surface
L168@P4: seawater temperature varies between monthly means of 0.3 and
L169@P4-5: 90 Rhodora [Vol. 11316.4uC, whereas salinity varies between monthly means of 30.5%
L170@P5: and 31.0% (Fisheries and Oceans Canada 2009). Figure 1 compares
L171@P5: monthly water temperature and salinity at Pomquet Harbour and
L172@P5: the Whitehead region.
L173@P5: Water temperature and salinity were recorded at the Pomquet
L174@P5: Harbour site on 16 June 2007 and at the Whitehead site on 18 June
L175@P5: 2007. Water temperature was measured with a mercury thermometer and salinity with a refractometer. Percentage cover of
L176@P5: Acrosiphonia arcta and the associated macroalgae and macrofauna
L177@P5: was also documented at the two sites in mid-June 2007. Twentymeter transect lines were placed parallel to the shore in the A. arcta
L178@P5: zone, and random number tables generated 25 locations for
L179@P5: placement of 20 cm 3 20 cm quadrats along the transects. Quadrats
L180@P5: were centered on the transect line, and monofilament divided each
L181@P5: quadrat into 100 squares.
L182@P5: At each site, 15 Acrosiphonia arcta thalli were haphazardly
L183@P5: collected to determine reproductive status. Forty Chondrus crispus
L184@P5: Stackh. blades (the prevalent foliose red alga) were collected at
L185@P5: Whitehead and examined for the presence of ‘Chlorochytrium
L186@P5: inclusum,’ the sporophyte of Acrosiphonia known to colonize bladed
L187@P5: red algae (Chihara 1969; Freeman 1899; Hudson 1974; Kjellman
L188@P5: 1883; Kornmann 1964; Miyaji and Kurogi 1976; Printz 1926;
L189@P5: Setchell and Gardner 1920; Sussmann and DeWreede 2002). At
L190@P5: Pomquet Harbour, the tubular brown alga, Scytosiphon lomentaria
L191@P5: (Lyngb.) Link, was collected, the search being prompted by a report
L192@P5: of ‘Chlorochytrium inclusum’ present in Scytosiphon collected from
L193@P5: Pomquet Harbour in June 1974 (Bird et al. 1976). Thirty segments,
L194@P5: 5–10 cm long, from separate thalli were cut open and flattened on a
L195@P5: slide to check for possible colonization by ‘Chlorochytrium.’

L310@P11: DISCUSSION
L311@P11: Delimitation of Acrosiphonia and Spongomorpha. It is virtually
L312@P11: impossible to distinguish between Acrosiphonia and Spongomorpha
L313@P11: in the field (Figure 2A, 2B). Vegetative/morphological characteristics, in isolation, have often been unreliable for separation of the
L314@P11: two genera, probably in part due to the extreme plasticity of
L315@P11: characters in response to temperature and light intensity (Hudson
L316@P11: 1974; Kornmann 1965, 1970b). We found a number of northwestern Atlantic herbarium specimens at St. Francis Xavier University,
L317@P11: Nova Scotia, that were misidentified (as Spongomorpha rather than
L318@P11: Acrosiphonia) due to the use of morphological characteristics alone.
L319@P11: We have found the number of nuclei per cell, first proposed by
L320@P11-12: 2011] Sussmann and Scrosati—Variation in Acrosiphonia 97Wille (1900), to be the best non-molecular diagnostic tool for the
