L39@P1: 24.1 Morphology
L40@P1: Habitus: Plants are bright green, sometimes yellowish or dark green and usually not
L41@P1: encrusted (Fig. 24.1). Branchlets of male plants are often curved inward (Fig. 24.2a).
L42@P1: Plants can be compact and stiff (shallow water, Fig. 24.3a) or slender with long
L43@P1: internodes (deeper water, Fig. 24.3b, c).

L64@P3: Size: The plants are small to medium-sized (5–20 cm) but may reach up to 50 cm if
L65@P3: growing in deeper water.
L66@P3: Main axis: The shoot diameter is 0.3–0.6 (1.2) mm, but based on Urbaniak and
L67@P3: G˛abka (2014) can reach up to 1.4 mm. The internodes are generally longer than the
L68@P3: branches, 0.3–2 (5.3) cm in length, in the upper whorls mainly shorter or of equal
L69@P3: length.
L70@P3: Cortex: The cortex is triplostichous and isostichous, the surface smooth and finely
L71@P3: striped. The cortex can be slightly twisted (Figs. 24.2e and 24.3f).
L72@P3: Spine-cells: Spine-cells are lacking or rudimentary. Cortex nodal cells are visible or
L73@P3: solitary rudimentary cells are present (Fig. 24.2e).
L74@P3: Stipulodes: Stipulodes are small and globular, arranged in two rows, but often
L75@P3: rudimentary and form irregular rows (Figs. 24.2f and 24.3f).
L76@P3: Branchlets: Predominantly 6–9 (up to 10) branchlets are arranged in one whorl. Each
L77@P3: branchlet has 8–10 segments of which the upper 1–2 short segments are ecorticated.
L78@P3: The end cell is narrow, short and pointed. The branchlets of the male plants are
L79@P3-4: mainly curved inward (Figs. 24.2c–d and 24.3d–f).368 K. Torn and E. Nat
L80@P4: Bract-cells: Rudimentary papillous cells are present.
L81@P4: Bracteoles: Elongated and slender. On male plants bracteoles are mainly shorter than
L82@P4: ripe antheridia, rarely of the same length. On female plants they are shorter than or
L83@P4: as long as the oogonia.
L84@P4: Bractlets: Elongate, shorter than or as long as the oogonia (Fig. 24.2d).
L85@P4: Sexuality: The species is dioecious. Gametangia solitary, only developed on the
L86@P4: lowest 1–4 (rarely 5) segments of the branchlets (Figs. 24.2c, d and 24.3d).
L87@P4: Oogonia: Solitary, coronula strongly convergent. Table 24.1 gives an overview about
L88@P4: morphometrical data available (see also Fig. 24.2d).
L89@P4: Oospores: Oospores are slender, dark brown to black when mature (Fig. 24.3e).
L90@P4: Membrane of the oospore is granular (Corillion, 1957; Groves and BullockWebster, 1924; Urbaniak and Blaženˇci´c, 2012). Table 24.2 gives an overview about
L91@P4: morphometrical data.
L92@P4: Antheridia: Antheridia are inconspicuous; maximum diameter 1100 µm. Colour is
L93@P4: orange red to brownish red, located relatively close to each other (Figs. 24.2c and
L94@P4: 24.3d). Table 24.2 gives an overview about morphometrical data available Table 24.3.
L95@P4: Bulbils: According to most authors bulbils are missing. Based on German, Danish
L96@P4: and Swedish material multicellular bulbils may be present (Becker, 2016; Blindow,
L97@P4: 2008; Schou et al., 2017; see Fig. 24.2b).
L98@P4: 24.2 Phenology and Reproduction
L99@P4: Chara connivens is mostly annual, data about germination are limited. Fertile plants
L100@P4: were found from June to September (Becker, 2016; Mouronval et al., 2015; Torn and
L101@P4: Martin, 2004). The plants in the Mediterranean Basin can begin to fructify in March
L102@P4: (R. Becker own data), then die off in temporary waters as they dehydrate in summer
L103@P4: and germinate again after the rainfall (Becker, 2016).
L104@P4: 24.3 Habitat Conditions and Ecology
L105@P4: Habitat type: Chara connivens has been found in lakes, ditches, temporary ponds,
L106@P4: swamps, artificial irrigation systems, as well as in the brackish Baltic Sea and its
L107@P4: associated lagoons and coastal lakes and in the Mediterranean area up to Israel
L108@P4: especially along the coast.
L109@P4: Sediment: Grows on sand, mud, silty sand, muddy sand, muddy clay, loamy
L110@P4-5: conditions, peat bogs and in the Baltic Sea also on mixtures of sand with pebbles.24 Chara connivens 369

L205@P7: present up to 4 m depth (Torn and Martin, 2004). In Germany, the Netherlands and
L206@P7: in Sardinia Chara connivens is often found at depths below 1 m.
L207@P7: Associated species: A large range of associated species is listed in literature. As
L208@P7: accompanying species are among others mentioned: Chara aspera, C. globularis, C.
L209@P7: hispida, C. tomentosa, C. vulgaris, C. virgata, Myriophyllum spicatum, Stuckenia
L210@P7: pectinata, Najas marina (Becker, 2016 and references therein). Chara connivens
L211@P7: also occurs in monospecific stands.
L212@P7: 24.4 Variability
L213@P7: Variability of the species is minor. Plants from deeper water or more dense communities are thin and slender with relatively long internodes and do not hold shape.
L214@P7: Branchlets of sterile specimens do not curve inward, also some fertile specimens
L215@P7: may have straight branchlets.
L216@P7: 24.5 Identification and Risk of Confusion
L217@P7: Chara connivens cannot be differentiated from C. globularis when sterile. The differences are also minor with C. virgata if it is sterile and when the stipulodes of C.
L218@P7: virgata are undeveloped. The spine-cells of C. virgata are larger compared to missing
L219@P7: or rudimentary spine-cells of C. connivens. From spineless C. aspera or C. aspera
L220@P7: with papilliformous spine-cells it can be differentiated by acute tips of stipulodes
L221@P7: of C. aspera (Nielsen, 2004a). Dioecious C. fragifera can be distinguished from C.
L222@P7: connivens by the characteristic multicellular bulbils of C. fragifera (Nielsen, 2004b).

L233@P8: 24.7 Status and Threats
L234@P8: The species is extinct in several localities (Becker, 2016) where it had been described
L235@P8: by Migula (1889–1897). Chara connivens was included in the previous HELCOM
L236@P8: list of threatened and/or declining species. The species is not considered threatened
L237@P8: in the Baltic Sea at the moment. In the Estonian waters C. connivens has become
L238@P8: more common in recent decades and the species is rather frequent also along the
L239@P8: Uppland coast in Sweden (HELCOM, 2013) with probably stable occurrence. One
L240@P8: of the Chara collections in the Linnaean herbarium is Chara connivens (Gregor et al.,
L241@P8: 2019). In Germany it is a rare species in the past and at present. In the Netherlands
L242@P8: it is rare at the Wadden Islands and the dune area of the North Sea and has more or
L243@P8: less stable vegetations in peat puddles. Chara connivens is very rare in Italy and in
L244@P8: other parts of the Mediterranean area (Becker, 2019) except the Iberian Peninsula,
L245@P8: where it is regarded as rather common (Cirujano et al., 2008).
L246@P8: 24.8 Remarks
L247@P8: The distribution vector of the species into the Baltic Sea is considered controversial.
L248@P8: The species is assumed to have been introduced into the Baltic Sea by shipping
L249@P8-9: (Leppäkoski and Olenin, 2000; Luther, 1979; Paavola et al., 2005). However, a natural24 Chara connivens 373
L250@P9: dispersal by waterfowl is rather probable (Blindow, 2009). In Finland, the species has
L251@P9: been considered unsuitable for IUCN Red List assessment as an introduced species
L252@P9: (Hyvärinen et al., 2019). Although, HELCOM (HELCOM, 2013), Sweden (SLU
L253@P9: Artdatabanken, 2020) and Estonia (eElurikkus, 2021) have assessed the threats to
L254@P9: the species. Trbojevi´c et al. (2020) reported Chara connivens from Serbia, but it did
L255@P9: not cluster with other C. connivens, but more closely with C. globularis.
