L95@P3: Thailand’s rich marine algal resources have attracted much interest
L96@P3: from both the industries as well as the scientists. Compared to its neighbouring
L97@P3: countries, Thailand has a high number of phycologists, especially from amongst
L98@P3: the younger generation. It is therefore timely that this field guide be published so
L99@P3: as to facilitate the identification and therefore sustainable management of the rich
L100@P3: marine algal resources of Thailand. This is the third field guide on tropical seaweeds
L101@P3: produced by the Ghent Phycology Research Group, this time in collaboration with
L102@P3: Seaweed and Seagrass Research Unit, Prince of Songkla University and a local,
L103@P3: well-respected senior algae taxonomist, Professor Khanjanapaj Lewmanomont.
L104@P3: This field guide provides a comprehensive account of the seaweeds
L105@P3: from Ko Samui – Mu Ko Tha Lae Tai, Thailand and includes a description of
L106@P3: this group of islands, starting from the geography and geology, to the physical
L107@P3: attributes and the seaweed communities with notes on seasonality, zonation and
L108@P3: threats to the ecosystem. This field guide also gives a historical perspective of
L109@P3: phycological research in Thailand. Additional chapters on the general biology of
L110@P3: seaweeds, classification and taxonomy, life-histories and reproduction, provide
L111@P3: good introductions to those who wish to learn about seaweeds in general.
L112@P3: Practical guidance to seaweed collection and research is provided for by the
L113@P3: chapter on survey methods for seaweeds, including collection and analysis of
L114@P3: ecological and environmental data.
L115@P3: A total of 77 taxa with 10 new records for Thailand and one new
L116@P3: species to science, are reported in this field guide. This represents one third
L117@P3: of the reported seaweed species of Thailand, indicating the importance of the
L118@P3: Ko Samui group of islands in the Gulf of Thailand. This checklist is also an
L119@P3: important contribution to the overall inventory of seaweeds in the Indo-west
L120@P3: Pacific region which has one of the world’s highest biodiversity.

L187@P7: 1 | Purpose of this book
L188@P7: In the first place, this book is meant to provide a summary of field and
L189@P7: laboratory techniques in phycology (the study of algae), and their ecosystems.
L190@P7: It also gives a glance to the dominant species of marine algae present along
L191@P7: the coast of the Nature Reserve of Mu Ko Tha Lae Tai (SE. Thailand, Fig 1).
L192@P7: In addition, it should, therefore, be clear that the species described and illustrated here are only part of the marine flora of Mu Ko Tha Lae Tai, not at all
L193@P7: a comprehensive Flora. It is an easy-to-use guide to the identification of the
L194@P7: most frequent seaweeds, intended to be used by biologists, students, amateur
L195@P7: naturalists, and others interested in the marine life of this area.
L196@P7: The taxonomic part covers the different groups of marine macroalgae
L197@P7: (Chlorophyta or green algae, Phaeophyceae or brown algae and Rhodophyta or
L198@P7: red algae). Numerous smaller seaweeds (mostly epiphytic ones) and turf algae
L199@P7: (low, dense mats of grazed algae) are omitted although they can locally be a very
L200@P7: important component of tropical and subtropical marine ecosystems, especially
L201@P7: • Fig. 2. The first settlers replaced the original coastal vegetation of Ko Samui by coconut
L202@P7-8: plantations.Introduction

L204@P8: in heavily grazed areas. Encrusting coralline algae are not included as they are
L205@P8: not well studied in the region and are mostly not easily identified by simple
L206@P8: observation and descriptions. The prokaryotic blue-green algae (= Cyanophyta
L207@P8: or Cyanobacteria) are not covered either.
L208@P8: Although the Thai seaweed flora has been relatively poorly studied, it
L209@P8: seems to be relatively rich, with about 326 taxa (excluding blue-greens), of

L213@P8: flora of the country.

L215@P8: between ease of use and technical detail. The photographs of the macroalgae

L218@P8: anybody to identify these most frequent seaweeds in the field.

L224@P8: 2.1. Introduction
L225@P8: Apparently, the first settlers on the volcanic island of Ko Samui were
L226@P8: ethnic Malay fishermen and seafarers from Hainan (S. China) at a time that
L227@P8: the surrounding waters were teemed with fish. At that time (1687) the island
L228@P8: was called ‘Pulo Cornam’. The name Ko Samui would either be derived from
L229@P8: ‘samui’, the name of a native tree, or from ‘Saboey’, a Chinese word for ‘safe
L230@P8: haven’, an apt description of the island’s largely protected waters. These settlers
L231@P8: replaced the original coastal vegetation by coconut plantations (Fig. 2) on the
L232@P8-9: coastal plains and by rubber plantations on the lower hill slopes.Introduction

L275@P12: 2.2. Location
L276@P12: Ko Samui, the third largest island of Thailand, is situated in the Gulf of
L277@P12: Thailand, a shallow semi-enclosed bay in the Pacific Ocean. The gulf roughly
L278@P12: has a rectangular shape with a width of 400 km and a length of 720 km. It is
L279@P12: directed in a NW-SE direction. Ko Samui is more or less polygonal, 15 km
L280@P12: across, with a huge bay in the north, has a total surface of 229 km2
L281@P12:  and is peaking at 635 m (Khao Pom). It lays at 16 km east of continental Thailand, about
L282@P12: 500 km south of Bangkok and 275 km north of Hat Yai (Fig. 1).
L283@P12: Ko Samui is surrounded by about 60 islands, of which Ko Phangan is the
L284@P12: largest one, situated 16 km north of the main island. The islands of the Angthong National Marine Park in the West are at about 25 km from Ko Samui,
L285@P12: and the islands of Mu Ko Tha Lae Tai (‘Islands of the South Sea’) are close by
L286@P12: on the south. The last ones are spread along the SW of Ko Samui and East of
L287@P12: Ao Thong Nean along the continental shore. They are composed of 6 small
L288@P12: islands (Figs. 1, 4), of which 5 have been studied: Ko Taen (Fig. 4), Ko Mat Sum,
L289@P12: Ko Wang Nai (‘Inner Island’), Ko Wang Nok (‘Outer Island’) and Ko Rap (‘Flat
L290@P12: Island’), as well as 5 small rock outcrops (Fig. 5A). It should be emphasized that
L291@P12: we exclude Ko Tha Rai from this field guide as it is far more west and extremely
L292@P12: close to the continental coast than the other islands which are more clustered. The
L293@P12: surface of the individual islands varies between 7.14 km2
L294@P12:  and 0.32 km2 whereas
L295@P12: the total seasurface on which they are spread is approximately 316 km2
L296@P12: . The total
L297@P12: coastline of the studied islands is approximately 42 km. The islands are situated
L298@P12: between 9°17’ and 9°23’ N latitude and 99°54’ and 99°59’ E longitude. The
L299@P12: highest island (Ko Taen) culminates at 214 m, whereas the lowest one (Ko Rap)
L300@P12: only reaches 43 m of heigth. Ko Taen is only 2 km away from Ko Samui (Fig.
L301@P12: 5B), whereas Ko Wang Nai, the furthermost one, is at 13 km from it and only 6
L302@P12: km from the continental coast. The middle island, Ko Rap is on equidistance (12
L303@P12: km) between the mainland and Ko Samui. They are all completely and densely

L320@P14: 2.3. Geography and geology
L321@P14: The coast of Khanom Mu Ko Tha Lae Tai is situated in the Gulf of Thailand. It is composed of a huge plain and connected to the open water of the
L322@P14: South China Sea. The huge plain consists of ridges and basins, and is known
L323@P14: as several large thick basins which provides several gas fields and petroleum
L324@P14: for the country. The Gulf of Thailand is known to emerge since Oligocene,
L325@P14: having a thick rock layer of over 8 000 meters. Geophysicists believe that there
L326@P14: was rifting in the Mesozoic Era and quickly lifting in the Quaternary Period.
L327@P14: They formed the rock floor during late Cretatious - Tertiary Period, resulting
L328@P14: in large tertiary basins.
