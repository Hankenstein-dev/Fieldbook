L15@P0: Abstract
L16@P0: The trebouxiophycean genus Phyllosiphon contains unique green algae that thrive as endophytic parasites in the leaves of
L17@P0: various members of the Araceae. The DNA sequences of the parasitic populations were originally acquired from infected
L18@P0: leaves of subtropical Arisarum vulgare. However, several previous studies showed that the members of the Phyllosiphon
L19@P0: clade also occur as free-living algae on various subaerial substrates across Europe. Phyllosiphon infection was also observed
L20@P0: on the leaves of the temperate genus Arum, but no molecular data was available for these parasites. We recently found these
L21@P0: parasites at a single sub-Mediterranean locality. These algae were genetically different from those previously obtained from
L22@P0: Arisarum leaves on the basis of their 18S rDNA and rbcL gene sequences. In the present study, we describe this organism as
L23@P0: a new species, Phyllosiphon ari. Phylogenetic differentiation of Phyllosiphon taxa, correlated with host specificity to different genera of the Araceae, suggests concerted host-pathogen co-evolution driving species diversification within this peculiar
L24@P0: green algal lineage.
L25@P0: Keywords: 18S rDNA, Arum, parasitic algae, Phyllosiphon, rbcL, subaerial microalgae, Trebouxiophyceae, Watanabea
L26@P0: clade
L27@P0: Introduction
L28@P0: Parasitic microorganisms form a considerable part of eukaryotic diversity. They have evolved from free-living ancestors
L29@P0: multiple times during eukaryotic evolution. However, some parasitic taxa transitioned back to a free-living lifestyle
L30@P0: from parasitism (Poulin 2007). Knowledge about the strength of a relationship between the host taxa and its parasite
L31@P0: helps us to understand the mechanisms that generate and maintain species diversity in parasitic taxa. Parasites are often
L32@P0: specialized to a single or a few host species (Poulin 2007).
L33@P0: The parasitic life strategy is relatively uncommon in phototrophic microorganisms. However, a few parasitic algae
L34@P0: have adapted to infecting not only other algae or animals, but also vascular plants. Interestingly, microalgae that infect
L35@P0: vascular plants belong exclusively to the division Chlorophyta (Smith 1933, Joubert & Rijkenberg 1971).
L36@P0: The chlorophyte order Trentepohliales represents futher green algal lineage thriving on vascular plants in
L37@P0: subaerial habitats, both epiphytically, endophytically and parasitically. The most known genera Trentepohlia and
L38@P0: Phycopeltis grows on tree bark or leaf surface of various plants (Guiry & Guiry 2016). This group also includes the
L39@P0: genus Stomatochroon that occupy the substomatal chambers of plants leaves in tropics, and the genus Cephaleuros
L40@P0: thriving in intercellular spaces of various plants in tropics and subtropics and cause leaf necrosis (Joubert & Rijkenberg
L41@P0: 1971). The division Chlorophyta also encompasses the parasitic genera Chlorochytrium and Rhorochytrium thriving in
L42@P0: intercellular spaces of various freshwater and terrestrial plants, respectively (Joubert & Rijkenberg 1971).
L43@P0: In this study, we focused on the genus Phyllosiphon, a little-known member of the trebouxiophycean Watanabea
L44@P0: clade with a parasitic lifestyle, which infect the leaves of various plants of the Araceae. The type species, P. arisari
L45@P0: J.G. Kühn, was described from leaves of Arisarum vulgare O. Targ. Tozz., growing in the Mediterranean region (Kühn
L46@P0: 1878). Five additional Phyllosiphon species were described from the leaves of five different aracean genera growing
L47@P0: in the tropics, such as Alocasia sp., Philodendron sp. (Lagerheim 1892), Zamioculcas zamiifolia (Tobler 1917), and
L48@P0: Anchomanes difformis (Mangenot 1948). However, Phyllosiphon infection was also morphologically observed on
L49@P0: leaves of other host taxa of the Araceae growing in temperate regions, such as Arisaema triphyllum (L.) Scott. in
L50@P0: North America (Collins 1909, Smith 1933) and Arum italicum Mill. (Nicolas 1912) and Arum maculatum L. in Eastern
L51@P0-1: France and Scotland (Maire 1908, Sowter 1949). The Phyllosiphon thalli are formed by branched filaments containing 144 • Phytotaxa 283 (2) © 2016 Magnolia Press PROCHÁZKOVÁ ET AL.
L52@P1: unicellular endospores, which penetrate the intercellular matrix of the leaf parenchyma and thus cause leaf necrosis.
L53@P1: This infection is macroscopically manifested by the formation of yellow-green spots on the leaves (Kühn 1878, Maire
L54@P1: 1908).
L55@P1: The only known DNA sequences of the parasitic members of the Phyllosiphon were acquired from infected
L56@P1: leaves of subtropical Arisarum vulgare (Aboal & Werner 2011, Procházková et al. 2015a). Interestingly, several recent
L57@P1: studies demonstrated that populations closely related to those obtained from parasitic populations thriving within the
L58@P1: leaves of A. vulgare also occur in various subaerial microhabitats throughout Europe as free-living microalgae (Cutler
L59@P1: et al. 2013, Hallmann et al. 2013, Procházková et al. 2015a). They thrive there as chlorelloid unicells with morphology
L60@P1: typical of the trebouxiophycean Watanabea clade (Procházková et al. 2015a).
L61@P1: The species and genera belonging to the phylogenetically defined Watanabea clade of Trebouxiophyceae occur
L62@P1: especially in various subaerial microhabitats. These unicellular chlorelloid microalgae typically reproduce by autospores
L63@P1: of unequal size. Three of four species of the genus Chloroidium have beeen frequently found in subaerial biofilms,
L64@P1: while a single species occurs in freshwater habitats (Darienko et al. 2010). The genera Kalinella and Heveochlorella
L65@P1: were described from corticolous biofilms in tropical South-East Asia (Zhang et al. 2008, Neustupa et al. 2009, Ma et
L66@P1: al. 2013). The genus Parachloroidium was recently described from corticolous samples in sub-Mediterranean habitats
L67@P1: (Neustupa et al. 2013b). Desertella, another recently described genus of the Watanabea clade, was found in desert
L68@P1: soil in Western USA (Fučíková et al. 2015). Futher genus Polulichloris was described from soil biofilms in Eastern
L69@P1: China (Song et al. 2015). Watanabea and Viridiella, two deep lineages of the Watanabea clade, occur in freshwater and
L70@P1: terrestrial, ofter very extreme, habitatats (Albertano et al. 1991, Hanagata et al. 1998, Fučíková et al. 2015). However,
L71@P1: this clade also containes several taxonomically undetermined DNA sequences that probably represent undescribed taxa
L72@P1: (Nyati et al. 2007, Kulichová et al. 2014).
L73@P1: During our recent investigations of algal leaf parasites thriving in leaves of the Araceae in Mediterranean Europe,
L74@P1: we discovered that Phyllosiphon populations that occur in the leaves of Arum italicum, as well as in corticolous
L75@P1: biofilms close to infected plants, form a new, previously unknown phylogenetic lineage within the genus. The main
L76@P1: purpose of this study is to describe this lineage as a new species of the genus Phyllosiphon, Phyllosiphon ari.
L77@P1: Materials and methods
L78@P1: Origin and cultivation of investigated strains
L79@P1: In total, 17 Phyllosiphon strains were isolated from infected leaves of Arisarum vulgare. At the same time, 10 strains
L80@P1: were isolated from infected leaves of Arum italicum and subaerial biofilms on tree bark growing in the vicinity of an
L81@P1: infected Arum population (Table 1). The strain CAUP H8803 isolated from the leaf of Arum italicum has been deposited
L82@P1: in the Culture Collection of Algae of Charles University Prague (CAUP) (http://botany.natur.cuni.cz/algo/caup.html).
L83@P1: Isolation and culturing techniques were performed as described by Procházková et al. (2015a). Clones from leaves
L84@P1: were isolated from approximatelly 3 mm2 of infected spots on Arisarum and Arum leaves under a stereomicroscope
L85@P1: Olympus SZ61 (Japan). The leaf sections containing the siphonal filaments with endospores inside were trasferred onto
L86@P1: agar-solidified BBM medium in Petri dishes. The endospores were released from these filaments after the addition
L87@P1: of liquid BBM medium. After one-week cultivations, these endospores were transformed into autosporangia. In this
L88@P1: stage, a piece of clonal colony from each Petri dish was used for DNA isolation. Another piece of the same cell colony
L89@P1: was isolated onto agar-solidified BBM medium in test tubes after 1 week.
L90@P1: The biofilm samples were isolated from approximatelly 1 cm2
L91@P1:  of tree bark surface taken from northern parts of
L92@P1: tree trunks at a heigh of approximatelly 150 cm above the soil surface. The phototrophic biofilm from each sample was
L93@P1: scraped using a sterille dissecting needle into 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube and vortex-mixed for 10s with 1.0 ml of sterile
L94@P1: liquid BBM medium and sterile glass beads (0.75 mm in diameter). Then, 40 µl of suspension from each Eppendorf
L95@P1: tube was placed onto agar-solidified BBM medium in Petri dishes. After 6 weeks, 2 to 4 algal microcolonies with
L96@P1: chlorelloid morphology were isolated onto agar-solidified BBM medium in test tubes.

L232@P2: DNA extraction, PCR, and sequencing
L233@P2: The genomic DNA of single algal colonies was isolated following the protocol described by Procházková et al. (2015a).
L234@P2-3: The extracted solution was diluted to 5–10 ng/μL and used for PCR. Two molecular markers were PCR-amplified 146 • Phytotaxa 283 (2) © 2016 Magnolia Press PROCHÁZKOVÁ ET AL.
L235@P3: from the genomic DNA: the plastid rbcL gene and the nuclear 18S rDNA. The rbcL gene was primarily amplified
L236@P3: from the strains using the nonspecific primers rbcL-203F (5′-GAATCWTCWACWGGWACTTGGACWAC-3′) and
L237@P3: rbcL-991R (5′-CCTTCTARTTTACCWACAAC-3′) as described in Nelsen et al. (2011). Consequently, the rbcL gene
L238@P3: was amplified both from the strains belonging to the genus Phyllosiphon acquired from the corticolous biofilms and
L239@P3: those isolated from samples of plant leaves using the primers phyllrbcLF (5′-TTCCGTATGACTCCACAACAAGG3′, Procházková et al. 2015a) or PRASF1 (5′-ATGGTTCCACAAACAGAAAC-3′, Sherwood et al. 2000) and ellaR2
L240@P3: (5′-TCACGACCTTCATTACGAGCTTG-3′, Neustupa et al. 2013). The 18S rDNA sequences were obtained from
L241@P3: Phyllosiphon strains with unique rbcL sequences using the primers 18S-F and18S-R as described in Katana et al.
L242@P3: (2001), or using the primer combination phy-F2 (5′-ACTGCGAATGGCTCATTAAATC-3′, Procházková et al. 2015a)
L243@P3: and 1636-57-R (5′-GGTAGGAGCGACGGGCGGTGTG-3′, Katana et al. 2001). The PCR mix was performed as
L244@P3: described in Procházková et al. (2015a). The amplification conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94°C for
L245@P3: 5/4 min (rbcL/18S rDNA); 40/35 cycles of denaturation at 95/94°C for 1 min /45 s, annealing at 50/47/54/52/54°C
L246@P3: (primer combination rbcL-203F and rbcL-991R/phyllrbcLF and ellaR2/PRASF1 and ellaR2/phyF2 and 1636-57-
L247@P3: R/18S-F and 18S-R) for 1/1.5min, and elongation at 72°C for 2/2.5 min; final extension at 72°C for 10 min. PCR
L248@P3: products were analysed by electrophoresis on 1 % agarose gel and stained with ethidium bromide. Correctly amplified
L249@P3: products were cleaned using the GenElute PCR Clean-Up Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s
L250@P3: protocol. The purified PCR products were sequenced with the amplification primers at Macrogen Inc. in Seoul, Korea.
L251@P3: Sequencing reads were assembled and edited using SeqAssem 09/2004 (Hepperle 2004). The unique 18S rDNA and
L252@P3: rbcL sequences of the species, described below as Phyllosiphon ari, are available in the GenBank database under the
L253@P3: accession numbers KU640390-KU640392 (Table 1).
L254@P3: Phylogenetic analyses
L255@P3: Newly determined rbcL and 18S rDNA sequences were added to alignments published by Procházková et al. (2015a)
L256@P3: and manually aligned with newly published sequences from the GenBank database using MEGA 6 (Tamura et al.
L257@P3: 2013). Two sequences of the Chlorellales were used as an outgroup. The final 18S rDNA alignment consisted of 1773
L258@P3: nucleotides and 1211 nucleotides for rbcL gene. Both alignments are available at http://botany.natur.cuni.cz/neustupa/
L259@P3: phyllosiphon_ari.html. The appropriate evolutionary models were determined using the Bayesian information criterion
L260@P3: (BIC) in MEGA. The BIC selected the GTR+G+I model for the entire 18S rDNA dataset and the 3rd codon position
L261@P3: of rbcL, the GTR+G model for 1st codon position of rbcL, and the JC+G+I model for 2nd codon position of rbcL.
L262@P3: Phylogenetic trees were inferred with Bayesian inference using MrBayes 3.2.2. (Ronquist et al. 2012). Two parallel
L263@P3: Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs were carried out for 2 million generations, each with one cold and three
L264@P3: heated chains. Analyses of the rbcL dataset were carried out using a partitioned dataset to assign distinct substitution
L265@P3: models to the codon positions. Parameters and trees were sampled every 100th generation for a total of 20 000 trees.
L266@P3: After visual inspection of log-likelihood values of sampled trees, the initial 5001 trees of each run were discarded and
L267@P3: posterior probabilities of tree bipartitions were calculated on the basis of the consensus of the remaining 30 000 (15 000
L268@P3: × 2) trees. The maximum likelihood (ML) and weighted maximum parsimony (wMP) analyses for bootstrap supports
L269@P3: of individual phylogenetic lineages were calculated using Garli 2.01 (Zwickl 2006) and PAUP 4.0b10 (Swofford
L270@P3: 2002), respectively. ML analyses consisted of 100 replicates, using default settings and the automatic termination set at
L271@P3: 100 000 generations, under the unpartitioned 18S rDNA and partitioned rbcL datasets. The wMP bootstrapping (1000
L272@P3: replicates) was performed using heuristic searches, with 1000 random sequence addition replicates, tree bisection,
L273@P3: and reconnection (TBR) swapping, and random addition of sequences (the number was limited to 10 000 for each
L274@P3: replicate), with gap characters treated as a fifth character state. The rescaled consistency index was used to assign
L275@P3: weight to the characters on a scale of 0–1000. New weights were based on the mean of the fit values for each character
L276@P3: over all of the trees in the memory. The phylogenetic trees were graphically adjusted in FigTree 1.3.1 (Rambaut 2009)
L277@P3: and Adobe Illustrator CS3.
