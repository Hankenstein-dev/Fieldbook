L70@P7: The degree of variation of life forms within a given ecosystem is
L71@P7: referred to as “biodiversity.” For centuries, scientists in many disciplines
L72@P7: have engaged in extensive exploration of the biodiversity of the Black
L73@P7: Sea. Initially, the Black Sea was considered relatively poor in species
L74@P7: richness compared to other seas. Often, the Black Sea was called the
L75@P7: ‘impoverished pocket’ of the Mediterranean Sea.
L76@P7: However, recent investigations had shown that there was a need to
L77@P7: seriously revise our perception of Black Sea biodiversity. Why? Many
L78@P7: exciting advances in the study of life forms have taken place in the last
L79@P7: decade, as new theoretical frameworks have been identified, and new
L80@P7: technologies to observe organisms in the field and the laboratory were
L81@P7: developed. Hence, new data were analysed leading to the discovery of
L82@P7: numerous previously unknown creatures for the Black Sea or correcting
L83@P7: the taxonomic descriptions of the familiar species.
L84@P7: The story of the Black Sea macroalgae and high plants was a little bit
L85@P7: different. First studies on the species composition and the distribution of
L86@P7: Black Sea macroalgae date back to the middle of the 18th century. Initially
L87@P7: about 10 different species were described (Gmelin, 1768; Gablicella,1785).
L88@P7: During the 19th century the number of known algal species for the
L89@P7: Black Sea grew to 100. S.A. Zernov, and N.V. Morozova-Vodyanizkaya
L90@P7: created the Black Sea collections of herbaria in the beginning of the
L91@P7: 20th century, and they were widely used by scientists to prepare
L92@P7: taxonomic descriptions of algae.
L93@P7: In the 1960s and 1970s, Black Sea benthic communities along the coasts
L94@P7: of Ukraine, Russian Federation, Georgia, Bulgaria and Romania had been
L95@P7: already thoroughly studied, variability and life cycle of species were
L96@P7: well known, their ecological niches and role within ecosystems were
L97@P7: identified. The results of numerous taxonomic investigations have been
L98@P7: summarized in the famous manual of A. D. Zinova (1967): ‘Guideline
L99@P7: for identification of green, brown and red algae of the USSR Southern
L100@P7: Seas’ (in Russian), where 277 different species of macroalgae have been
L101@P7: described in detail and presented schematically. In 1975 A.A. KaluginaGutnik published her prominent book ‘Phytobenthos of the Black Sea’
L102@P7: (in Russian), with wide-ranging taxonomic, biological, ecological, phytogeographical and other characteristics of Black Sea algal communities
L103@P7: and included a list of 292 species.
L104@P7: In the period from 2000 to 2010, numerous publications highlighted
L105@P7: the species richness along the Ukrainian and Turkish Black Sea coast,
L106@P7: and today the Black Sea check-list is comprised of 332 macroalgae and
L107@P7: high plants (Milchakova, 2007). In 2006, Daciana Sava published the
L108@P7: ‘Algele Macrofite’ in Romanian, where 35 common Black Sea macroalgae
L109@P7: were presented with respective photos. Yet up to the present, there was
L110@P7: no comprehensive, modern and well illustrated guide for Black Sea
L111@P7: flora and bottom vegetation identification, accompanied with concise
L112@P7: and update information on the ecology, distribution and value of the
L113@P7: species, which was readable by all nations around the Black Sea or
L114@P7: outside of the region.

L160@P9: INTRODUCTION
L161@P9: Very delicate and complicated relationships underlie the universe in
L162@P9: which marine plants and animals dwell; nowadays inhabitants of the
L163@P9: World Ocean too often surrender, being unable to withstand human pressures and consequent impacts.
L164@P9: Everyone who steps from the beach into the sea meets algae and seagrasses, those basic primary producers which account for proper functioning of the coastal marine ecosystem. Seaweeds and seagrasses are
L165@P9: coastal resources of inestimable value throughout the world. They create
L166@P9: stable habitats for many types of food chains that support the goods and
L167@P9: services of marine ecosystems (commercial species, recreation, utilization of land-based and ship-borne pollution, etc.). These marine plants
L168@P9: also stabilize bottom sediments thus preventing coastal erosion, and aid
L169@P9: in maintaining clear coastal waters since they buffer strong currents and
L170@P9: wave action. The extremely high biodiversity of seaweed communities
L171@P9: is well known. Besides, a variety of marine plants are used as food and
L172@P9: for chemical compounds required in many industries. The first records
L173@P9: about using seaweed and seagrass as medicinal agents were made about
L174@P9: two thousand years ago.
L175@P9: The greatest risks for disruption and extinction of marine plants are in
L176@P9: the shallow coastal zones which, along with estuaries, are the places
L177@P9: where most of the high concentrations of population occur in the world.
L178@P9: This is where shipping and port activities take place, where large rivers
L179@P9: end after draining vast areas of agricultural activities, and where largest
L180@P9: tourist resorts are located. The loads resulting from human activities,
L181@P9: marine traffic and tourism coupled with the global climate change have
L182@P9: disturbed the coastal seaweed and seagrass communities significantly
L183@P9: in the long-term run. Regrettably, many countries ignore the ecological
L184@P9: importance of the coastal zone and abandon any activity that suggests
L185@P9: conservation of the seaweed beds. The recent oil blowout and spill in the
L186@P9: Gulf of Mexico has demonstrated how human activity may kill off marine
L187@P9: life; the most gravely endangered are semi-enclosed seas, e.g., the Black
L188@P9: Sea, which is one of the worst polluted seas in the world. Therefore, preserving and conserving the diversity of the Black Sea life is a challenge
L189@P9: of special importance.
L190@P9: Though key species of marine plants and their natural habitats in the
L191@P9: Black Sea have been placed under protection of regional and national
L192@P9: laws and international conventions, too many underwater communities
L193@P9: along the shoreline are still affected by degradation. Marine protected
L194@P9: areas (MPAs) have proved efficient in preserving, restoring and maintaining the biological diversity but at present their total area is not large. Their
L195@P9: expansion through the Black Sea net of MPAs acquires high priority in
L196@P9: nature conservation.
L197@P9: Unacceptably, present legislation level of designation of MPAs fail to
L198@P9: adequately preserve the diversity of marine organisms. Besides, the
L199@P9: general public awareness is low, people are not well challenged to acquire
L200@P9-10: and enlarge their knowledge of plants and animals inhabiting the sea, their 9
L201@P10: life cycles and natural habitats. Marine biologists should better communicate their research on underwater life to public–today the number of
L202@P10: illustrated manuals describing seaweeds and marine life is insufficient.
L203@P10: This Guide was written to bridge this gap and acquaint the reader with
L204@P10: common seaweed and seagrasses of the Black Sea.
L205@P10: Chapters of this book describe the Black Sea, marine plants growing on
L206@P10: the sea floor, commercial use and protection and techniques which are
L207@P10: applied to prepare a seaweed herbarium. The most extensive part of the
L208@P10: Guide describes 74 macroscopic marine plants which are widespread
L209@P10: along the shorelines of the Black Sea and form extensive beds and communities. They belong to the Kindom Plantae (Phylum Chlorophyta,
L210@P10: Rhodophyta and Magnoliophyta) and Kindom Chromista (Phylum
L211@P10: Heterokontophyta).
L212@P10: Species names are cited in alphabetic order and in concordance with the
L213@P10: recent taxonomic revisions and nomenclature alterations synonyms are
L214@P10: also given. The descriptions include details of the taxonomy, morphology, ecology, vegetation, reproduction, distribution and economic use.
L215@P10: In compiling this Guide, the author used her own materials and data as well
L216@P10: as thematic handbooks, monographs and other references. Almost each
L217@P10: species is illustrated with underwater photos that permit identification of
L218@P10: the marine plant. Most of the photos are from the archives of the Laboratory
L219@P10: of Phytoresources (Institute of Biology of the Southern Seas, IBSS).
L220@P10: Annexes to the Guide include a list of researchers working in different
L221@P10: fields of marine biology, an MPAs inventory for the Black Sea, the pertinent international conventions and agreements on preservation of the
L222@P10: biological and landscape diversity. The glossary explains special scientific
L223@P10: terms. The names and publications of all those who contributed to the
L224@P10: general topics of the Guide are included in the references section.
L225@P10: The author was fortunate enough to had been taught by friendly and
L226@P10: competent tutors in marine biology and to work in cooperation with
L227@P10: colleagues who are as enthusiastic as I am. As a student of Rostov-on-Don
L228@P10: State University, I repeatedly went to IBSS, one of the oldest European
L229@P10: marine research institutes. Later, in this Institute I have specialized in
L230@P10: biology and the ecology of the Black Sea marine plants.
L231@P10: Some of the results obtained are included in this Guide and may be of use
L232@P10: to algologists, ecologists, hydrobiologists, specialists in nature protection
L233@P10: management, students, and to anyone sincerely interested in marine life.

L260@P13: A BRIEF ENVIRONMENTAL SURVEY OF THE BLACK SEA
L261@P13: Morphometry and geomorphology
L262@P13: The Black Sea is a semi-closed sea separating Europe from Asia Minor.
L263@P13: The Kerch Strait connects the Black Sea with the Sea of Azov while
L264@P13: the Straits of Bosporus and Dardanelles connect to the Mediterranean
L265@P13: Sea. The total area of the Black Sea is 423,000 km2
L266@P13:  and the maximum
L267@P13: north-to-south distance is approximately 580 km. The greatest depth is
L268@P13: 2,245 m and the mean depth is 1,240 m.
L269@P13: The shoreline of the Black Sea is 4,125 km. The longest coasts,
L270@P13: 1,450 km and 1,330 km, are in Turkey and in Ukraine, respectively.
L271@P13: Others are Russia (410 km), Bulgaria (380 km), Georgia (315 km) and
L272@P13: Romania (240 km). The west and north-western of the Black Sea has
L273@P13: no mountains and uplifts. There is the Caucasian mountains on the east
L274@P13: coast, the Crimean mountain ridge to the north and the mountainous terrain of Asia Minor to the south. The coastline is more or less smooth and
L275@P13: indented only in the north and in the northwestern.
L276@P13: The largest gulfs in the Black Sea are Karkinitsky, Kalamitsky, Feodosiysky, Odessa, Varna, Burgas, Sinop and Samsun. The largest bays are
L277@P13: of Sevastopol and Novorossiysk. In the north, the Crimean peninsula
L278@P13: protrudes far into the sea. The Black Sea is not rich with islands. The
L279@P13: largest is Dzarilgatch (62 km2) and all the rest are small, e.g., Berezan
L280@P13: and Zmeiny (Snake) islets are less than 1 km2 each. The coastal reliefs
L281@P13: vary from the prevailing abrasive and accumulative to occasional ria and
L282@P13: lagoon-type stretches.
L283@P13: Characteristic features of an abrasive coast are a high steep cliff, narrow beach and 150–200 m wide rocky sloping bench. The accumulative
L284@P13: submarine terrace of abrasive rock debris often rests against the outward
L285@P13: side of the bench.
L286@P13-14: The general map of the Black Sea and regions13
L287@P14: The abrasive coast and rocky bench slope (mt Tuapkhat, Caucasus)
L288@P14: The submarine bench usually divides into pebble and boulder surf zones,
L289@P14: the mid-part where large coarse predominates, and the lower part of broken natural stone and gruss lying from 2 to 20 m deep. Loose silty-sand
L290@P14: sediments are found below 20m.
L291@P14: The coastal reliefs vary from the prevailing abrasive and accumulative
L292@P14: to occasional ria and lagoon-type stretches. Characteristic features of an
L293@P14: abrasive coast are a high steep cliff, narrow beach and 150–200 m wide
L294@P14: rocky sloping bench.
L295@P14: The share of abrasive shores aggregates to 60%. Beaches, submarine and
L296@P14: coastal banks and coastal sand-bars are most typical of the accumulative
L297@P14: coasts at river deltas and coastal salt lake mouths. The ria coasts are rich
L298@P14: in numerous sinuous gulfs and bays, e. g. Sevastopol Bay, and the lagoon
L299@P14: seashore – in coastal lakes, estuaries, sand spits and earth embankments
L300@P14: that is characteristic of Dzharilgatsky and Yagorlitsky gulfs.
L301@P14: Coasts of different types are unevenly distributed along the lithologic
L302@P14: contour of the sea. In the northwestern and western parts they are mainly
L303@P14: accumulative or abrasive-landslide and lagoon, richly indented with large
L304@P14: coastal lakes and lagoons with long sand spits and banks. The sea there
L305@P14: is shallow and the submarine relief uniformly flat. The southwestern
L306@P14: and southern coasts of Crimea descend steeply into the sea where the
L307@P14: underwater relief is surprisingly diverse. The Crimean coast is mainly
L308@P14: abrasive, with many rocky capes, small bays, coves and pebble-gravel
L309@P14: beaches. Numerous near-shore kekurs are elevated above the sea surface.
L310@P14: Accumulative forms are very infrequent and fluvial sediments settle to
L311@P14: the sea floor at considerable depths. Only a few small coastal lakes and
L312@P14: lagoons may be found along the coast of the Kerch Strait. In the northeastern part of the Black Sea the coast is usually contour ridge bench
L313@P14: built of sedimentary rock in which elevations alternate with crevices and
L314@P14: clefts. The long coast of Turkey is abrasive-denudational and abrasive
L315@P14: nearly everywhere, with high steep cliffs. The submarine slope harbours
L316@P14-15: numerous rocks and kekurs. The abrasive and landslip coasts are characteristic of the Bulgarian sector of the Black Sea; one-third of the coastal 14

L346@P17: The submarine relief of the Black Sea
L347@P17: zone are beaches, wide and narrow. Accumulative shores are relatively
L348@P17: few, e. g. in the Gulf of Varna. The Romanian sector is remarkable for
L349@P17: the Danube’s delta, one of the largest in Europe. The strip of lagoons and
L350@P17: predominantly abrasive seashore lies southward.
L351@P17: Shelf zone
L352@P17: The shelf occupies 29.9% of the total area of the Black Sea. The shallow
L353@P17: (less than 200 m deep) continental shelf in the northwestern part makes
L354@P17: up about 25% of the total area of the sea. Coastal zones of Crimea, the
L355@P17: Caucasus and the southern part of the Black Sea adjoin very narrow shelf
L356@P17: zones often intersected by submarine canyons.
L357@P17: The width and structure of the shelf differ depending upon the location.
L358@P17: The northwestern shelf is as wide as 190 km. In the steep-coasted southern and eastern parts it narrows to 20 km and is intersected by numerous
L359@P17: submarine canyons and depressions. Abrupt continental slope is characteristic of the northeastern Black Sea and the Crimean peninsula, where
L360@P17: the sea is 500 m deep a few kilometers of the shore off. Typical features
L361@P17: of these areas are boulder-and-block deposits 20-30-m high or shoals
L362@P17: situated at a large distance from the shore. There are steep and narrow
L363@P17: submarine canyons with the 15–25 m walls.

L365@P17: prevail; farther from the shore coarse deposits give way to fine-grained
