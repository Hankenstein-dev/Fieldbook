L71@P2: RARE SPECIES IN THE RHODODENDRON GARDEN.
L72@P2: The National Rhododendron Garden at Olinda has around 400 different Asiatic
L73@P2: species.(including species, sub-species, and varieties) This is a world-class collection and it is
L74@P2: important to maintain these, particularly the rarer species, because it is becoming almost
L75@P2: impossible to import plants from overseas..
L76@P2: With Global warming becoming a reality, we need to take extra care of our rarest plants, and if
L77@P2: possible propagate them from cuttings so we do not lose them entirely.
L78@P2:  bayleyi—Subseries Bayleyana
L79@P2: A compact shrub in the lepidote group, this has narrow foliage which is densely scaly underneath.
L80@P2: The flowers are purple and freely produced in Spring.
L81@P2: From Sikkim, Bhutan, and S.Tibet at 2400-4300 m.
L82@P2: Only one plant in the Garden.
L83@P2:  championae—Series Chionastrum.
L84@P2: The species in this section are all from relatively warm climates and generally do well at Olinda.
L85@P2: This is a leggy plant with bristly petioles and leaves. The funnel-shaped flowers are white or pink
L86@P2: with yellow spots.
L87@P2: From S,China and Hong Kong
L88@P2: Two plants in the Garden.
L89@P2:  concinnum var. pseudoyanthinum—Triflorum Series.
L90@P2: We had trouble identifying this plant which had no tag and was growing in the Triflora bed.
L91@P2: Fortunately, there is only one species in the Triflora section with red flowers, and this proved to
L92@P2: be the selected form mentioned in the R.H.S.Handbook. The leaves are similar to other Triflora
L93@P2: and the flowers are a bright ruby-red. It should be a good parent for lepidote hybrids.
L94@P2: From West and Central China, 1500-4400 m.
L95@P2:  excellens—Series Maddenia, Dalhousiae Alliance.
L96@P2: A recently introduced species from S.Yunnan and N.Vietnam. This is a tall shrub with bullate
L97@P2: leaves, like a smaller version of nuttallii. The flowers are white, trumpet-shaped, and perfumed.
L98@P2: (Bullate – blistered appearance, puckered).
L99@P2: Three plants in the Garden.
L100@P2:  glischroides—Glischra Series.
L101@P2: This species has interesting foliage; dark green, bullate, and with intermittent thin indumentum.
L102@P2: The flowers are white, flushed pink, with a maroon blotch.
L103@P2: From N.E.Upper Burma, 3000-3700 m.
L104@P2: Only one plant in the Garden.
L105@P2: lanatum—Lanata Subsection.
L106@P2-3: This species is a little gem, usually growing to less than 1 metre. The leaves have a thick brown 4
L107@P3: indumentum and the flowers are pale yellow with red spots. Said to be difficult to grow in
L108@P3: England but ours are doing well so far.
L109@P3: From Sikkim and Bhutan, 3000-4300 m.
L110@P3: Two plants in the bank below the glasshouse.
L111@P3: ochraceum—Maculifera Subsection.
L112@P3: A recently introduced species from China, this has excellent foliage with a woolly yellow-brown
L113@P3: indumentum, and deep red flowers.
L114@P3: From Sichuan and Yunnan, 1700-3000 m.
L115@P3: Only two plants in the Garden.
L116@P3:  piercei—Neriiflora Subsection.
L117@P3: Rare in the wild, this is a low spreading shrub to 1 metre, with dark green glossy leaves and
L118@P3: intense blood-red flowers.
L119@P3: From S.E.Tibet, 3700-4000 m.
L120@P3: Only two plants in the Garden, just below the Azalea species bed.
L121@P3:  recurvoides. – subseries Glischra. [photo page 10]
L122@P3: A compact shrub to 1 metre. Excellent foliage, glossy above with rich cinnamon indumentum
L123@P3: below. The stems, petioles, and leaves covered in stiff bristles. The flowers are pink spotted with
L124@P3: crimson.
L125@P3: From upper Burma, 3500 m.
L126@P3: Only four plants, in the Maddenia walk.
L127@P3:  venator—Venatora Subsection.
L128@P3: The only species in this Subsection, this is a medium-sized shrub with smooth leaves and bloodred flowers.
L129@P3: From S.E.Tibet, Tsangpo Gorge, 2400-2600 m.
L130@P3: Three plants in the Garden, one near the show-hall, and two in the Maddenia Walk.
L131@P3:  traillianum var. dictyotum.—Taliensia Subsection.
L132@P3: Like most of the Taliensia group,this species has excellent glossy green leaves with a brown
L133@P3: indumentum.The flowers are white, sometimes flushed with pink. The Taliensias are generally
L134@P3: slow-growing plants from high altitudes but do well in moderate shade.
L135@P3: From Tibet, Sichuan, and Yunnan, 3400-4300 m.
L136@P3: Three plants near the Maddenia Walk.
L137@P3: Propagation of these plants from cuttings is already underway with the aid of the fog propagator.

L140@P4: VIREYA SPECIES COLUMN
L141@P4: R rarilepidotum [photo page 10]
L142@P4: Section VII: Euvireya- Subsection v: Euvireya [according to the classification proposed by Dr.
L143@P4: George Argent rhododendrons of Subgenus Vireya RHS 2006]. This subsection is a very big one
L144@P4: of 88 species covering a very wide geographical area from mainland Asia, through Malaysia,
L145@P4: Sumatra, Java, Borneo, the Philippines, Sulawesi and Maluku, New Guinea to Australia. The
L146@P4: Subsection includes the two Australian species lochiae and viriosum .
L147@P4: Name
L148@P4: A mixture of Latin, rari – rare or seldom, and Greek, lepidotum –covered with small scales.
L149@P4: Argent surmises the name derives from the fact that the scales easily fall of so that the leaves are
L150@P4: glabrescent. He thinks that the name is not particularly apt.
L151@P4: Description [taken, though not completely, from Argent pp. 243-244]
L152@P4: Shrub to 4m. This species was first introduced into cultivation by John Dransfield in 1975 and it
L153@P4: flowered at Kew in November 1983. It has been in cultivation in Edinburgh since 1981. The
L154@P4: original collections had dark reddish orange flowers and were scented. This species was quite
L155@P4: widely held in Australia before Murray McAllister and Bill Taylor collected species in New
L156@P4: Zealand in 2003. I know Geraldine Roelink has this species [from where mine derived] and so, I
L157@P4: assume, Brian Clancy also has it. One of my plants is flowering now [a single umbel] and is red
L158@P4: rather than orange. One of Andrew Rouse’s plants is covered in flowers now. They are distinctly
L159@P4: orange. Neither set of flowers is scented. The umbel is supposed to have 6-12 flowers. Andrew’s
L160@P4: plant is in this range. My umbel has 13 flowers. I deduce R rarilepidotum is summer flowering in
L161@P4: Olinda. In its place of origin it flowers from December to January.
L162@P4: Leaves 3-7 in loose pseudowhorls, blade 50-90 x 20-35mm elliptic, apex shortly acuminate,
L163@P4: margin entire, flat. Petiole 7-18 x 3-4mm smooth or grooved distally, scaly.
L164@P4: Corolla 20-25 x 35-40 mm broadly funnel-shaped, orange often darker at the base of the tube, or
L165@P4: yellow, sometimes scented; tube 12-20 x 8-9 x 16-18 mm .
L166@P4: Origin
L167@P4: Indonesia; Sumatra, Gajo Lands; East Coast; West Coast, Mt. Kaba. In subalpine shrubberies and
L168@P4: on open volcanic areas, mostly terrestrial. From 1000-2500m.
L169@P4: Hybrids
L170@P4: Argent notes R x ootrichum, a natural hybrid of R rarilepidotum with R sumatranum . I also think
L171@P4: I recall that Geraldine Roelink had some R rarilepidotum hybrids.
L172@P4: Verdict
L173@P4: Argent says ‘ As a species it grows vigorously as a compact, medium sized shrub, flowering
L174@P4: irregularly but very freely usually in spring or autumn, and is a very worthwhile horticultural
L175@P4: subject’. My first plant is still small but fits the description so far, save for the flowering time. I
L176@P4: have a number of smaller plants some from the Society’s Vireya Bank and cuttings therefrom. On
L177@P4: limited acquaintance a definite tick of approval.

L230@P6: RHODODENDRON LAPPONICUM
L231@P6: [According to the RHS Handbook, 1998 R lapponicum is a prostate to erect shrub to 1m flowers
L232@P6: 3-6 with corolla violet-rose to purple, sometimes white in March- April in its Artic habitat. It is
L233@P6: found all around the Arctic Circle, USA (Alaska), Canada, Greenland, Scandinavia and Arctic
L234@P6: Russia. Ed.]
L235@P6: Rhododendron lapponicum, as its name implies, is from Lapland. It sounds intriguing, a
L236@P6: rhododendron found in the Arctic. A major temperature difference from plants found in the
L237@P6: tropics!
L238@P6: [A websearch leads to Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. According to it, Lapland can refer to:
L239@P6: *The official English name of the Province of Lapland in Finland
L240@P6: * The English name of Swedish Lapland (the name is not in official use)
L241@P6: *The entire cultural region inhabited by the Sami people minority in Northern Europe
L242@P6: It can also refer to Norwegian Lapland or Russian Lapland Ed.]
L243@P6: I was asked to give a talk at the AGM of the Canadian Rhododendron Society at St Johns,
L244@P6: Newfoundland, and then to join a group which was interested to look for R. lapponicum in the
L245@P6: North of the island of Newfoundland.
L246@P6: In St Johns Botanical Garden, we were shown plants of R. lapponicum collected from various
L247@P6: areas in Newfoundland. It really is an insignificant plant, a few 200 mm high, with a purple
L248@P6: funnel shaped flower.
L249@P6: From St Johns we travelled from the east to the west coast, some 500 km on the Trans Canadian
L250@P6: Route. Passing many wet boggy areas from melting snow we found large drifts of the rose purple
L251@P6: flower of the azalea, R. canadense, a single flower stem not much to write home about, but en
L252@P6: masse, most impressive.
L253@P6: \
L254@P6: On Plum Point, 200 km into the Arctic Circle, we found the first small patch of R. lapponicum
L255@P6: growing in very thin soil on limestone rock. The population was about 100 plants, with evidence
L256@P6: that a large number of plants had succumbed during the previous winter.
L257@P6: At Woody Point, we stopped to view an area where there was very little plant life and the acid air
L258@P6-7: stank with sulphur fumes. The soil was very thin on serpentine rock, mixed with iron pyrites and 8
L259@P7: acid. Nevertheless, we were surprised to find plants struggling to grow of rhododendron, willow
L260@P7: and juniper.
L261@P7: Further north around Boat Harbour, we found patches of R. lapponicum in flower hugging the
L262@P7: sides of mossy banks. What with watery eyes from very chilly winds and the smallness of the
L263@P7: rhododendron plants, we had to look twice to identify these plants.
L264@P7: At the most northerly end of the island, a cape juts out into the Atlantic Ocean. Here, we found a
L265@P7: large patch of the rhododendron. To find the flower required close scrutiny as the plants were
L266@P7: camouflaged with a covering of dead moss and grass 50mm high.
L267@P7: Besides Rhododendron, the trip was full of interest in seeing large areas of dwarf shrubs of birch,
L268@P7: willow, cornus and kalmia coming into flower. Most of these plants were no more than 250mm
L269@P7: high. Off the coast line were ice bergs floating on a smooth sea. A trip to be remembered.

L272@P7: NATIONAL RHODODENDRON GARDENS, OLINDA, 1967-1970
L273@P7: [Ralph Sangster’s historical narrative continued from the September 2007 Newsletter pp14-20.
L274@P7: Ed.]
L275@P7: With the finishing of the construction of the Hall and surrounds in 1967 the Planning Committee
L276@P7: did not rest on their laurels. They still thought to expand the Garden from the first 16 acres into
L277@P7: the next 50 acre block, i.e. about half the area allocated by the Government as a rhododendron
L278@P7: park.
L279@P7: The first step in expanding was the clearing of the new area of burnt scrub, bracken and stones
L280@P7: during 1967/68 by volunteer labour. In clearing, it became obvious that to balance the landscape
L281@P7: of the new area that the existing boundary of the creek line between the Garden and the Golf
L282@P7: Links needed to be shifted to take in part of the steep slope of the Golf Links. Negotiations
L283@P7: between the two Committees of Management were friendly and the common boundary between
L284@P7: bodies shifted approximately 3 chains up the slope of the Golf Links
L285@P7: This move allowed the Society to use both banks of the creek area as the first area designated for
L286@P7: species. The first planting of the large leaf rhododendron seedlings were planted in this area. We
L287@P7: obtained, from Boulters Nursery, a large number of their throw- out of hybrid rhododendron
L288@P7: stock plants and this collection of plants was planted along the top edge of the new area running
L289@P7: along the Olinda Forest Road.
L290@P7: All young plants need water. At first it was by buckets of water drawn from a pool at the creek
L291@P7: head. When temporary electric power was installed to the site, the pool was made into a small
L292@P7: dam, an electric pump installed to pump water to the top end of the Garden, which allowed a
L293@P7-8: limited reticulation system for the azalea bowl and nursery.9
L294@P8: In 1968 with the expansion into 50 acres, we started to think big of a watering system which
L295@P8: would cope, not only of the existing plants but for future requirements of the whole area allocated
L296@P8: for the Garden. The plan was to build a dam of such capacity to cope with summer demands of
L297@P8: young plants, a 5,000 gallon capacity tank installed as a head tank in the top forest area, and a
L298@P8: high pressure pump of 2,000 gallons per hour capacity. A reticulation system, drawing water
L299@P8: from the head tank, consisting of a network of plastic piping with overhead sprinklers, was
L300@P8: installed throughout the Garden.
L301@P8: The idea of a large capacity water dam which would ensure a good supply of water for fighting
L302@P8: bush fires in the area of Olinda was sold to the Government. Money for the project became
L303@P8: available from the Government on the basis of one part from the Society to four from
L304@P8: Government sources. From the Garden landscape point of view, the proposed large area of water
L305@P8: would provide a focal point in its landscape.
L306@P8: With the Government being prepared to allocate money for specific development projects, this
L307@P8: changed the Society’s view point of doing little by little to one of being able to plan boldly ahead
L308@P8: in time and to develop an orderly development program.
L309@P8: The year of 1968 was the end of Chapter 1 of the Garden development. A chapter which fulfilled
L310@P8: the boast of the Society’s founders “we can make a major botanic garden without any assistance
L311@P8: from the Government”.
L312@P8: Ralph Sangster
