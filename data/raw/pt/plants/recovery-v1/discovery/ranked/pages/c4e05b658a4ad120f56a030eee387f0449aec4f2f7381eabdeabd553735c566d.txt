L221: Allium ampeloprasum is a member of the cite104†onion genus cite98†Allium . The wild plant is commonly known as wild leek or broadleaf wild leek. Its native range includes southern Europe, southwestern Asia and North Africa, but it has been cultivated and cite105†naturalized in many other countries.
L222: 
L223: Allium ampeloprasum has been differentiated into five cultivated vegetables: cite106†leek , cite107†elephant garlic , cite108†pearl onion , kurrat, and Persian leek.
L224: ## Etymology
L225: 
L226: [cite109†edit ]
L227: 
L228: The specific epithet ampeloprasum derives from the Greek ampelos ("vine") and prason ("leek"), literally meaning "vine-leek" or "leek of the vineyard".^{cite110†[3] }
L229: ## Description
L230: 
L231: [cite111†edit ]
L232: 
L233: Wild populations produce bulbs up to 3 centimetres (1+1⁄4 inches) across. cite112†Scapes are round in cross-section, each up to 180 cm (71 in) tall, bearing an cite113†umbel of as many as 500 flowers. Flowers are urn-shaped, up to 6 millimetres (1⁄4 inch) across; cite114†tepals white, pink or red; cite115†anthers yellow or purple; cite116†pollen yellow.^{cite117†[4] }^{cite118†[5] }
L234: ## Distribution and habitat
L235: 
L236: [cite119†edit ]
L237: Allium ampeloprasum is an ecologically diverse species complex and the progenitor of several key cultivated varieties, including the common leek (A. porrum L. sensu stricto), characterized by its robust "pseudostem," and the A. kurrat Schweinf, which is grown for its slender leaves. Despite their physical differences, these forms remain closely related to, and fully interfertile with, the wild tetraploid A. ampeloprasum, which is widely distributed in the Mediterranean basin.^{cite120†[6] }
L238: The plant's cite121†native range is southern Europe to southwestern Asia and North Africa,^{cite103†[2] } including all countries bordering the cite122†Black , cite123†Adriatic , and cite124†Mediterranean Seas from Portugal to Egypt to Romania. In Russia and Ukraine, it is considered invasive except in cite125†Crimea , where it is native. It is also native to Ethiopia, Uzbekistan, Iran and Iraq.
L239: It is considered naturalized in the United Kingdom, Ireland, the Czech Republic, the cite126†Baltic States , Belarus, the cite127†Azores , Madeira, the cite128†Canary Islands , Armenia, Azerbaijan, Afghanistan, China, Australia (all states except cite129†Queensland and cite130†Tasmania ), Mexico, the Dominican Republic, cite131†Puerto Rico , Haiti, the United States (southeastern region plus cite132†California , cite133†New York State , cite134†Ohio and cite135†Illinois ), cite136†Galápagos , and Argentina.^{cite103†[2] }^{cite117†[4] }^{cite137†[7] }^{cite138†[8] }^{cite139†[9] }
L240: The species may have been introduced to Britain by prehistoric people, where its habitat consists of rocky places near the coast in south-west England and Wales.^{cite140†[10] }^{cite141†[11] }
L241: ## Conservation
L242: 
L243: [cite142†edit ]
L244: 
L245: The plant is protected by law in cite143†Israel ^{cite144†[12] } as well as cite145†York County , cite146†Virginia , where it is commonly known as the "cite147†Yorktown onion".^{cite148†[13] }
L246: ## Cultivation
L247: 
L248: [cite149†edit ]
L249: 
L250: Allium ampeloprasum is the source of several vegetables, most notably:
L251:   * cite106†Leek (var. porrum)
L252:   * cite107†Elephant garlic or great-headed garlic (var. ampeloprasum)
L253:   * cite108†Pearl onion (var. sectivum)
L254:   * Kurrat (var. kurrat),^{cite140†[10] }^{cite150†[14] } Egyptian leek or salad leek. This variety has small bulbs, and primarily the leaves are eaten.
L255:   * Persian leek (A. ampeloprasum ssp. persicum). A cultivated allium native to the Middle East and Iran, grown for culinary purposes and called tareh in Persian. The linear green leaves have a mild onion flavor and are eaten raw, either alone, or in food combinations.^{cite151†[15] }
L256: Some sources (especially archeological ones) refer to each of these as a separate species,^{cite152†[16] } but they are now united as A. ampeloprasum.
