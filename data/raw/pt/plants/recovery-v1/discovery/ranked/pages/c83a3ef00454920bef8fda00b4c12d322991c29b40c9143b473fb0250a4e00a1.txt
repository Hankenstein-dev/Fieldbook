L209: ## Abstract
L210: The genus Allium has been extensively studied in recent decades to elucidate the evolutionary processes shaping its phylogeny and taxonomic relationships. However, significant gaps remain, particularly concerning infrageneric classification and evolutionary relationships within several sections. One such example is section Codonoprasum, comprising approximately 160 diploid and polyploid taxa centred mainly in the Mediterranean and western Irano–Turanian regions.
L211: High species diversity in this section is accompanied by taxonomic inconsistencies and a poorly resolved phylogenetic structure. Here, we improve current understanding of the evolution of section Codonoprasum by analysing sequence variation in three genomic loci (nrITS, trnH–psbA, and trnL–ndhJ) and by mapping selected plant traits onto the resulting phylogenetic trees. Our dataset includes 48 taxa—around 30% of the total diversity of the section—each represented by multiple accessions.
L212: The results support the monophyly of section Codonoprasum and indicate a close relationship with section Cupanioscordum. Within section Codonoprasum, five major lineages (clades I–V) were identified, some of which were further subdivided into morphologically and/or karyologically distinct subclades. The placement of taxa within these lineages is evaluated in the light of existing knowledge on their morphology, karyology, and geographic distribution.
L213: Overall, the phylogenetic patterns recovered here are incongruent with traditional subsectional taxonomy. Genome size appears to follow a more punctuated than gradual mode of evolution along the phylogeny. We interpret these findings with a focus on lineage composition, interclade relationships, and their implications for understanding evolutionary patterns and taxonomy within this diverse section.
L214: Allium, character evolution, genome size, infrageneric classification, nrITS, molecular phylogeny, plastid DNA, taxonomy
L215: ## INTRODUCTION
L216: The genus Allium L. comprises more than 1000 species (POWO 2026), making it one of the largest monocotyledonous genera. Its major centre of diversity extends from the Mediterranean Basin to central and north-eastern Asia, with a secondary, less pronounced centre in North America (Dale and Jacobsen 2002, Fritsch and Friesen 2002, Li et al. 2010).
L217: Early phylogenetic studies based on the nuclear ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region and several plastid (cpDNA) loci identified three major evolutionary lines containing 15 subgenera and 72 sections (Friesen et al. 2006, Li et al. 2010). Over the past 15 years, similar approaches have provided valuable insights into evolutionary processes and taxonomic relationships within selected subgenera—e.g. subgenus Melanocrommyum (Webb & Berth.) Rouy (Gurushidze et al. 2008, 2010, Fritsch et al.
L218: 2010, Ergashov et al. 2025), subgenus Anguinum (G.Don ex W.D.J.Koch) N.Friesen (Li et al. 2016, Yang et al. 2023), and subgenus Amerallium Traub (Friesen et al. 2024)—as well as within particular sections, such as section Cepa (Mill.) Prokh. (Gurushidze et al. 2007), section Allium (Hirschegger et al. 2010), and section Rhizirideum G.Don ex W.D.J.Koch (Sinitsyna et al. 2016). Several geographically focused studies, including those from western North America (Nguyen et al.
L219: 2008), Korea (Choi and Oh 2011), China (Li et al. 2010), and Iran (Veiskarami et al. 2019), have contributed further insights. The most comprehensive phylogeny of Allium to date was published by Han et al. (2020), who analysed 448 species using eight plastid loci and one nuclear locus to explore the role of polyploidy in species diversification. Collectively, these studies substantially extend the knowledge acquired through earlier morphological, anatomical, and molecular research (see e.g. Hanelt et al.
L220: 1992, Friesen et al. 2006, Li et al. 2010 for reviews), building on the first and still the only comprehensive monograph by Regel (1875). Despite this progress, substantial gaps remain in infrageneric taxonomy, and evolutionary relationships among subgenera, sections, and species within the main evolutionary lineages remain insufficiently resolved (Zhang et al. 2024).
L221: Allium section Codonoprasum Rchb. (hereafter section Codonoprasum), part of the largest subgenus Allium within the third evolutionary lineage, represents a remarkable example of high species diversity, inconsistent taxonomy, and poorly resolved phylogenetic relationships (Friesen et al. 2006, Salmeri et al. 2016, Duchoslav et al. 2020, 2026). The section has its centre of diversity in the Mediterranean and the western part of the Irano–Turanian region (e.g.
L222: Cheshmedzhiev 1975a, 1975b, 1975c, Zahariadi 1975, Wilde-Duyfjes de 1976, Stearn 1981, Kollmann 1984, Özhatay 1984, 1990, 1993, Pastor and Valdés 1985, Tzanoudakis and Vosa 1988, Brullo et al. 1996a, 1996b, 1997a, 1997b, 1998, 2007, Hanelt 1996, Trigas et al. 2010, 2017, Özhatay and Koçyiğit 2019, Trigas and Bareka 2020, Brullo and Salmeri 2021, Güner et al. 2023). The section is burdened by an exceptionally large number of published names, with new species still being added.
L223: It currently comprises ∼160 accepted species (POWO 2026), including many polyploid taxa (Kobrlová et al. 2024).
L224: Although the section is considered morphologically relatively homogeneous and well differentiated from most other sections of subgenus Allium (Hanelt et al. 1992), it displays internal diversity, allowing for the distinction of several groups on the basis of their morphology. Historically, several subsections (Table 1) and series (Omelczuk 1962, Omelczuk-Mjakushko 1976) have been proposed, although the partially flawed concept introduced by Zahariadi (1975) led to subsequent nomenclatural inconsistencies.
L225: More commonly, a number of informal species complexes are used in taxonomic literature (e.g. the A. paniculatum group, A. carinatum group, A. stamineum group, and A. hirtovaginum P.Candargy group; Levan 1933, 1937, Jauzein and Tison 2001, Brullo et al. 2007, Salmeri et al. 2016, Brullo and Salmeri 2021) to accommodate sets of morphologically similar, taxonomically critical taxa.
L226: However, since these groups were defined solely on morphological grounds, a revision supported by molecular evidence is highly desirable.

L245: The unpublished thesis of Giacalone (2011), briefly summarized by Salmeri et al. (2014), analysed phylogenetic relationships among 31 species belonging to the closely related sections Codonoprasum, Cupanioscordum Cheshm., and Allium, using ITS and two plastid DNA regions (psbA-trnH, rbcL).
L246: The study confirmed the monophyly of section Codonoprasum, but revealed that rbcL was phylogenetically uninformative and that deep nodes remained unresolved, forming polytomies in both ITS and concatenated trees (Giacalone 2011). However, as sequences were not deposited in a public database, these data remain unavailable for further research.
L247: In recent years, several studies have published ITS or plastid sequences for only a small number of species from the section, mostly as supplementary evidence in the description of newly recognized taxa (Jandová et al. 2017, Bagheri et al. 2020, Duchoslav et al. 2026) or within broader phylogenetic analyses of the genus (e.g. Veiskarami et al. 2019, Han et al. 2020).
L248: To date, only two published studies have specifically addressed phylogenetic relationships within section Codonoprasum, analysing a total of 18 species using plastid DNA and/or ITS (Salmeri et al. 2016, Vojtěchová et al. 2024). These studies revealed a largely resolved topology and identified two major clades within the section. However, this topology conflicts with the traditionally recognized subsectional taxonomy based on morphological traits (Table 1).
L249: Such discordance may reflect methodological limitations (Duminil and Di Michele 2009), highlighting the need for more sensitive genomic approaches, such as whole-genome genotyping. Yet these approaches sacrifice the advantage of incorporating previously deposited sequences often derived from type material or otherwise difficult-to-access taxa (e.g. Salmeri et al. 2016).
L250: Furthermore, most species were represented by a single accession in previous phylogenetic analyses, potentially masking taxonomic heterogeneity. Indeed, a recent population-level study of genome size in 25 taxa demonstrated considerable variation both among and within several species and subspecies (Kobrlová et al. 2024), which may have important evolutionary implications.
L251: In addition, insufficient taxon sampling (Degnan and Rosenberg 2009) in earlier studies may further limit our understanding of phylogenetic relationships within the section.
L252: Several authors have also highlighted the potential importance of particular traits in the evolution of section Codonoprasum. Similarities in traits among species may reflect either convergent evolution or shared evolutionary history. Integrating trait data with phylogenetic trees allows us to distinguish whether observed trait patterns arise from independent evolutionary changes or from similarity due to common ancestry (Blomberg et al. 2003, Gurushidze et al. 2012, Ávila-Lovera et al. 2023).
L253: Flowering time has frequently been mentioned as a trait associated with deep phylogenetic divergence within the section, potentially linked to past climatic conditions (e.g. Brullo et al. 2009, Özhatay et al. 2018). Other traits of potential evolutionary relevance include genome size (Kobrlová et al. 2024) and polyploidy (Han et al. 2020). These traits may represent characteristics whose evolution is causally connected to environmental factors (Greilhuber and Leitch 2013, Faizullah et al.
L254: 2021), but they may also display a strong phylogenetic signal and evolve independently of environmental influences (Bureš et al. 2023).
L255: The main goals of the present study are therefore to: (i) infer phylogenetic relationships within section Codonoprasum by sequencing three genomic loci (ITS, trnH–psbA, and trnL–ndhJ) using an expanded species dataset represented by multiple accessions, (ii) determine whether molecular phylogenetic data support the monophyly of the section and provide evidence for the delimitation of subsections, (iii) assess whether phylogenetic divergence exists within some morphologically defined taxa, potentially carrying taxonomic implications, and (iv) map selected plant traits onto the phylogenetic tree to identify phylogenetic structuring that may support the delineation of lineages recovered in the current analyses.
L256: ## MATERIALS AND METHODS
L257: ### Plant material and plant traits
L258: The present study includes 154 newly sampled accessions representing 43 Allium taxa, including several informally named entities. Sampling focused primarily on section Codonoprasum (41 taxa), but also incorporated species from several related sections to place the target group within a broader phylogenetic context. Wherever possible, multiple individuals per species were analysed. Additional sequences for 24 taxa were retrieved from GenBank and included in the phylogenetic analyses.
L259: In total, 48 taxa of section Codonoprasum were included. Several recently collected accessions could not be reliably identified, and some appear to represent yet undescribed species. Such accessions are treated as potentially distinct taxa and are coded in the trees and accompanying database at the sectional level using the abbreviation ‘A-cod’ followed by the population code.
L260: To provide an appropriate phylogenetic framework, nine species belonging to sections closely related to section Codonoprasum were also analysed: section Brevispatha Valsecchi (A. parciflorum Viv.), section Cupanioscordum Cheshm. (A. pentadactyli Brullo, Pavone & Spamp., A. hirtovaginatum Kunth, A. cupani agg.), section Pseudoscorodon Brullo, C.Brullo, Cambria, Giusso & Salmeri (A. obtusiflorum Redouté), and section Allium (A. vineale L., A. scorodoprasum L., A. chamaespathum Boiss., A. rotundum L.).
L261: In addition, four phylogenetically distant Allium species (A. victorialis L., A. ursinum L., A. angulosum L., A. tuberosum Rottler ex Spreng.) were included in the analyses as outgroups. Plant material was collected from natural populations as part of our ongoing research (Kobrlová et al. 2024) and subsequently cultivated in the experimental garden of Palacký University in Olomouc, Czech Republic. All living material used for sequencing in the present study was sampled from cultivated plants.
L262: For accessions of section Codonoprasum, the following plant traits were compiled: (i) ploidy level (x) and monoploid genome size (1Cx, in pg), both retrieved from Kobrlová et al. (2024); and (ii) flowering period (sensu  Özhatay et al. 2018), categorized as early summer and summer (up to mid-August) versus late summer and autumn (from late August to October), extracted from original species descriptions and regional floras.
L263: For several accessions lacking previous estimates of ploidy and genome size, we determined these parameters using flow cytometry following the same protocol and instrumentation as described in Kobrlová et al. (2024). A complete list of analysed taxa, populations, and localities, associated plant traits, voucher information, and GenBank accession numbers is provided in the cite52†Supporting Information, Tables S1 and S2†oup.silverchair-cdn.com .
L264: ### DNA extraction, amplification and sequencing
L265: Total genomic DNA was extracted from ∼70 mg of fresh leaves following the CTAB extraction protocol with two pre-washing steps using sorbitol solution (Inglis et al. 2018). DNA quality was verified on a 1% agarose gel and its concentration measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA). Based on previous phylogenetic studies in Allium (Friesen et al. 2006, Salmeri et al. 2016, Han et al.
L266: 2020) and preliminary assessment of polymorphisms (unpublished results), we selected the ITS region of nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) and two plastid regions—3'trnL^{UAA}-trnF-ndhJ (hereafter referred to as trnL–ndhJ) and trnH^{(GUG)}-psbA (trnH–psbA) – for sequencing.
L267: The use of ITS and trnH–psbA enabled us to incorporate publicly available nucleotide data (often derived from type material or otherwise difficult-to-obtain taxa), although trnH–psbA has been reported as less suitable for phylogenetic reconstruction within the section (Salmeri et al. 2016). The second plastid region (trnL–ndhJ) was selected after evaluating nucleotide variability across several other plastid regions in a subset of eight samples.
L268: The PCR reactions were performed following Vojtěchová et al. (2024), using primers and PCR conditions summarized in cite52†Supporting Information, Table S3†oup.silverchair-cdn.com . PCR products were purified using a one-step enzymatic purification protocol (Kim and Blackshaw 2001) and subsequently sequenced bidirectionally by Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam).
L269: ### DNA datasets, DNA sequence alignment, and phylogenetic analysis
L270: 
L271: Nucleotide sequences obtained from each PCR product were edited and assembled into single consensus sequences in Geneious v.7.1.7 (Kearse et al. 2012). Partial regions were aligned using MAFFT v7 (Katoh et al. 2019), and alignments were trimmed and concatenated in Geneious. All newly generated sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers provided in cite52†Supporting Information, Table S1†oup.silverchair-cdn.com .
L272: Phylogenetic analyses were performed on two accession datasets (Table 2). The first (‘full’) dataset comprised 180 accessions (OTUs) representing 57 taxa (species, subspecies, or unclassified accessions of potentially new species assigned at the sectional level). This dataset included 26 OTUs of 24 species for which ITS and trnH–psbA sequences were downloaded from GenBank, and 154 OTUs of 43 taxa from our sampled material newly sequenced for ITS and/or trnH–psbA.
L273: The second dataset (‘reduced’) was a subset of the full dataset and consisted of 154 OTUs from 43 taxa for which the trnL–ndhJ region was newly sequenced.
L274: Phylogenetic analyses were conducted for single regions as well as concatenated regions of each dataset, resulting in six (super)matrices (Table 2): F1, trnH–psbA of full dataset; F2, ITS of full dataset; F3, concatenated ITS+trnH–psbA of full dataset; R1, concatenated both cpDNA regions of reduced dataset; R2, ITS of reduced dataset; and R3, concatenated ITS + both cpDNA of reduced dataset. Allium ursinum and A. vineale were used as outgroups in analyses of the full and reduced datasets, respectively.
