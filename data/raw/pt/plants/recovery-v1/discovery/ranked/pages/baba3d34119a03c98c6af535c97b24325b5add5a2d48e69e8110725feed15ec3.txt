L215: Fuchsia (cite128†/ˈfjuːʃə/ ^{cite129†ⓘ } cite130†FEW-shə ) is a cite131†genus of flowering plants that consists mostly of shrubs or small trees.
L216: Almost 110 species of Fuchsia are recognized; the vast majority are native to South America, but a few occur north through Central America to Mexico, and also several from New Zealand to Tahiti. One species, cite132†F. magellanica , extends as far as the southern tip of South America, occurring on cite133†Tierra del Fuego in the cool cite134†temperate zone, but the majority are cite135†tropical or cite136†subtropical .

L219: ## Taxonomy
L220: 
L221: [cite137†edit ]
L222: 
L223: The first to be cite138†scientifically described , cite139†Fuchsia triphylla , was discovered on the Caribbean island of cite140†Hispaniola (cite141†Haiti and the cite142†Dominican Republic ) about 1696–1697 by the French cite143†Minim friar and botanist, cite144†Charles Plumier ,^{cite145†[4] } during his third expedition to the cite146†Greater Antilles . He named the new genus after German cite147†botanist cite148†Leonhart Fuchs (1501–1566, pronounced cite149†[fʊks] ^{cite150†ⓘ }).^{cite145†[4] }^{cite151†[5] }
L224: The fuchsias are most closely related to the northern hemisphere genus cite152†Circaea , the two lineages having diverged around 41 million years ago.^{cite153†[6] } The presence of Fuchsia in both the Neotropics and the islands of New Zealand and Tahiti likely owes to several dispersal events of Fuchsia across the cite154†Antarctic land bridge during the cite155†Late Eocene .^{cite156†[7] }
L225: ## Description
L226: 
L227: [cite157†edit ]
L228: 
L229:  | This section needs cite158†more citations . Please help cite159†improve this section by cite160†adding citations to reliable sources . Unsourced material may be challenged and cite161†removed . (August 2023) (cite162†Learn how and when to remove this message )
L230: cite163†Volcano hummingbird feeding from a Fuchsia flower, in cite164†Costa Rica .
L231: Fuchsias prefer climates with cool temperatures all year round with no cite165†frost ; they prefer partial shade and warm temperatures in the morning in most other areas. They need plenty of watering to grow well.^{cite166†[8] }
L232: Most fuchsias are shrubs from 0.2 to 4 m (8 in to 13 ft 1 in) tall, but one New Zealand species, the kōtukutuku (cite167†F. excorticata ), is unusual in the genus in being a tree, growing up to 12–15 m (39–49 ft) tall. Fuchsia cite168†leaves are opposite or in whorls of three to five, simple lanceolate, and usually have serrated margins (entire in some species), 1–25 cm long, and can be either cite169†deciduous or cite170†evergreen , depending on the species.
L233: The cite171†flowers are very decorative; they have a pendulous teardrop shape and are displayed in profusion from late spring throughout the summer and early autumn in 12 weeks,^{cite166†[8] } and all year in tropical species.
L234: They have four long, slender sepals and four shorter, broader petals; in many species, the sepals are bright red and the petals purple (colours that attract the cite172†hummingbirds that pollinate them), but the colours can vary from white to dark red, purple-blue, and orange or salmon.^{cite166†[8] } A few have yellowish tones. The ovary is inferior.
L235: The cite173†fruit is a small (5–25 mm) dark reddish green, deep red, or deep purple cite174†berry , containing numerous very small seeds. The fruit of the berry of F. splendens is reportedly among the best-tasting with a flavor is reminiscent of cite175†citrus and cite176†black pepper , and it can be made into cite177†jam .^{cite178†[9] } cite167†F.
L236: excorticata fruit have a pleasant sweet taste with a slight astringency.^{cite179†[10] } Fruit of some other fuchsias however are flavorless or leave a bad cite180†aftertaste .^{cite178†[9] }
L237: In most species, the flowers are bird-pollinated and the seeds dispersed also by birds.^{cite181†[11] }
L238: ## Species
L239: 
L240: [cite182†edit ]
L241: 
L242: Fuchsia sp. in Japan Fuchsia hybrid
L243: The majority of Fuchsia species are native to Central and South America. A small additional number are found on Hispaniola (two species), in New Zealand (three species) and on Tahiti (one species). cite183†Philip A. Munz in his A Revision of the Genus Fuchsia classified the genus into seven sections of 100 species. More recent scientific publications, especially those by the botanists Dennis E. Breedlove of the University of California and, currently, Paul E.
L244: Berry of the University of Michigan, recognize 108 species and 122 taxa, organized into 12 cite184†sections . In New Zealand and Tahiti, section Skinnera now consists of only three species as F. × colensoi has been determined to be a naturally occurring hybrid between F. excorticata and F. perscandens. Also, F. procumbens has been placed into its own section, Procumbentes.
L245: Two other new sections are Pachyrrhiza and Verrucosa, each with one species.^{cite185†[12] } The Plant List, a cooperative endeavor by several leading botanical institutions to maintain a working list of all plant species, lists most currently accepted Fuchsia species and synonyms.^{cite186†[13] }
L246: The vast majority of garden hybrids have descended from a few parent species.^{cite187†[14] }

L453: 
L454: [cite319†edit ]
L455: 
L456: Selection of cultivated fuchsias at cite320†BBC Gardeners' World in 2011
L457: Fuchsias are popular garden shrubs, and once planted can live for years with a minimal amount of care. The British Fuchsia Society^{cite321†[23] } maintains a list of hardy fuchsias that have been proven to survive a number of winters throughout Britain and to be back in flower each year by July. Enthusiasts report that hundreds and even thousands of hybrids survive and prosper throughout Britain.
L458: In the United States, the Northwest Fuchsia Society maintains an extensive list of fuchsias that have proven hardy in members' gardens in the Pacific Northwest over at least three winters.^{cite322†[24] }
L459: Fuchsias from sections Quelusia (F. magellanica, F. regia), Encliandra, Skinnera (F. excorticata, F. perscandens) and Procumbentes (F. procumbens) have especially proven to be hardy in widespread areas of Britain and Ireland, as well as in many other countries such as New Zealand (aside from its native species) or the Pacific Northwest region of the United States and Canada. A number of species will easily survive outdoors in agreeable mild temperate areas.
L460: Though some may not always flower in the average British summer, they will often perform well in other favorable climatic zones. Even in somewhat colder regions, a number of the hardier species will often survive as herbaceous perennials, dying back and reshooting from below ground in the spring.
L461: Due to the favorably mild, temperate climate created by the North Atlantic Current fuchsias grow abundantly in the West Kerry and West Cork region of Ireland and in the cite323†Isles of Scilly , even colonising wild areas there. While F. magellanica is not widespread in Scotland it has been known to grow wild in sheltered areas, such as the banks of local streams in Fife.^{cite324†[25] } In the Pacific Northwest region of the United States, F. magellanica also easily survives regional winters.
L462: ### Categories
L463: 
L464: [cite325†edit ]
L465: 
L466: Horticultural fuchsias may be categorised as upright and bushy, or trailing. Some can be trained as cite326†hedges , such as F. magellanica. Faster-growing varieties are easiest to train.^{cite327†[26] } Care should be taken to choose the hardier cultivars for permanent plantings in the garden as many popular upright Fuchsias such as 'Ernie', 'Jollies Nantes' and 'Maria Landy' are not reliably winter hardy,^{cite178†[9] } but rather extremely tender (cite328†hardiness zone 10).
