L236: Musa is one of three cite164†genera in the family cite157†Musaceae . The genus includes 83 fruit species of cite153†flowering plants producing edible cite148†bananas and cite165†plantains (also known as cite166†cooking bananas ), and fiber (cite167†abacá ), used to make paper and cloth.^{cite168†[3] }^{cite169†[4] } Though they grow as high as cite170†trees , banana and plantain plants are not cite171†woody and their apparent "cite172†stem " is made up of the bases of the huge cite173†leaf cite174†stalks .
L237: Thus, they are technically gigantic cite175†herbaceous plants .
L238: ## Description
L239: 
L240: [cite176†edit ]
L241: Banana plants are among the largest extant herbaceous plants, some reaching up to 9 m (30 ft) in height or 18 m (59 ft) in the case of cite177†Musa ingens . The large herb is composed of a modified underground stem (cite178†rhizome ), a false trunk or pseudostem formed by the basal parts of tightly rolled leaves, a network of roots, and a large flower spike. A single leaf is divided into a leaf sheath, a contracted part called a cite174†petiole , and a terminal leaf blade.
L242: The false trunk is an aggregation of leaf sheaths;^{cite179†[5] } only when the plant is ready to flower does a true stem grow up through the sheath.^{cite180†[6] } At the end of this stem, a cite181†peduncle forms (with M. ingens having the second-longest peduncle known, exceeded only by cite182†Agave salmiana ), bearing many female flowers protected by large purple-red bracts. The extension of the stem (the rachis) continues growth downward, where terminal male flowers grow.
L243: The leaves originate from a pseudostem and unroll to show a leaf blade with two lamina halves.^{cite179†[5] } The lamina can be as much as 7 m (23 ft) long in the case of M. acuminata subsp. truncata (syn. M. truncata) of the cite183†Malay Peninsula ).^{cite184†[7] } Musa species reproduce by both sexual (seed) and asexual (suckers) processes, using asexual means when producing sterile (unseeded) fruits.
L244: Further qualities to distinguish Musa include spirally arranged leaves, fruits as berries, the presence of latex-producing cells, flowers with five connate tepals and one member of the inner whorl distinct, and a petiole with one row of air channels.^{cite185†[8] }
L245: Bananas have a green skin when unripe. While cite166†plantains remain green all the way, many varieties change their skin to a yellow, orange or reddish colour as they ripen.^{cite186†[9] }

L249: ### History
L250: 
L251: [cite188†edit ]
L252: 
L253: cite189†Pliny the Elder used the name cite190†ariena†en.wiktionary.org for the fruit of a tree found in India. It has been speculated that this might have been the banana,^{cite191†[10] }^{cite192†[11] } although Pliny says that a single fruit was sufficient to satisfy four persons.^{cite191†[10] }
L254: During the cite193†late Middle Ages , international trade brought bananas to Europe, which created the need for a name. In the 11th century, cite194†Medieval Latin innovated a term musa: this was most likely^{cite195†[Note 1] } the cite196†latinization of the Arabic name for the fruit, mawz (موز).
L255: Thus, the 11th-century Arabic encyclopedia cite197†The Canon of Medicine , which was translated to Latin in medieval times and well known in Europe, shows a correspondence between Arabic mauz and Latin musa.^{cite198†[Note 2] } Muz is also the Turkish, Persian, and Somali name for the fruit.
L256: According to linguist cite199†Mark Donohue and archaeologist Tim Denham, the ultimate origin of the Latinized form musa is in the cite200†Trans–New Guinea languages , where certain cultivars of bananas are known under a form *muku.^{cite201†[12] } From there, the term was borrowed into the cite202†Austronesian languages of the area, and migrated across Asia, via the cite203†Dravidian languages of India, into cite204†Persian , Greek, and Arabic as a cite205†Wanderwort :^{cite206†[13] }^{cite207†[14] }
L257:     Possible transmission of musa from New Guinea to Latin
L258: Trans-New Guinea  | Austronesian  | Dravidian  | Indic  | Persian  | Arabic  | Greek  | Latin
L259: #mugu  | #mugu > muku > mu ʼ u  | mōttai/mōte  | mocā  | mōč⁠  | mawz/mawza  | mozā  | musa
L260: 
L261: The late Latin term musa was later chosen by cite158†Carl Linnaeus in 1753, as the name for the genus.^{cite208†[15] }
L262: From the time of Linnaeus until the 1940s, different types of edible bananas and plantains were given Linnaean binomial names, such as Musa cavendishii, as if they were species. In fact, edible bananas have an extremely complicated origin involving cite209†hybridization , cite210†mutation , and finally cite211†selection by humans. Most edible bananas are seedless (cite212†parthenocarpic ), hence sterile, so they are propagated vegetatively.
L263: The giving of species names to what are actually very complex, largely asexual, hybrids (mostly of two species of wild bananas, cite213†Musa acuminata and cite214†Musa balbisiana ) led to endless confusion in banana cite215†botany . In the 1940s and 1950s, it became clear to botanists that the cultivated bananas and plantains could not usefully be assigned Linnean binomials, but were better given cite216†cultivar names.
L264: Serving food on a banana leaf is a popular tradition in certain parts of India, Sri Lanka, and parts of Southeast Asia.
L265: 
L266: As for the word cite148†banana , it came to English from Spanish and Portuguese, which had apparently obtained it from a West African language, possibly cite217†Wolof (Senegal).^{cite218†[16] } The Wolof form might itself be a cite219†loanword from Arabic banān mawz (<banān 'finger').
L267: ### Sections
L268: 
L269: [cite220†edit ]
L270: Musa sections have a history dating back to 1887, when M.P. Sagot published "Sur le genre Bananier", where the genus Musa was first formally classified.^{cite221†[17] } In this article, Sagot arranged the Musa species into three groups, although no section names were assigned to them. The grouping was based on morphological traits, establishing the trio as bananas with fleshy fruit, ornamental bananas with upright inflorescences and bracts that were vibrantly colored, and bananas that were giant in size.
L271: Five years after Sagot's article, J.G. Baker made the first formal designation of Musa sections. To do so, he named three subgenera that almost paralleled the sections that had been described by Sagot.^{cite222†[18] } These sections were:
L272:   * M. subg. Physocaulis Baker – defined by a bract with many flowers, inedible fruits, and a bottle-shaped stem
L273:   * M. subg. Rhodochlamys Baker – defined by brightly colored bracts with few flowers, usually inedible fruits, and cylindrical stems
L274:   * M. subg. Eumusa Baker – defined by green, brown, or dull-violet bracts with many flowers, usually edible fruits, and cylindrical stems.
L275: After this classification, in 1947, Cheeseman reclassified the taxa based on morphological features and chromosome number.^{cite223†[19] } This project proposed four sections:
L276: 
L277:   * M. sect. Eumusa Cheesman (2n = 2x = 22)
L278:   * M. sect. Rhodochlamys (Baker) Cheesman (2n = 2x = 22)
L279:   * M. sect. Australimusa Cheesman (2n = 2x = 20)
L280:   * M. sect. Callimusa Cheesman (2n = 2x = 20)
L281: The addition of another Musa section came in 1976 by G.C.G. Ardent. The added section, M. sect. Ingentimusa, Ardent was based on a single species, Musa ingens.^{cite224†[20] } This designation put the number of sections in Musa at five: Eumusa, Rhodochlamys, Callimusa, Australimusa, and Ingentimusa.
L282: In the 21st century, genomics have become cheaper, more efficient, and more accurate, and Musa genetic research has increased exponentially. Research was conducted around a diversity of genomic markers (cpDNA, cite225†nrDNA , rDNA, introns, various spacers, etc.). The results of many of these studies suggested that the five sections of Musa defined by morphology (and listed above) were not monophyletic.^{cite226†[21] }^{cite227†[22] }
L283: Based on the incorrect section grouping, Markku Häkkinen proposed another reclassification of the Musa sections in 2013. Using a multitude of genetic evidence and markers from other studies, Häkkinen suggested the reduction of five Musa sections into two: Musa and Callimusa.^{cite227†[22] } Unlike sectional classifications of the past, this hypothesis was based on genetic markers rather than morphological features or chromosome number.
L284: The two groups were generally formed by the clustering of the previously defined groups:
L285:   * Musa sect. Rhotochlamys and M. sect. Eumusa  became M. sect. Musa
L286:   * M. sect. Ingetimusa, M. sect. Callimusa and M. sect. Australimusa became M. sect. Callimusa
L287: The advance of genomic analysis technologies and further data on the relatedness of Musa species, formulated Häkkinen's two sections and later corroborated them as correct subcategories for the genus.^{cite228†[23] }^{cite229†[24] }^{cite230†[25] } The history of Musa sections provides an example of genomics superseding morphological evidence and thus classifications.
