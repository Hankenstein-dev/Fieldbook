L120@P4-5: 2 LANKESTERIANA Nº 5In April 2000, I found a leafless, terrestrial
L121@P5: Spiranthine orchid with small whitish flowers at La
L122@P5: Selva Biological Station, in Heredia province, Costa
L123@P5: Rica. It was immediately apparent that it was a novelty for the site’s orchid flora (revised by Atwood 1988,
L124@P5: Wilbur 1994), but its identity was a mystery to me.
L125@P5: There were remains of dead leaves at the bases of the
L126@P5: plants, indicating that they were deciduous.
L127@P5: Flowering while leafless is characteristic of a number
L128@P5: of genera in the Spiranthinae, including Sarcoglottis,
L129@P5: Funkiella, Brachystele, Spiranthes, and Schiedeella.
L130@P5: Most species in other genera produce flowers while
L131@P5: their leaves are still present. This vegetative dichotomy originally led me to believe that the plant in question belonged in the former group. Assuming it to be
L132@P5: a species of Schiedeella Schltr., I concluded it was a
L133@P5: new species, and prepared a description.
L134@P5: Fortunately, Gerardo Salazar - who is studying the
L135@P5: generic delimitations within Spiranthinae - saw the
L136@P5: description and recognized the plant as a species of
L137@P5: Cyclopogon, and suggested it might be the enigmatic
L138@P5: C. obliquus (J.J. Sm.) Szlach., which I was later able
L139@P5: to confirm. So, the publication of another superfluous
L140@P5: name for this taxon was prevented (see below).

L177@P5: ABSTRACT. The occurrence of Cyclopogon obliquus (J.J. Sm.) Szlach. [= Pelexia obliqua (J. J. Sm.) Garay]
L178@P5: is reported for the first time in Costa Rica. Its taxonomic placement is discussed, and its current geographic
L179@P5: distribution is reviewed. Observations on its habitat, phenology and reproduction are presented.

L181@P5: [= Pelexia obliqua (J. J. Sm.) Garay] en Costa Rica. Se discute su posición taxonómica y se presenta una
L182@P5: revisión de su actual distribución geográfica. Se presentan observaciones sobre su hábitat, fenología y reproducción.

L232@P6: Cyclopogon obliquus has an interesting historical
L233@P6: record. Even though the genus is otherwise exclusively
L234@P6: Neotropical, it was first found growing spontaneously
L235@P6: in the Bogor Botanic Gardens in western Java, and
L236@P6: described in 1910 by Johannes Jacobus Smith – then
L237@P6: director of the Herbarium Bogoriense – as a species of
L238@P6: Spiranthes Rich. It was not until 1976 when it was collected again, this time in Hong Kong, and described as
L239@P6: Manniella hongkongensis by Hu and Barretto. It was
L240@P6: collected a few years later in El Salvador, and
L241@P6: described by Garay as Pelexia hameri. A couple of
L242@P6: years later, Garay himself recognized his species as
L243@P6: synonymous with Smith’s Spiranthes obliqua, transferring the latter to Pelexia. In 1994, Szlachetko transferred it once more, this time to the genus Cyclopogon.
L244@P6: The latter convention is followed here.
L245@P6: Most recent treatments place this taxon under the
L246@P6: genus Pelexia (Hamer 1984, 1990, 2001; MoraRetana & García 1992, Dressler 1993, Ackerman
L247@P6: 2000, Cribb & Ormerod 2000). However, the combination of a short column, an oblong, fleshy, blunt rostellum, and a nectary that is totally adnate to the
L248@P6: ovary, suggests a better placement in Cyclopogon (G.
L249@P6: Salazar, pers. commun. 2001). On the other hand,
L250@P6: Ackerman (2000) also used floral morphology to support a placement in Pelexia. Recent cladistic analyses
L251@P6: based on DNA data (G. Salazar, unpubl.) indicate that
L252@P6: these two genera are not as closely related to each
L253@P6: other as previously assumed (e.g., Burns-Balogh &
L254@P6: Robinson 1983); therefore, molecular data will likely
L255@P6: settle this species’ systematic affinities.
L256@P6: Cyclopogon obliquus has also turned up in eastern
L257@P6: Java (Comber 1990), Sri Lanka (Hamer 1984),
L258@P6: Samoa (Cribb & Ormerod 2000), Nicaragua (Hamer
L259@P6: 1984, 2001), Guadeloupe, and Cuba (Ackerman
L260@P6: 2000). Ackerman (2000) lists vouchers from most of
L261@P6: these locations. According to Comber (1990), it also
L262@P6: occurs in Argentina and Brazil, but no vouchers are
L263@P6: cited. Though the species’ geographical origin is
L264@P6: unknown, it is clear it must be Neotropical, as are the
L265@P6: rest of the species in both Cyclopogon and Pelexia.

L287@P8: HABITAT: The only collection of Cyclopogon
L288@P8: obliquus from what appears to be a natural habitat is
L289@P8: McGillivary 85 (SEL, not found) (Hamer 1984, Cribb
L290@P8: & Ormerod 2000), in shady, wet, lower montane
L291@P8: tropical forest in the Island of Ometepe in Nicaragua.
L292@P8: The Costa Rican collection comes from a lowland
L293@P8: site, even less seasonal as does the original type collection from Bogor. Comber (1990) notes that it has
L294@P8: become more common in Tretes, as “the seasonal climate in East Java is obviously more to its liking than
L295@P8: the almost everwet climate of Bogor”. These observations, together with its deciduous behavior, support
L296@P8: the idea that this species is better adapted to seasonally dry habitats.
L297@P8: Most of the collections from both the Americas and
L298@P8: southeast Asia come from man-made habitats, especially lawns and pots (Garay 1978, Comber 1990,
L299@P8: Cribb & Ormerod 2000), suggesting that the seeds
L300@P8: –or even whole plants– are accidentally transported in
L301@P8: the soil of transplanted crops or ornamentals. This is
L302@P8: the most likely scenario on how it got to Bogor in the
L303@P8: first place; the Botanic Gardens had been actively
L304@P8: importing plants from throughout the tropical world
L305@P8: since the 1840´s (Indonesian Institute of Sciences
L306@P8: 2002). Cyclopogon obliquus is possibly more widely
L307@P8: distributed in tropical and subtropical areas around
L308@P8: the world than currently recognized, but flowering
L309@P8: plants are easily overlooked due to their inconspicuous flowers and deciduous habit.
L310@P8: This might also explain why this species stayed
L311@P8: undetected at La Selva, where an intensive floristic
L312@P8: inventory program has taken place since 1979
L313@P8: (Hammel & Grayum 1982, Hartshorn & Hammel
L314@P8: 1994). The inflorescences look like dry grass stems
L315@P8: from a distance, and they merge with the colors of the
L316@P8: surrounding leaf litter. I noticed them almost accidentally, but a careful inspection of the surrounding area
L317@P8: revealed a small colony of 12 flowering individuals.

L319@P8: paved trail which runs through a late secondary tropical wet forest, part of an abandoned –and recently
