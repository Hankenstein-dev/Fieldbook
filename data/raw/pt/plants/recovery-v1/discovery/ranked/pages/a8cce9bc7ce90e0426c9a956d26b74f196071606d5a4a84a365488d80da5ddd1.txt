L216: Linnaeus' description of Ornithogalum 1753
L217: Ornithogalum is a cite112†genus of cite113†perennial plants mostly native to southern cite114†Europe and cite115†southern Africa ^{cite116†[3] } belonging to the family cite104†Asparagaceae . Some species are native to other areas such as the cite117†Caucasus .^{cite118†[4] } Some species are classified as noxious invasive weeds in some portions of North America.
L218: Growing from a cite119†bulb , species have linear basal leaves and a slender stalk, up to 30 cm (12 in) tall, bearing clusters of typically white star-shaped flowers, often striped with green. The common name of the genus, star-of-Bethlehem, is based on its star-shaped flowers, after the cite120†Star of Bethlehem that appears in the cite121†biblical account of the birth of cite122†Jesus . The number of species has varied considerably, depending on authority, from 50 to 300.
L219: ## Description
L220: 
L221: [cite123†edit ]
L222: Ornithogalum species are cite124†perennial cite125†bulbous cite126†geophytes with basal leaves. Sensu lato, the genus has the characteristics of the tribe cite106†Ornithogaleae as a whole, since the tribe is cite127†monotypic in that sense.
L223: Sensu stricto, the genus is characterised by long linear to oblong-lanceolate (lance-shaped) leaves, sometimes with a white longitudinal band on the adaxial (upper) side, an cite128†inflorescence that is cite129†corymbose or pseudocorymbose, cite130†tepals that are white with a longitudinal green band only visible on the abaxial (lower) side, a cite131†capsule that is obovate or oblong, and truncate with six noticeable ribs in section and cite132†seeds that are cite133†globose with a prominently cite134†reticulate†en.wiktionary.org (net-like pattern) cite135†testa .^{cite136†[5] } The bulbs are ovoid with free or concrescent scales.^{cite137†[6] }
L224: The longitudinal band on the leaves is thought to be caused by an interruption of cite138†palisade tissue in the central portion of the leaf. This is an cite139†apomorphy that was not present in the early lineage of this cite140†clade , but is also seen in some cite141†Albuca species.^{cite136†[5] }
L225: ## Taxonomy
L226: 
L227: [cite142†edit ]
L228: 
L229: Ornithogalum was originally described by Linnaeus in 1753, with 12 species, which he placed in the Hexandria Monogynia (six stamens, one carpel).^{cite108†[1] } When cite143†Michel Adanson formed the family cite144†Liliaceae in 1763, he placed Ornithogalum there, where it largely remained till this very large family was dismembered towards the end of the 20th century. Specifically, he included the genus with the onions (now cite145†Allioideae ).^{cite146†[7] }
L230: By the 1870s, as Baker describes in his revision of the family,^{cite147†[8] } the cite148†taxonomy of Liliaceae had become vast and complicated. Baker placed Ornithogalum in the tribe cite149†Scilleae ,^{cite150†[9] } one of eight tribes into which he divided the Liliaceae. He then further subdivided the genus into seven subgenera. Of those, the first, Heliocharmos, corresponds to the modern Ornithogalum sensu stricto, with 23 species.^{cite151†[10] }
L231: Later, in the United Kingdom, cite152†Bentham and cite153†Hooker published their volume on the Liliaceae in Latin in 1883.^{cite154†[11] } They divided the family into 20 cite155†tribes and placed Ornithogalum in the tribe Scilleae with 19 other genera, and indicated 70 species existed.^{cite156†[12] } In the German literature the cite157†taxonomic system of cite158†Engler completed its classification of the Liliaceae in 1888.^{cite159†[13] } He divided the family into 12 cite160†subfamilies and subordinate tribes.
L232: Ornithogalum was then placed in the subfamily cite161†Lilioideae and tribe Scilleae together with 21 other genera. The 70 species of Ornithogalum were then further divided into six sections,^{cite162†[14] } with section Heliocharmos corresponding to Baker's subgenus.
L233: Ornithogalum is one of four genera in the tribe cite106†Ornithogaleae , the largest tribe within the subfamily cite105†Scilloideae of the cite104†Asparagaceae .^{cite163†[15] } Historically, it was treated as part of the subfamily Ornithogaloideae of Hyacinthaceae, now obsolete terms. The preferred treatment is to consider the Hyacinthaceae as subfamily Scilloideae of the Asparagaceae. The original subfamilies within Hyacinthaceae became tribes of subfamily Scilloideae.
L234: Thus subfamily Ornithogaloideae became tribe Ornithogaleae.^{cite163†[15] }^{cite136†[5] }
L235: The precise taxonomy of the Ornithogaloideae/Ornithogaleae has been problematic since at least the time of cite107†Linnaeus .^{cite164†[16] } The Ornithogaloideae were one of four major clades within the Hyacinthaceae.^{cite165†[17] } cite166†Phylogenetic analysis subsumed all of that subfamily into the genus Ornithogalum with about 300 species. This cite167†sensu lato reduction of Speta's 14 genera^{cite168†[18] } into one was not widely accepted, though they were cite169†polyphyletic , and had a number of problems.
L236: (This also had the effect of eliminating cite170†Galtonia as a genus, under which a number of Ornithogalum species are still sold.)^{cite136†[5] }
L237: Further analysis with wider sampling (70 compared to 40 taxa) and a third cite171†plastid region (cite172†matK ) revealed the presence of three clades (A, B and C) within Ornithogaleae/Ornithogalum. Consequently, a new classification was proposed with three tribes and four genera, Ornithogalum corresponding to clade C, placed in tribe Ornithogaleae, but further subdivided into subgenera and sections, with 160 species.^{cite173†[19] }^{cite136†[5] } Galtonia was retained as a taxon, but at the subgenus level.
L238: An alternative approach was suggested by combining plastid gene sequences with cite174†nuclear DNA sequences, cite175†morphology , and cite176†biogeography .^{cite136†[5] } This supported Manning's clade C within which Ornithogalum was contained, but the very large subgenus Ornithogalum was noted to still be cite177†heterogeneous , which they had managed by treating it as seven sections.
L239: This study suggested reversing the sensu lato (lumping) approach of Manning et al., reverting to separate genera (splitting), thus resurrecting Galtonia.
L240: The sensu stricto classification of cite178†Martinez-Azorin et al. (2011) reduces the number of species to 50 as originally proposed by Speta.^{cite168†[18] } Thus, any consideration of the genus needs to be examined as to whether it refers to sensu stricto, the 50 species considered by cite179†Speta (1998) and cite178†Martinez-Azorin et al. (2011) , or sensu lato, the much larger genus envisaged by cite180†Manning et al. (2009) .
L241: ### Subdivision
L242: 
L243: [cite181†edit ]
L244: This very large genus has long been divided into many cite182†subgenera . The Flora Europaea (1980) lists 15 subgenera, many of which had at various times been separate distinct genera.^{cite137†[6] } Having originally subsumed all of the Ornithogaleae genera into the single genus Ornithogalum,^{cite165†[17] } cite180†Manning et al.
L245: (2009) later subdivided this now very large genus into four subgenera after resurrecting three of the original subsumed genera (cite141†Albuca , cite183†Pseudogaltonia , cite184†Dipcadi ).^{cite173†[19] } As proposed by them the genus has the following structure:
L246:   * subgenus Avonsera (Speta) J.C.Manning & Goldblatt (cite127†monotypic : Ornithogalum convallarioides)
L247:   * subgenus cite170†Galtonia (Decne.) J.C.Manning & Goldblatt (7 species)
L248:   * subgenus Aspasia (Salisb.) Oberm.^{cite185†[20] } (30 species)
L249:   * subgenus Ornithogalum (7 sections, 120 species)

L277: The Latin genus name ornithogalum derives from cite205†Greek ὀρνιθόγαλον ornithogalon referring to cite97†ornithogalum umbellatum , itself deriving from ὄρνις ornis "bird" (cite206†GEN ὄρνιθος ornithos) and γάλα gala "milk".^{cite207†[23] }^{cite208†[24] }^{cite209†[25] } The name is thought to be related to the white colour of the flowers;^{cite185†[20] }^{cite210†[26] } in some species, they resemble bird droppings.^{cite210†[26] } The possible (non-attested) alternative form in ancient Greek ὀρνιθογάλη ornithogalē seems to be the source of cite211†classical Latin ornithogale as used by cite212†Pliny the Elder .^{cite213†[27] }^{cite214†[28] }
L278: ## Distribution and habitat
L279: 
L280: [cite215†edit ]
L281: 
L282: When the genus is broadly cite216†circumscribed , as for example by cite217†Plants of the World Online , species are widely distributed over several continents including Africa (other than the tropics), Madagascar, Europe, and temperate Asia (as far as Afghanistan).^{cite111†[2] }
L283: ## Uses
L284: 
L285: [cite218†edit ]
L286: 
L287: Vase of cut cite196†Ornithogalum thyrsoides flower stems
L288: 
L289: Ornithogalum species may be sold as cite219†cut flowers , particularly O. arabicum, O. dubium, O. saundersiae, and O.thyrsoides.^{cite220†[29] } They are also sold as ornamental garden flowers.
L290: ## Toxicity and use in alternative medicine
L291: 
L292: [cite221†edit ]
L293: 
L294: Some of the plants in the genus are poisonous, and have been known to kill grazing animals. Others are edible and used as vegetables. The cite119†bulbs contain various toxic cite222†alkaloids and cite223†steroids .^{cite224†[30] } ^{cite116†[3] }
L295: Ornithogalum has been listed as one of the 38 plants used to prepare cite225†Bach flower remedies ,^{cite226†[31] } a kind of cite227†alternative medicine promoted for its effect on health. However, according to cite228†Cancer Research UK , "there is no scientific evidence to prove that flower remedies can control, cure or prevent any type of disease, including cancer".^{cite229†[32] }
