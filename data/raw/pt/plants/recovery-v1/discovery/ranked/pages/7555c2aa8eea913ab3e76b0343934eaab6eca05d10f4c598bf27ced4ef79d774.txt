L23: Veronica sect. Hebe is a group of plants within the genus cite11†Veronica , native to New Zealand, cite12†Rapa in French Polynesia, the cite13†Falkland Islands and South America. It was formerly treated as the separate genus Hebe (/ˈ h iː b iː/^{cite14†[1] }). It includes about 90 species. Almost all species occur in New Zealand, apart from Veronica rapensis (cite15†endemic to Rapa) and cite16†Veronica salicifolia , found in South America. It is named after the cite17†Greek goddess of youth, cite18†Hebe .
L24: Informally, species in the section may be called shrubby veronicas or hebes.
L25: Hebes are widely grown as ornamental plants (see cite19†Cultivation below).
L26: ## Description
L27: Species in Veronica sect. Hebe have four perpendicular rows of cite20†leaves in opposite cite21†decussate pairs. The cite22†flowers are cite23†perfect , the cite24†corolla usually has four slightly unequal lobes, the flower has two cite25†stamens and a long cite26†style . Flowers are arranged in a cite27†spiked cite28†inflorescence . Identification of species is difficult, especially if they are not in flower.
L28: The plants range in size from dwarf shrubs to small trees up to 7 metres (23 feet), and are distributed from coastal to cite29†alpine ecosystems. Large-leaved species are normally found on the coast, in lowland scrub and along forest margins. At higher altitudes smaller-leaved species grow, and in alpine areas there are whipcord species with leaves reduced to thick scales.
L29: ## Taxonomy
L30: 
L31: There are differing classifications for the genus. The former genus Hebe, together with the related Australasian genera Chionohebe, cite30†Derwentia , Detzneria, Parahebe, Heliohebe and Leonohebe are now included in the larger genus cite11†Veronica (hence the common name shrubby veronicas).^{cite31†[2] }^{cite32†[3] }^{cite33†[4] }

L98: 
L99: Hebes are valued in gardens in cite68†temperate climates as cite69†evergreen shrubs with decorative (sometimes cite70†variegated ) leaves. The flowers, in shades of blue, purple, pink or white, appear throughout summer and autumn. Their ability to withstand salt-laden winds makes them especially suited to coastal areas, for instance the South West of England, where they are often grown as hedges. cite71†Prostrate varieties can be used as cite72†groundcover .^{cite73†[6] }^{cite74†[7] }
L100: Hebes cope with most soil types, and can be cite75†propagated easily from both seed and cite76†cuttings . Wild cite77†hybrids in section Hebe are uncommon; however, there are many cultivated hybrids, such as Veronica × franciscana.
L101: 
L102: The cite78†Hebe Society , formed in 1985 under the auspices of the British cite79†Royal Horticultural Society , promotes the cultivation and conservation of hebes and other cite80†New Zealand native plants .
