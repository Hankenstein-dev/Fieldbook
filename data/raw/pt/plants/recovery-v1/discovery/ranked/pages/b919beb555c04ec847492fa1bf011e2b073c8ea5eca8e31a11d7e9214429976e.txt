L149: Forrestia Raf.
L150: Ceanothus is a cite80†genus of about 50–60 species of cite81†nitrogen-fixing cite82†shrubs and small cite83†trees in the cite84†buckthorn family (cite75†Rhamnaceae ).^{cite85†[3] }^{cite86†[4] }^{cite79†[2] }^{cite87†[5] } Common names for members of this genus are buckbrush, California lilac, soap bush, or just ceanothus.^{cite88†[6] }^{cite89†[7] } The genus is native to cite90†North America ,^{cite79†[2] }^{cite91†[8] } ranging from cite92†Canada and the cite93†United States south through cite94†Mexico to cite95†Panama , with the center of diversity in the cite96†California floristic province of the western United States and Mexico, where around ~42 species are cite97†endemic out of the roughly ~58 species in the genus.^{cite98†[9] }^{cite99†[10] } Most Ceanothus are shrubs, with some species rarely tree-like while others have a short mat-like habit.
L151: Species of Ceanothus can be evergreen or deciduous, may have thorns, and also have a diverse number of petal colors ranging from cream to deep blue to pink. Some reproduce strictly from seed, while others can resprout from their roots after a fire.^{cite99†[10] }^{cite100†[11] }
L152: Valued as cite101†ornamental plants , prolific cite102†hybridization in Ceanothus makes them a popular source of horticultural cite103†cultivars , of which there are over 200 named selections. Ornamental Ceanothus hybrids have been cultivated since the 1830s, and a number of them have won the cite104†Royal Horticultural Society 's cite105†Award of Garden Merit .
L153: ## Description
L154: 
L155: [cite106†edit ]
L156: Ceanothus consists of cite82†shrubs that are rarely tree-like, cite107†twigs that are cite108†thorn -like or not, leaves that are persistent (cite109†evergreen ) or cite110†deciduous in alternate or opposite cite111†arrangements (with the stems usually following the leaf arrangement), and generally have a venation on the underside of the leaves that is either pinnate or 3-veined from the base.
L157: The cite112†inflorescences may be terminal (emerging from the end of branches) or axillary, taking up cite113†umbel -like, cite114†raceme -like, and cite115†panicle -like aggregations of flower clusters. The flowers are cite116†bisexual , with 5 cite117†petals , and come in a variety of colors from white to cream, blue, purple, or rarely pink. The fruits are cite118†capsules , with a more or less spherical shape, generally divided into lobes of 3. Within the fruits are 3 small cite119†seeds .^{cite99†[10] }^{cite100†[11] }
L158: Because of the difficulty in identifying Ceanothus due to the limited barriers in genetic exchange resulting in hybrids, and local and geographical variations within species, special attention must be given to identifying key characteristics of the species. The flower colors and mature fruits are important characters, but finding these characteristics can be challenging due to the short duration of flowering and the long maturation period of the fruits that follows.
L159: Other important characters include the life form, understanding the patterns of geographic distribution for the species, and the recognizing the soils preferred by different species.^{cite99†[10] }
L160: ## Taxonomy
L161: 
L162: [cite120†edit ]
L163: 
L164: "Ceanothus" comes from cite121†Ancient Greek : κεάνωθος (keánōthos), which was applied by cite122†Theophrastus (371–287 BC) to a spiny^{cite99†[10] } Old World plant, believed to be cite123†Cirsium arvense .^{cite124†[12] }^{cite125†[13] }
L165: There are two cite126†subgenera within this genus: Ceanothus and Cerastes. The former clade is less drought-resistant, having bigger leaves. The evolution of these two clades likely started with a divergence in the niches filled in local communities, rather than a divergence on the basis of geography.^{cite127†[14] } The two clades are also sometimes recognized as cite128†sections , with the usage of section or subgenus being inconsistent in the literature on the genus.
L166: Subgenus Cerastes was not properly typified nor formally recognized until 2016. Despite the taxonomic difficulties, both clades are generally regarded as being deeply divergent and distinct based on phylogenetic research and morphological characteristics.^{cite98†[9] }
L167: The Californian species of Ceanothus are commonly known collectively as California lilacs, with individual species having more descriptive common names. Species native elsewhere have other common names such as New Jersey tea for C. americanus, as its leaves were used as a black tea substitute during the American Revolution.^{cite85†[3] }^{cite129†[15] } In garden use, most are simply called by their scientific names or an adaptation of the scientific name, such as 'Maritime ceanothus' for C. maritimus.

L285: [cite205†edit ]

L309: ## Distribution
L310: 
L311: [cite215†edit ]
L312: 
L313: Ceanothus americanus (fruit left, flowers right)
L314: 
L315: Plants in this genus are widely distributed and can be found on dry, sunny hillsides from coastal scrub lands to open forest clearings, from near sea level to 9,000 feet (2,700 m) in elevation. These plants are profusely distributed throughout the Rocky Mountains from British Columbia south through Colorado, the Cascades of Oregon and California, and the Coastal Ranges of California.
L316: cite174†Ceanothus velutinus is perhaps the most widespread member of this genus, occurring through much of western North America.^{cite216†[21] } The plants in this genus often co-occur with one another, especially when they are more distantly related.^{cite127†[14] }

L323: 
L324: [cite219†edit ]
L325: 
L326: Ceanothus is a good source of nutrition for deer, specifically cite220†mule deer along the cite221†West Coast of the United States . However, the leaves are not as nutritious from late spring to early fall as they are in early spring. Porcupines and quail have also been seen eating stems and seeds of these shrubs. The leaves are a good source of protein and the stems and leaves have been found to contain a high amount of calcium.

L328: 
L329: [cite222†edit ]

L358: #### Propagation
L359: 
L360: [cite246†edit ]
L361: cite247†Propagation of ceanothus is by cite119†seed , following cite248†scarification and cite249†stratification . Seeds are soaked in water for 12 hours followed by chilling at 1 °C for one to three months. It can also sprout from roots and/or stems. Seeds are stored in plant litter in large quantities. It is estimated that there are about two million seeds per acre in forest habitats. Seeds are dispersed propulsively from capsules and, it has been estimated, can remain viable for hundreds of years.
L362: In habitat, the seeds of plants in this genus germinate only in response to range fires and forest fires.
