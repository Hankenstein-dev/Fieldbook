L307: Euphorbia as a small tree: cite150†Euphorbia dendroides L308: Euphorbia is a large and diverse cite151†genus of cite132†flowering plants , commonly called spurge, in the cite152†family cite126†Euphorbiaceae .
L309: Euphorbias range from tiny cite153†annual plants to large and long-lived trees,^{cite154†[2] } with perhaps the tallest being cite155†Euphorbia ampliphylla at 30 m (98 ft) or more.^{cite156†[3] }^{cite157†[4] } The genus has roughly 2,000 members,^{cite158†[5] }^{cite159†[6] } making it one of the cite160†largest genera of flowering plants .^{cite161†[7] }^{cite162†[8] } It also has one of the largest ranges of cite163†chromosome counts , along with cite164†Rumex and cite165†Senecio .^{cite161†[7] } cite141†Euphorbia antiquorum is the type species for the genus Euphorbia.^{cite166†[9] } It was first described by cite139†Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in cite167†Species Plantarum .
L310: Some euphorbias are widely available commercially, such as cite168†poinsettias at Christmas. Some are commonly cultivated as ornamentals, or collected and highly valued for the aesthetic appearance of their unique floral structures, such as the crown of thorns plant (cite169†Euphorbia milii ).
L311: cite170†Succulent euphorbias from the deserts of Southern cite171†Africa and cite172†Madagascar have evolved physical characteristics and forms similar to cite173†cacti of North and South America, so they are often incorrectly referred to as cacti.^{cite174†[10] } Some are used as ornamentals in landscaping, because of beautiful or striking overall forms, and drought and heat tolerance.^{cite159†[6] }^{cite154†[2] }
L312: Euphorbia all share the feature of having a poisonous, cite175†latex -like sap and unique floral structures.^{cite159†[6] } When viewed as a whole, the head of flowers looks like a single flower (a cite176†pseudanthium ).^{cite159†[6] } It has a unique kind of pseudanthium, called a cite177†cyathium , where each flower in the head is reduced to its barest essential part needed for sexual reproduction.^{cite159†[6] } The individual flowers are either male or female, with the male flowers reduced to only the cite178†stamen , and the females to the cite179†pistil .^{cite159†[6] } These flowers have no cite180†sepals , cite181†petals , or other parts that are typical of flowers in other kinds of plants.^{cite159†[6] } Structures supporting the flower head and other structures underneath have evolved to attract pollinators with nectar, and with shapes and colors that function in a way cite181†petals and other flower parts do in other flowers.
L313: It is the only genus of plants that has all three kinds of cite182†photosynthesis , cite183†CAM , cite184†C3 and cite185†C4 .^{cite159†[6] }
L314: ## Etymology
L315: 
L316: [cite186†edit ]
L317: The common name "spurge" derives from the cite187†Middle English /cite188†Old French espurge ("to purge"), due to the use of the plant's sap as a cite189†purgative . The cite190†botanical name Euphorbia derives from cite191†Euphorbos , the cite192†Greek physician of King cite193†Juba II of cite194†Numidia and cite195†Mauretania (52–50 BC – 23 AD), who married the daughter of Anthony and Cleopatra.^{cite196†[11] } Juba was a prolific writer on various subjects, including cite197†natural history .
L318: Euphorbos wrote that one of the cactus-like euphorbias (now called Euphorbia obtusifolia ssp.
L319: cite198†regis-jubae ) was used as a powerful cite199†laxative .^{cite196†[11] } In 12 BC, Juba named this plant after his physician Euphorbos, as cite200†Augustus Caesar had dedicated a statue to the brother of Euphorbos, cite201†Antonius Musa , who was the personal physician of Augustus.^{cite196†[11] } In 1753, botanist and taxonomist cite139†Carl Linnaeus assigned the name Euphorbia to the entire genus in the physician's honor.^{cite202†[12] }
L320: ## Description
L321: 
L322: [cite203†edit ]
L323: The plants are cite204†annual , cite205†biennial or cite206†perennial herbs, woody shrubs, or trees with a caustic, poisonous milky latex. The cite207†roots are fine or thick and fleshy or tuberous. Many species are more or less cite170†succulent , thorny, or unarmed. The main stem and mostly also the side arms of the succulent species are thick and fleshy, and often winged, 15–91 cm (6–36 in) tall. The succulent trees and large shrubs are mostly confined to southern and eastern Africa, but others are found elsewhere.
L324: For example, cite208†Euphorbia neutra is native to the cite209†Caatinga of cite210†Brazil and cite211†Euphorbia royleana is found in the cite212†Himalayan foothills.^{cite213†[13] } The cite214†leaves may be cite215†opposite , cite216†alternate , or in cite217†whorls . In succulent species, the leaves are mostly small and short-lived. The cite218†stipules are mostly small, partly transformed into cite219†spines or cite220†glands , or missing.

L331: 
L332: Like all members of the family Euphorbiaceae, spurges have unisexual flowers.
L333: In Euphorbia, flowers occur in a head, called the cite177†cyathium (plural cyathia). Each male or female flower in the cyathium head has only its essential sexual part, in males the cite178†stamen , and in females the cite179†pistil . The flowers do not have cite180†sepals , cite181†petals , or cite227†nectar to attract pollinators, although other nonflower parts of the plant have an appearance and nectar glands with similar roles. Euphorbias are the only plants known to have this kind of flower head.^{cite228†[14] }
L334: Nectar glands and nectar that attract pollinators are held in the involucre, a cup-like part below and supporting the cyathium head. The "involucre" in the genus Euphorbia is not to be confused with the "involucre" in family Asteraceae members, which is a collection of bracts called cite229†phyllaries , which surround and encase the unopened flower head, then support the receptacle under it after the flower head opens.
L335: The involucre is above and supported by cite230†bract -like modified leaf structures (usually in pairs) called cyathophylls', or cyathial leaves. The cyathophyll often has a superficial appearance of being petals of a flower.
L336: 
L337: Euphorbia flowers are tiny, and the variation attracting different pollinators, with different forms and colors occurs, in the cyathium, involucre, cyathophyll, or additional parts such as glands that attached to these.
L338: The collection of many flowers may be shaped and arranged to appear collectively as a single individual flower, sometimes called a cite176†pseudanthium in the Asteraceae, and also in Euphorbia.
L339: The majority of species are cite231†monoecious (bearing male and female flowers on the same plant), although some are cite232†dioecious with male and female flowers occurring on different plants. It is not unusual for the central cyathia of a cite233†cyme to be purely male, and for lateral cyathia to carry both sexes. Sometimes, young plants or those growing under unfavorable conditions are male only, and only produce female flowers in the cyathia with maturity or as growing conditions improve.
L340: The female flowers reduced to a single pistil usually split into three parts, often with two stigmas at each tip. Male flowers often have anthers in twos. Nectar glands usually occur in fives,^{cite234†[15] } may be as few as one,^{cite234†[15] } and may be fused into a "U" shape.^{cite228†[14] } The cyathophylls often occur in twos, are leaf-like, and may be showy and brightly coloured and attractive to pollinators, or be reduced to barely visible tiny scales.
L341: The fruits are three- or rarely two-compartment cite235†capsules , sometimes fleshy, but almost always ripening to a woody container that then splits open, sometimes cite236†explosively . The cite237†seeds are four-angled, oval, or spherical, and some species have a cite238†caruncle .
L342: ### Xerophytes and succulents
L343: 
L344: [cite239†edit ]
L345: 
L346: cite240†Euphorbia turbiniformis , a rare succulent Euphorbia from Somalia
L347: In the genus Euphorbia, succulence in the species has often evolved divergently and to differing degrees. Sometimes, it is difficult to decide, and is a question of interpretation, whether or not a species is really succulent or "only" cite241†xerophytic . In some cases, especially with cite242†geophytes , plants closely related to the succulents are normal herbs. About 850 species are succulent in the strictest sense.
L348: If one includes slightly succulent and xerophytic species, this figure rises to about 1000, representing about 45% of all Euphorbia species.
L349: ### Irritants
L350: 
L351: [cite243†edit ]
L352: The milky sap of spurges (called "latex") evolved as a deterrent to cite244†herbivores . It is white, and transparent when dry, except in cite245†E. abdelkuri , where it is yellow. The pressurized sap seeps from the slightest wound and congeals after a few minutes in air. The skin-irritating and caustic effects are largely caused by varying amounts of cite246†diterpenes .
L353: cite247†Triterpenes such as cite248†betulin and corresponding cite249†esters are other major components of the latex.^{cite250†[16] } In contact with mucous membranes (eyes, nose, mouth), the latex can produce extremely painful cite251†inflammation . The sap has also been known to cause mild to extreme Keratouveitis, which affects vision.^{cite252†[17] } Therefore, spurges should be handled with caution and kept away from children and pets.
L354: Wearing eye protection while working in close contact with Euphorbia is advised.^{cite252†[17] } Latex on skin should be washed off immediately and thoroughly. Congealed latex is insoluble in water, but can be removed with an emulsifier such as milk or soap. A physician should be consulted if inflammation occurs, as severe eye damage including permanent blindness may result from exposure to the sap.^{cite253†[18] }
L355: The poisonous qualities were well known: in the Ethiopian cite254†Kebra Nagast , the serpent king cite255†Arwe is killed with juice from the Euphorbia.^{cite256†[19] }

L366: ### Misidentification as cacti
L367: 
L368: [cite275†edit ]
L369: 
L370: cite169†Euphorbia milii L371: Among laypeople, Euphorbia species are among the plant taxa most commonly confused with cite173†cacti , especially the cite276†stem succulents .^{cite277†[21] } Euphorbias secrete a sticky, milky-white fluid with latex, but cacti do not.^{cite277†[21] } Individual flowers of euphorbias are usually tiny and nondescript (although structures around the individual flowers may not be), without petals and sepals, unlike cacti, which often have fantastically showy flowers.^{cite277†[21] }
L372: ## Systematics and taxonomy
L373: 
L374: [cite278†edit ]
L375: The present taxon "Euphorbia" corresponds to its own former subtribe, the cite279†Euphorbiinae . It has over 2000 species.^{cite159†[6] } cite280†Morphological description using the presence of a cyathium (see section above) is consistent with nuclear and chloroplast DNA sequence data in testing of about 10% of its members. This testing supports inclusion of formerly other genera as being best placed in this single genus, including cite281†Chamaesyce , Monadenium, Pedilanthus, and poinsettia (E. pulcherrima).
L376: Genetic tests have shown that similar flower head structures or forms within the genus, might not mean close ancestry within the genus. The genetic data show that within the genus, convergent evolution of cite282†inflorescence structures may be from ancestral subunits that are not related. So using morphology within the genus becomes problematic for further subgeneric grouping. As stated on the Euphorbia Planetary Biodiversity Inventory project webpage:^{cite159†[6] }
L377: > Previous morphologically based delimitations of subgenera or sections within the genus should not be taken at face value. The genus is in fact rife with striking examples of morphological cite283†convergence in cyathial and vegetative features, which justifies a global approach to studying the genus to obtain a proper phylogenetic understanding of the whole group.... The bottom line is that a number of clades have been placed inside or outside of Euphorbia at different times...
L378: few of the subgeneric circumscriptions hold up under DNA sequence analysis.
L379: According to a 2002 publication on studies of cite284†DNA sequence data,^{cite285†[22] }^{cite286†[23] }^{cite287†[24] } most of the smaller "satellite genera" around the huge genus Euphorbia nest deep within the latter. Consequently, these cite288†taxa , namely the never generally accepted genus Chamaesyce, as well as the smaller genera cite289†Cubanthus ,^{cite290†[25] } Elaeophorbia, Endadenium, Monadenium, Synadenium, and Pedilanthus were transferred to Euphorbia.
L380: The entire cite291†subtribe Euphorbiinae now consists solely of the genus Euphorbia. Euphorbia is now subdivided into four cite292†subgenera : cite293†Athymalus , cite142†Chamaesyce , cite143†Esula and cite144†Euphorbia .^{cite294†[26] }^{cite295†[27] }
