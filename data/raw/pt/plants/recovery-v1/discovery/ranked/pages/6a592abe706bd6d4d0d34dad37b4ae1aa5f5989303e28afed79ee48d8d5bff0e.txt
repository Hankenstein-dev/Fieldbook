L15@P0: ABSTRACT
L16@P0:  Taxonomy of the Rudbeckia fulgida Ait. complex is reviewed, resulting in description of a
L17@P0: new species, Rudbeckia terranigrae J.J.N. Campbell & Seymour, sp. nov., and notes on other
L18@P0: poorly understood taxa. This species is known mostly from the Black Belt and Jackson Prairie
L19@P0: regions of Alabama and Mississippi. However, it appears closely related to a few outlying collections
L20@P0: from southwest Missouri and central Texas, plus a larger group of collections centered in the cedar
L21@P0: glade region of Tennessee, which could include another undescribed species. Rudbeckia terranigrae
L22@P0: and these other plants are southern relatives of previously described taxa with more central or
L23@P0: northern ranges in eastern states, especially R. tenax and R. speciosa sensu stricto. Appropriate
L24@P0: taxonomic treatment for all of these plants remains uncertain, but their morphology and biogeography
L25@P0: suggests that they should not be simply combined with R. fulgida sensu stricto, which is largely
L26@P0: restricted to southern Appalachian regions, the Piedmont, and mid-Atlantic Coastal Plain. They all
L27@P0: produce stoloniferous offsets after flowering, their paleae are generally eciliate or weakly ciliate at
L28@P0: apices, and they tend to occur on calcareous substrates. In contrast, typical R. fulgida and its closest
L29@P0: relatives do not produce elongated offsets, their paleae have ciliate apices, and they usually occur on
L30@P0: non-calcareous substrates.
L31@P0:  A provisional key to all taxa within the fulgida complex is presented, with three subgroups.
L32@P0: (1) Rudbeckia truncata and R. missouriensis have relatively narrow leaves without distinct petioles,
L33@P0: paleae glabrous or nearly so, and a lack of stoloniferous offsets. They typically occur on somewhat
L34@P0: xeric sites near calcareous outcrops. (2) The fulgida-tenax subgroup (including R. speciosa and R.
L35@P0: terranigrae) have largest basal and lower cauline leaves with petioles ca. 2–12 cm long and blades
L36@P0: cuneate, ca. 2–8 × 1–5 cm; mid-cauline leaves are shallowly dentate to almost entire (except for
L37@P0: irregular lacerations), the regular teeth usually no more than 1(–2) mm deep; outer paleae often have
L38@P0: distinctly thickened waxy apices; rays number ca. 6–14 and are mostly 1–2.5 cm long. These species
L39@P0: typically occur in grassy openings that are maintained by disturbance rather than just xeric or hydric
L40@P0: extremes. (3) The umbrosa-palustris subgroup (including R. chapmannii, R. sullivantii, and R.
L41@P0: deamii) have largest basal and lower cauline leaves with ca. 12–30 cm petioles, their blades broadly
L42@P0: cuneate, truncate or subcordate, ca. 8–15 × 3–10 cm; mid-cauline leaves are often coarsely dentate
L43@P0: with teeth at least 2 mm deep; outer paleae usually lack thickened waxy apices; rays number ca. 12–
L44@P0: 21 and are mostly 1.5–3 cm long. These species occur typically on mesic to hydric sites, especially in
L45@P0: calcareous seeps, fens, or similar habitats.

L47@P1: Rudbeckia contains about 20–30 species, all native to North America and concentrated in the
L48@P1: southeastern USA. Several taxa are rare to endangered, with small ranges and restricted habitats.
L49@P1: The genus has also become widely known in Europe and elsewhere through introductions for
L50@P1: horticultural interest. Several species or species complexes contain significant variation that has been
L51@P1: hard to describe in traditional taxonomic terms. Rudbeckia fulgida Ait. and its allies form a complex
L52@P1: of taxa that has been particularly difficult to understand and variously treated (e.g., Small 1903, 1933;
L53@P1: Fernald 1950; Perdue 1957; Cronquist 1980; Urbatsch & Cox 2006; Weakley 2012).
L54@P1:  This paper is derived from general observations on the fulgida complex in the field,
L55@P1: herbarium, and garden during the last 30 years. During the 1980s, some botanists in Kentucky
L56@P1: realized that at least two described taxa in the state had been generally ignored within published
L57@P1: treatments after 1950: Rudbeckia tenax C.L. Boynt. & Beadle and R. truncata Small (e.g., PalmerBall et al. 1988; Cranfill 1991; Medley 1993). Subsequent investigations across southeastern states
L58@P1: confirmed the need for a reassessment of the fulgida complex. The ‘last straw’—prompting
L59@P1: publication now—was the recent discovery of an undescribed species in Mississippi, as reported
L60@P1: below. Note, however, that much deeper analysis is still needed, with investigation of cytology,
L61@P1: molecular characters, and comparative ecology. This paper will hopefully stimulate such work.
L62@P1: OUTLINE OF THE RUDBECKIA FULGIDA COMPLEX
L63@P1:  The complex belongs to Rudbeckia sect. Rudbeckia, which is concentrated in the eastern half
L64@P1: of the USA. (Urbatsch & Cox 2006). Some taxa of this section extend further west (especially those
L65@P1: of the R. hirta complex), and a few occur in Mexico or Canada. The other major section of the genus,
L66@P1: sect. Macrocline Torr. & Gray, is more wide-ranging. Distinctive characters of sect. Rudbeckia, as
L67@P1: compared to sect. Macrocline, include these: pappus coronoid [toothed] or glandular or lacking
L68@P1: (versus coronoid); cypselae [fruits] smaller, ca. 1.5–3.5 mm long (versus 3.5–7.5 mm in most taxa),
L69@P1: exceeded (versus not exceeded in most taxa) by their paleae [receptacular bracts]; receptacles usually
L70@P1: hemispheric to conic (versus conic to columnar in most taxa); disc corollas somewhat brownishpurple, at least distally (versus light to reddish brown, at least proximally, sometimes greenish to
L71@P1: yellow distally); laminae of ray corollas often becoming orange to maroon in proximal third or more,
L72@P1: at least when dried (versus remaining uniformly bright yellow); leaves and stems not strongly
L73@P1: glaucous (versus usually glaucous).
L74@P1:  Reported chromosome numbers in Rudbeckia, broadly defined, appear to be based on x = 16,
L75@P1: 18, or 19. Reported full complements (2n) are 32 (only in R. amplexicaulis Vahl), 36, 38, 54, 57, 72,
L76@P1: 76, and 102 (Perdue 1959; Urbatsch & Cox 2006; Palmer et al. 2009). Also, occasional aneuploids
L77@P1: have been found in the fulgida complex (Perdue 1959) and in the hirta L. complex (Palmer et al.
L78@P1: 2009). Numbers above 2n = 38 occur mostly in taxa with relatively large or divided lower leaves,
L79@P1: including R. triloba L. and R. laciniata L. Such taxa occur in relatively mesic or subhydric habitats,
L80@P1: where denser ground vegetation may have selected plants with more capacity to change their foliar
L81@P1: architecture, from basally-concentrated coverage early in the season to taller flowering stems later in
L82@P1: the season. Within sect. Rudbeckia reported numbers are mostly 2n = 38 or 76, but some forms of R.
L83@P1: triloba have ca. 57. In contrast, numbers for sect. Macroline are mostly 2n = 36, but some forms of
L84@P1: R. laciniata have 54, 72, 102, or perhaps more. Both R. triloba and R. laciniata may be largely
L85@P1: apomictic in their reproduction from seed (Gustafsson 1947).
L86@P1:  Palmer et al. (2009) recently showed that within sect. Rudbeckia there are large, characteristic
L87@P1: differences in DNA content between species, independent of chromosome number, suggesting “recent
L88@P1: and substantial changes in genome size.” They found that these plants are generally self-incompatible
L89@P1: in pollination, as also reported by Scott and Molano-Flores (2007). However, small numbers of seed
L90@P1: were produced in some cases through apomixis (pseudogamy, especially after interspecific pollen
L91@P1-2: reception) or perhaps even rarer self-pollination. Artificial interspecific crosses generally fail but  Campbell and Seymour: Rudbeckia fulgida complex 3
L92@P2: have been successful in two reported cases: “fulgida” × missouriensis (McCrea 1981); and “hirta” ×
L93@P2: subtomentosa (Palmer et al. 2009). Perdue (1957) noted that “No plants have been observed in the
L94@P2: herbarium or in the course of extensive field work that offer even the most remote suggestion of
L95@P2: interspecific hybridization.” However, he adopted broad definitions of both R. fulgida and R. hirta.
L96@P2: In a pioneering survey of ribosomal DNA, King and Schaal (1989) showed that R. missouriensis has
L97@P2: relatively little variation within or between populations, which they noted might be attributed to a
L98@P2: ‘founder effect’ in colonization of its fragmented habitat patches.
L99@P2:  Rudbeckia fulgida and its close relatives form the core of sect. Rudbeckia, together with R.
L100@P2: grandiflora (D. Don.) J.F. Gmel. ex DC, R. heliopsidis Torr. & Gray, R. subtomentosa Pursh, and R.
L101@P2: triloba. The other species placed by Urbatsch and Cox (2006) in sect. Rudbeckia are the R. hirta
L102@P2: complex (including R. serotina Nutt. and several other potential segregates), R. mollis Elliott, and R.
L103@P2: graminifolia (Torr. & Gray) C.L. Boynt. & Beadle. Morphological characters that together generally
L104@P2: distinguish the fulgida complex within sect. Rudbeckia are as follows: perennials, ca. 0.5–1.5 m tall;
L105@P2: larger leaf blades ca. 5–15 cm long, unlobed, not amplexicaul, not gland-dotted; heads 1–7(–12+) in
L106@P2: loose corymbiform arrays; paleae rounded to acute but not cuspidate, their outer surfaces glabrous to
L107@P2: pilose but not canescent or strigose. Small (1933) divided species of the fulgida complex into ser.
L108@P2: Fulgidae and ser. Speciosae, but series within the genus have not been adopted by subsequent
L109@P2: authors. The currently reported distribution of R. fulgida sensu lato (Kartesz 2010; Fig. 1) reveals a
L110@P2: number of somewhat disjunct populations, which are detailed further below.
L111@P2:  As in most species of sect. Rudbeckia, a feature of the fulgida complex is the tendency for at
L112@P2: least proximal sections of ligules [in ray florets] to turn orange or maroon (as in R. graminifolia),
L113@P2: especially when matured and dried. Moreover, disc corollas, paleae, phyllaries, and stems often dry
L114@P2: to a somewhat orange, reddish, or purplish hue, versus simply yellowish-brown [stramineous]. This
L115@P2: ‘orangeness’ deserves investigation at the molecular level. It tends to be less clearly visible to human
L116@P2: eyes in flowers of the highly variable R. hirta complex (Palmer et al. 2009) and in R. mollis, though
L117@P2: cryptic ultraviolet patterns can often occur as well or instead (Abrahamson & McCrea 1977; McCrea
L118@P2: 1981; McCrea & Levy 1983). The R. hirta complex and R. mollis are also distinct from the rest of
L119@P2: sect. Rudbeckia in the hirsute-hispid apices of their paleae (with relatively dense, long, multicellular
L120@P2: hairs up to ca. 0.5 mm) and in their generally annual to biennial, tap-rooted growth form, which may
L121@P2: be a factor in their general exclusion from damp soils by diseases or other factors. All other taxa in
L122@P2: sect. Rudbeckia are perennials with branching caudices or, in some cases, rhizomes or stolons.
L123@P2:  Based on morphology, it may be reasonable to ally Small’s ser. Graminifoliae with the
L124@P2: fulgida complex. But that pair of species—Rudbeckia graminifolia and R. mohrii Gray—have
L125@P2: extraordinarily long narrow graminoid leaves and probably other distinctive features. They are typical
L126@P2: of damp southeastern pinelands and ponds. Urbatsch and Cox (2006) placed R. mohrii in sect.
L127@P2: Macrocline based partly on reported chromosome numbers, though it seems somewhat transitional,
L128@P2: with cypselae (seeds) only 3.4–4.2 mm long and an “ovoid or ellipsoid to hemispheric” receptacle.
L129@P2:  Other somewhat anomalous species that have appeared superficially similar to the fulgida
L130@P2: complex include Rudbeckia auriculata (Perdue) Kral and R. scabrifolia L.E. Brown. Both species
L131@P2: have small ranges, in Alabama-Georgia-Florida and Louisiana-Texas respectively. Chromosome
L132@P2: numbers and other characters support their placement in sect. Macrocline (Urbatsch & Cox 2006), but
L133@P2: further investigation is warranted. Compared to more typical members of sect. Macrocline, they have
L134@P2: smaller seeds, receptacles are not strongly columnar, and leaves do not appear particularly glaucous.
L135@P2: Their mid-stem leaves tend to have auriculate bases, especially those of R. auriculata. Such leaves
L136@P2: are rare in the genus but pronounced in R. amplexicaulis Vahl, which is an annual placed by itself in
