L292: Pines (genus Pinus) (cite214†/ˈpaɪ.nəs/ ^{cite215†[2] }) are a genus of conifers in the family cite202†Pinaceae . Pinus is the sole genus in the cite216†subfamily Pinoideae. The species are cite217†trees or shrubs with their leaves in cite218†bunches , usually of 2 to 5 needles. The seeds are carried on woody cones, with two seeds to each cone scale.
L293: Pines are widely cite211†distributed in the cite219†Northern Hemisphere ; they occupy large areas of cite212†taiga (boreal forest), but are found in many habitats, including the cite220†Mediterranean Basin , and cite221†dry tropical forests in cite222†southeast Asia and cite223†Central America . Some are fire-resistant or fire-dependent.
L294: Pine trees provide one of the most extensively used types of cite224†timber . The pine nuts are used to make dishes such as cite225†pesto , while cite226†retsina wine is flavoured with pine resin.

L300: [cite228†edit ]

L302: Pine trees are coniferous cite229†resinous cite217†trees (or, rarely, cite230†shrubs ) growing 3–80 metres (10–260 feet) tall, with the majority of species reaching 15–45 m (50–150 ft) tall.^{cite231†[3] } The smallest are cite232†Siberian dwarf pine and cite233†Potosi pinyon , and the tallest is an 83.45 m (273.8 ft) tall cite234†sugar pine (Pinus lambertiana) located in cite235†Yosemite National Park .^{cite236†[4] }
L303: Pines are long lived and typically reach ages of 100–1,000 years, some even more. The longest-lived is the cite237†Great Basin bristlecone pine (Pinus longaeva). One individual in the cite238†White Mountains of California, dubbed "cite239†Methuselah ", is among the cite240†world's oldest living organisms at around 4,800 years old.^{cite241†[5] } cite242†An older tree near cite243†Wheeler Peak , now cut down, was dated at 4,900 years old.^{cite244†[6] }^{cite245†[7] }
L304: The spirals of branches, needles, and cite246†cone scales cite247†are arranged in cite248†Fibonacci number ratios.^{cite249†[8] }^{cite250†[9] }

L311: ### Bark
L312: 
L313: [cite254†edit ]
L314: 
L315: The cite255†bark of most pines is thick and scaly, but some species have thin, flaky bark. The branches are produced in "pseudo-whorls", actually a very tight spiral but appearing like a ring of branches arising from the same point. Many pines are uninodal, producing just one such whorl of branches each year, from cite256†buds at the tip of the year's new cite257†shoot , but others are multinodal, producing two or more whorls of branches per year.^{cite258†[10] }
L316:   * Many pines, like cite259†Pinus taeda , have thick bark that flakes into scales.
L317: 
L318:   * Some species, like cite260†Pinus bungeana , have thin bark.

L324:   * Seed leaves (cite263†cotyledons ) on seedlings are borne in a whorl of 4–24.
L325:   * Juvenile leaves, which follow immediately on seedlings and young plants, are 2–6 centimetres (3⁄4–2+1⁄4 inches) long, single, green or often blue-green, and arranged spirally on the shoot. These are produced for six months to five years, rarely longer.
L326:   * Scale leaves, similar to bud scales, are small, brown and not photosynthetic, and arranged spirally like the juvenile leaves.
L327:   * Needles, the adult leaves, are green (cite264†photosynthetic ) and bundled in clusters called cite218†fascicles . The needles can number from one to seven per fascicle, but generally number from two to five. Each fascicle is produced from a small bud on a dwarf shoot in the axil of a scale leaf. These bud scales often remain on the fascicle as a basal sheath. The needles persist for 1.5–40 years, depending on species. If a shoot's cite265†growing tip is damaged (e.g.
L328: eaten by an animal), the needle fascicles just below the damage generate a stem-producing bud, which can then replace the lost growth tip.

L337: ### Cones
L338: 
L339: [cite270†edit ]
L340: Pines are cite271†monoecious , having the male and female cones on the same tree.^{cite272†[11] } The male cones are small, typically 1–5 cm long, and only present for a short period (usually in spring, though autumn in a few pines), falling as soon as they have shed their cite273†pollen . The female cones take 1.5–3 years (depending on species) to mature after cite274†pollination , with actual fertilisation delayed one year. At maturity, the female cones are 3–60 cm long.
L341: Each cone has numerous spirally-arranged scales, with two seeds on each fertile scale; the scales at the base and tip of the cone are small and sterile without seeds.^{cite258†[10] }
L342: The seeds (cite275†pine nuts ) are mostly small and winged, and are cite276†anemochorous (wind-dispersed). Some are larger, have only a vestigial wing, and are cite277†bird -dispersed. Female cones are woody and sometimes armed to protect developing seeds from foragers. At maturity, the cones usually open to release the seeds. In some of the bird-dispersed species, for example cite278†whitebark pine ,^{cite279†[12] } the seeds are only released by the bird breaking the cones open.
L343: In others, the seeds are stored in closed cones for many years until an environmental cue triggers the cones to open, releasing the seeds. This is called cite280†serotiny . The most common form of serotiny is cite281†pyriscence , in which resin binds the cones shut until the resin is melted by a forest fire, for example in cite282†P. radiata and cite283†P. muricata .
L344: The seeds are then released after the fire, enabling them to colonise the burnt ground with minimal competition from other plants.^{cite258†[10] }^{cite284†[13] }

L352: ## Naming
L353: 
L354: [cite287†edit ]
L355: The modern English name "pine" derives from Latin pinus, traced to the Indo-European base *pīt- 'resin'.^{cite288†[14] } Before the 19th century, pines were often called firs, a name now applied to another genus, cite289†Abies . In some European languages, Germanic cognates of the Old Norse name are still in use for pines, as in cite290†Danish fyr and cite291†German Föhre.^{cite292†[15] } The genus Pinus was named by cite203†Carl Linnaeus in 1753.
L356: cite205†Pinus sylvestris , the Scots pine, was later chosen as the cite204†type species .^{cite293†[16] }^{cite294†[17] }
L357: Several other conifers are commonly known as pines, such as the cite295†Norfolk Island pine (Araucaria heterophylla); however, they belong to other genera.^{cite296†[18] }
