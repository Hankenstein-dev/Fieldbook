L208: ## Description
L209: 
L210: [cite147†edit ]
L211: 
L212: Phalaenopsis difformis of Phalaenopsis subg. Ornithochilus Phalaenopsis japonica of Phalaenopsis subg. Hygrochilus cite148†Phalaenopsis hieroglyphica of Phalaenopsis sect. Polychilos Phalaenopsis taenialis of Phalaenopsis sect. Aphyllae Phalaenopsis lobbii of Phalaenopsis sect. Parishianae cite149†Pneumatodes in roots of cite100†Phalaenopsis philippinensis L213: Orchids in the genus Phalaenopsis are monopodial epiphytic, sometimes lithophytic cite150†herbs with long, coarse roots with cite149†pneumatodes (which allow for gaseous exchange of the cite151†photosynthetic roots),^{cite152†[3] }^{cite153†[4] } and short leafy stems hidden by overlapping leaf bases. The leaves are usually arranged in two rows, relatively large and leathery, oblong to elliptic and sometimes succulent.
L214: A few to many, small to large, long-lasting, flat, often fragrant flowers are arranged on erect to hanging cite154†racemes or cite155†panicles . The cite156†sepals and cite157†petals are free from and spread widely apart from each other. The cite158†lateral†en.wiktionary.org sepals are usually larger than the cite159†dorsal†en.wiktionary.org sepal and the petals much wider than the sepals. The cite160†labellum is joined stiffly to the cite161†column and has three lobes.
L215: The side lobes are erect and more or less parallel to each other and the middle lobe sometimes has a pair of appendages or antennae.^{cite137†[2] }^{cite162†[5] }^{cite163†[6] }^{cite164†[7] }
L216: ### Reproduction
L217: 
L218: [cite165†edit ]
L219: Unlike most other angiosperms, the development of the cite166†ovules is triggered by cite167†pollination . Therefore a temporal mismatch of male and female cite168†gametes exists (i.e. the male structures are ready but the female structures have not yet been fully developed). In orchids, cite169†fertilisation occurs within days or months after the pollination event. After pollination occurs, the cite170†stigmatic cavity is often closed through swelling of the cite161†column .
L220: As a result, the cite171†pollinia are completely enclosed. The pollen germinates after enclosure and tubes are produced. In cite172†Phalaenopsis aphrodite , pollen tubes entered the ovary within 3 days after pollination. Within 15 days, pollen tubes evenly spread throughout the cite173†placental cavities. Within 60 days after the pollination event, 30.6% of the tips of the pollen tubes started to enter the micropyles (i.e. entry points) of the ovules.
L221: Finally, 65 to 70 days after the pollination event, fertilisation occurred.^{cite174†[8] }
L222: Phalaenopsis are unique in that in some species of the subgenus Polychilos, the flowers turn into green leaves after pollination. As in many other plants, the petals of the orchid flowers serve to attract pollinating insects and protect essential organs. Following pollination, petals usually will undergo cite175†senescence (i.e. wilt and disintegrate) because it is metabolically expensive to maintain them. However, in many Phalaenopsis species, such as P.
L223: violacea, the petals and sepals find new uses following pollination, thus escaping cite176†programmed cell death . In producing cite177†chloroplasts , they turn green, become fleshy, and start to photosynthesize, as leaves do.^{cite178†[9] }

L237: ### Infrageneric structure
L238: 
L239: [cite193†edit ]
L240: The large monophyletic genus Phalaenopsis is divided into several sub-units. They are either regarded as subgenera or as sections. Not all of them are monophyletic. The subgenus Polychilos is monophyletic and it is divided into two subclades. The subgenus Phalaenopsis is paraphyletic under its current definition. Likewise the evidence shows that the subgenera Aphyllae and Parishianae, as currently defined, are not monophyletic.
L241: The position of the monotypic subgenus Proboscidioides, which only consists of cite194†Phalaenopsis lowii , suggests a close relationship with the subgenus Aphyllae.^{cite195†[16] }

L286: ## Ecology
L287: 
L288: [cite224†edit ]
L289: Pollination of Phalaenopsis pulcherrima through bees, specifically Amegilla nigritar, has been recorded in China.^{cite225†[25] } This species employs a deceptive pollination strategy. The orchid does not provide rewards to pollinators. It benefits from blooming in the same period as rewarding species.
L290: cite226†Amegilla is the most important pollinator, but diurnal insects – such as four species of bees, two species of butterflies, one species of moth and two other unidentified insects – have also been observed to interact with the flowers.^{cite227†[26] }

L296: ## Use in horticulture
L297: 
L298: [cite238†edit ]
L299: 
L300: cite180†Phalaenopsis bellina exhibiting typical post-pollination changes of the subgenus Polychilos, in which petals and sepals become photosynthetic
L301: Phalaenopsis, abbreviated Phal in the cite239†horticultural trade,^{cite240†[32] } are among the most popular orchids sold as potted plants, owing to the ease of propagation and flowering under artificial conditions. They were among the first tropical orchids in cite241†Victorian collections.
L302: Since the advent of the cite242†tetraploid hybrid Phalaenopsis Doris, they have become extremely easy to grow and flower in the home, as long as some care is taken to provide them with conditions that approximate their native habitats. Their commercial production has become an industry.
L303: If very healthy, a Phalaenopsis plant may have up to ten or more leaves. They bloom in their full glory for several weeks. If kept in the home, the flowers may last two to three months after which a Phalaenopsis orchid will need to conserve energy for further leaf, bud, and root development.^{cite243†[33] }
L304: In nature, Phalaenopsis species are typically fond of warm temperatures, thriving in temperatures around 20 to 35 cite244†°C (68–95 °F), but are adaptable to conditions more comfortable for human habitation in temperate zones (15 to 30 °C or 59–86 °F); at temperatures below 18 °C (64.4 °F) overwatering causes root rot and the plants will also drop their leaves if they experience temperatures below 60 °F for extended periods.
L305: Phalaenopsis prefer moderate humidity (60–70%) and low light of 12,000 to 20,000 cite245†lux . However, Phalaenopsis orchids can adapt to the lower humidity found in most homes. They are also typically hardier than other species of orchids, and this makes them particularly popular among first-time orchid growers.^{cite246†[34] }
L306: The flower spikes appear from the pockets near the base of each leaf. The first sign is a light green "mitten-like" object that protrudes from the basal leaf tissue. Over approximately three months the spike elongates until it begins to swell fat buds that will bloom.
L307: It previously was believed that flowering is triggered by a night-time drop in temperature of around 5 to 6 degrees over two to four consecutive weeks, usually in the fall, and a day-time drop in temperature to below 29 °C (84 °F). Using two Phalaenopsis clones, Matthew G. Blanchard and Erik S.
L308: Runkle (2006) established that, other culture conditions being optimal, flower initiation is controlled by daytime temperatures declining below 27 °C (81 °F), with a definite inhibition of flowering at temperatures exceeding 29 °C (84 °F). The long-held belief that reduced evening temperatures control flower initiation in Phalaenopsis was shown to be false. Rather, lower daytime temperatures influence flowering, while night time temperatures do not appear to have any effect.^{cite247†[35] }
