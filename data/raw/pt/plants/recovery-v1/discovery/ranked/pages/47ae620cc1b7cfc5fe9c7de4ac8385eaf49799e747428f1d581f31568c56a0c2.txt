L16@P0: Abstract
L17@P0: A new species of Magnolia from the southern Sierra Madre Oriental, Mexico, is described and illustrated, providing
L18@P0: information about its habitat distribution, ecology, biogeography and conservation status. After 12 fieldwork expeditions
L19@P0: near the border of the states of Hidalgo and Puebla, we have developed morphological, ecological and biogeographic data
L20@P0: to support recognition of populations from Acaxochitlán, Hidalgo and Pahuatlán, Puebla as a distinct species of Magnolia
L21@P0: sect. Macrophylla. A key to species of this section and a distribution map for Mexican taxa are provided. The species was
L22@P0: assessed as critically endangered (CR).
L23@P0: Keywords: Cloud-forest species, Mexican endemic, Mexican flora, Magnolia dealbata
L24@P0: Resumen
L25@P0: Se describe e ilustra una nueva especie de Magnolia del sur de la Sierra Madre Oriental, México, proporcionando información
L26@P0: sobre su distribución, hábitat, ecología, biogeografía y estado de conservación de la especie. A través de 12 expediciones
L27@P0: de trabajo de campo cerca de la frontera de los estados de Hidalgo y Puebla, se recabaron datos morfológicos, ecológicos y
L28@P0: biogeográficos que apoyan el reconocimiento de las poblaciones de Acaxochitlán, Hidalgo y Pahuatlán, Puebla como una
L29@P0: especie distintiva de Magnolia de la sección Macrophylla. Se proporciona una clave de las especies de esta sección y un
L30@P0: mapa de distribución de los taxones mexicanos. La especie fue evaluada como en peligro crítico (CR).
L31@P0: Palabras clave: flora del bosques nuboso, Especie endémica mexicana, flora mexicana, Magnolia dealbata
L32@P0: Introduction
L33@P0: Magnoliaceae are a well-defined family of evergreen and deciduous trees and shrubs with two genera and ca. 350
L34@P0: species (Azuma et al. 2001, Kim & Suh 2013, Vázquez-García et al. 2015). Magnolia comprises 99% of the species
L35@P0: in the family (Weaver 1987, Doyle 1989, Vázquez-García et al. 2015, 2021, Sánchez-Velásquez et al. 2016) and is
L36@P0: subdivided into 12 sections (Kim & Suh 2013, Wang et al. 2020).
L37@P0: Magnolia sect. Macrophylla Figlar & Noteboom (2004: 92) includes seven species that are characterized by large
L38@P0: deciduous leaves with an auriculate to cordate base that are glaucous to pubescent on the underside, frequently with
L39@P0: a purple spot at the base of the adaxial side of the petals and a stomatal number of four (Figlar & Nooteboom 2004,
L40@P0-1: Vázquez-García 1990, Vázquez-García 1994, Vázquez-García et al. 2015).272 • Phytotaxa 513 (4) © 2021 Magnolia Press SÁNCHEZ-GONZÁLEZ et al.
L41@P1: An isolated population of Magnolia was first found in Zotictla, southeastern Hidalgo, composed of about 25 trees
L42@P1: in disturbed and highly fragmented cloud forest remnants. These differed from the others in phenology and some floral
L43@P1: and foliar characteristics. The distribution of this new species is restricted to Acaxochitlán, Hidalgo and Pahuatlán,
L44@P1: Puebla. However, a more detailed exploration is required in other forest remnants of neighboring municipalities in
L45@P1: southeastern Hidalgo and northern Puebla, where additional populations of this species may occur. The present work
L46@P1: describes and illustrates this new species of Magnolia sect. Macrophylla.
L47@P1: Fieldwork included twelve expeditions between June 2013 and April 2021, the earliest aimed to study pteridophytes
L48@P1: in the cloud forest of Zotictla in southern Hidalgo. AH had seen the trees thriving there since her childhood, thanks
L49@P1: to her father, a rural teacher, who moved to Zotictla with his family several years earlier. Soon after, morphological
L50@P1: variation of populations of Magnolia section Macrophylla in the Sierra Madre Oriental (Querétaro, Hidalgo, San Luis
L51@P1: Potosí) was studied as part of the master’s thesis of MGL.
L52@P1: Following the general lineage concept of de Queiroz (1998, 2007) we used morphological, ecological, genetic
L53@P1: (Chávez-Cortázar et al. 2021) and biogeographical (Morrone et al. 2017) data as independent lines of evidence to
L54@P1: support species status. Based on keys to species of Magnolia sect. Macrophylla (Vázquez-García et al. 2015, 2016,
L55@P1: García-Morales et al. 2017) and specimens examined at IBUG, XAL, WIS and MEXU herbaria (acronyms according to
L56@P1: Thiers 2020), we confirmed that the morphologically most similar species is M. dealbata. Recently collected material
L57@P1: from Zotictla and Acaxochitlán and herbarium specimens at XAL were the basis of this morphological description.
L58@P1: Major characters were contrasted against the single morphologically closest species, M. dealbata, occurring in the
L59@P1: southern edge of the Sierra Madre del Sur province and one geographically nearby species, M. vovidesii, occurring in
L60@P1: the intermediate area, at the eastern edge of the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt (Table 1). We used ArcMap 10.3 to plot
L61@P1: coordinates of M. sect. Macrophylla obtained from field exploration, herbarium specimens and Global Biodiversity
L62@P1: Information Facility database (GBIF 2020). A digital elevation model GMTED2010 and a layer of biogeographic
L63@P1: provinces (Morrone et al. 2017) were added. The taxonomic key was adapted from Vázquez-García et al. (2016);
L64@P1: we updated a map for M. sect. Macrophylla through examination of available specimens at pertinent herbaria and
L65@P1: consulted the MEXU virtual collection. Habitat and phenology data were obtained from herbarium specimen labels,
L66@P1: fieldwork and pertinent taxonomic literature. Conservation status was assessed using the IUCN Red List Criteria
L67@P1: (IUCN 2019) and the information collected in fieldtrips. The extent of occurrence (EOO) and area of occupancy
L68@P1: (AOO) were delimited from georeferenced records obtained in field explorations and some revised and analyzed GBIF
L69@P1: records (GBIF 2020).

L193@P3: Distribution and ecology:—Distributed on the southern edge of the biogeographic province of Sierra Madre
L194@P3: Oriental (on the border between the states of Hidalgo and Puebla). A few magnolia trees still thrive wild in ravines or
L195@P3: hills within highly fragmented, disturbed and open cloud forest remnants. Occasionally they form small groups but
L196@P3: mostly are isolated near crops or home orchards, among widely spaced houses and bordered by secondary cloud forest
L197@P3: vegetation dominated by Liquidambar styraciflua Linnaeus (1753: 999; Altingiaceae) and Alnus acuminata Kunth
L198@P3: (1817: 20; Betulaceae). Most Magnolia trees are close to or within the hilly villages of the Acaxochitlán Municipality,
L199@P3: Santa Catarina, Toxtla and Zotictla, the last with three barrios: Centro, Tlaloc and Yeloxóchitl. Another population was

L211@P8: Phenology:—During the study period, it was observed that the production of leaves begins in February, and the
L212@P8: production of flower buds begins in the first week of March. The ripening of the fruits was observed from the end of
L213@P8: April and the beginning of May. Falling of leaves for this species is from November to January. In M. rzedowskiana,
L214@P8: flower buds appear at the end of April and beginning of May.
L215@P8: Ethnobotany:—In the ancient cultures of America and Asia, magnolias were known for their ornamental
L216@P8: and pharmacological use, essentially to alleviate various types of human pathologies (Pfaffman 1975, Dodd 1980,
L217@P8: Gutiérrez-Carvajal 1993, Gutiérrez and Vovides 1997, Satyajiy et al. 2002, Vázquez-García et al. 2015). This species
L218@P8: is known locally as magnolia, eloxóchitl or yeloxóchitl (cornflower in Náhuatl), and its showy and fragrant flowers
L219@P8: are usually collected for sale locally. The most important use in the region is to prepare floral infusions to treat heart
L220@P8: problems, nerve problems and scars. It is also used as an ornamental plant. A few people from Santa Catarina, Toxtla
L221@P8: and Zotictla sell the petals largely on Sunday in the local markets of Hidalgo (Actopan, Pachuca, San Miguel Resgate
L222@P8: and Tulancingo), Puebla (Honey, Pahuatlán), Veracruz (Poza Rica and Tuxpan), and Mexico City (Sonora traditional
L223@P8: market). A kg of petals sells at $4–6 USD for use as infusions.
L224@P8: Conservation status:—Magnolia zotictla has a narrow distribution, found only in the towns of the Acaxochitlán,
L225@P8: Hidalgo (Santa Catarina, Toxtla and Zotictla) and Pahuatlán municipalities in Puebla (Ahila). The habitat is highly
L226@P8: fragmented and disturbed, and trees are mostly isolated. The four populations observed have fewer than 60 adult
L227@P8: individuals in total. Only a few seedlings and juveniles were observed inside the forest (E. Méndez, pers. comm., 6
L228@P8: July 2020). In addition to this, it was observed that the flowers are collected without control because they are showy
L229@P8: and have a pleasant smell; the local people are well acquainted with this plant species but have no interest or concern
L230@P8: for its conservation. It was observed that the trees fall easily during the hurricane season or when the wind and rain
L231@P8: are intense. Small mammals and birds eat the seeds. Thus, it is suggested that it should be immediately listed in some
L232@P8: risk category within the Official Mexican Standard (NOM-059-2010). According to the IUCN criteria (IUCN 2019),
L233@P8: the species meets four different criteria [A2ad, B1ab(iii, v), B2ab(iii), C2a(i)] and should be assigned to the critically
L234@P8: endangered category.
L235@P8: Notes:—Magnolia zotictla lacks the purple spot at the base of the petals usually found on M. dealbata and differs
L236@P8: from M. rzedowskiana in the size of the petals, number of stamens and carpels and size of the fruits (Table 1). The most
L237@P8: obvious difference in flowering time is that M. zotictla begins two months earlier than the two aforementioned species.
L238@P8: Magnolia zotictla and M. dealbata occur at different latitudes 20°13’–20°16’N vs. 17°18’–17°31’N and in contrasting
L239@P8: biogeographic provinces (Morrone et al. 2017, Domínguez-Yescas et al. 2020), the former at the southern edge of the
L240@P8: Sierra Madre Oriental vs. the latter at the northern edge of the Sierra Madre del Sur, separated by the Trans-Mexican
L241@P8: Volcanic Belt. The emergence of this volcanic chain may have formed a natural barrier and secured their isolation,
L242@P8: preventing gene flow and favoring allopatric radiation of three species M. zoctictla, M. vovidesii and M. dealbata, each
L243@P8: one now in different biogeographic provinces (Ibarra-Martínez 2020).
