L225: cite150†Around 280 species ^{cite151†[4] }
L226: Pelargonium (cite152†/ˌpɛlɑːrˈɡoʊni.əm/ )^{cite153†[5] } is a cite154†genus of cite138†flowering plants cite155†commonly called geraniums, pelargoniums, or storksbills. It includes about 280 species of cite156†perennials , cite157†succulents , and cite158†shrubs .^{cite151†[4] } Taxonomist cite159†Carl Linnaeus originally included all the species of Pelargonium and cite160†Geranium under the latter name. In 1789, cite143†Charles Louis L'Héritier de Brutelle separated them into two genera.
L227: While Geranium species are mostly cite161†temperate herbaceous plants, dying down in winter, Pelargonium species are evergreen perennials cite162†indigenous to warm temperate and tropical regions of the world, with many species in southern Africa. They are drought and heat tolerant but can tolerate only minor frosts. Some species are extremely popular garden plants, grown as houseplants and cite163†bedding plants in temperate regions.
L228: They have a long flowering period, with flowers mostly in red, orange, or white, but intensive breeding has produced a huge array of cite164†cultivars with great variety in size, flower colour, leaf form and aromatic foliage.
L229: ## Description
L230: 
L231: [cite165†edit ]
L232: 
L233: Pelargonium sp., probably a hybrid Pelargonium flower
L234: Pelargonium occurs in a large number of cite166†growth forms , including cite167†herbaceous cite168†annuals , cite158†shrubs , cite169†subshrubs , stem cite157†succulents and cite170†geophytes .^{cite171†[6] } The erect stems bear five-cite172†petaled cite173†flowers in cite174†umbel -like clusters, which are occasionally branched. Because not all flowers appear simultaneously, but open from the centre outwards, this is a form of inflorescence is referred to as pseudoumbels.
L235: The flower has a single symmetry plane (cite175†zygomorphic ), which distinguishes it from the cite160†Geranium flower, which has radial symmetry (cite176†actinomorphic ). Thus the lower three (anterior) petals are differentiated from the upper two (posterior) petals. The posterior cite177†sepal is fused with the cite178†pedicel to form a cite179†hypanthium (nectary tube).
L236: The nectary tube varies from only a few millimeters, up to several centimeters, and is an important floral characteristic in morphological classification. cite180†Stamens vary from 2 to 7, and their number, position relative to cite181†staminodes , and curvature are used to identify individual species. There are five cite182†stigmata in the cite183†style .^{cite184†[7] } For the considerable diversity in flower morphology, see figure 1 of Röschenbleck et al. (2014).^{cite171†[6] }
L237: cite185†Leaves are usually alternate, and cite186†palmately lobed or cite187†pinnate , often on long stalks, and sometimes with light or dark patterns. The leaves of cite188†Pelargonium peltatum (Ivy-leaved Geranium), have a thick cuticle better adapting them for drought tolerance.^{cite189†[8] }
L238: ## Taxonomy
L239: 
L240: [cite190†edit ]
L241: 
L242: cite191†Dillenius ' introduction of the term 'Pelargonium' in Hortus Elthamensis 1732 cite192†Pelargonium inquinans , (Geranium Afric. arborescens), Hortus Elthamensis
L243: Pelargonium is the second largest genus (after Geranium) within the cite193†family cite142†Geraniaceae , within which it is cite194†sister to the remaining genera of the family in its strict sense,^{cite195†[9] } cite196†Erodium , cite160†Geranium , and cite197†Monsonia including Sarcocaulon. The Geraniaceae have a number of genetic features unique amongst angiosperms, including highly rearranged cite198†plastid cite199†genomes differing in cite200†gene content, order and expansion of the cite201†inverted repeat .^{cite171†[6] }
L244: ### Genus history
L245: 
L246: [cite202†edit ]
L247: The name Pelargonium was first proposed by cite191†Dillenius in 1732,^{cite203†[10] } who described and illustrated seven species of geraniums from cite204†South Africa that are now classified as Pelargonium.^{cite205†[11] }^{cite206†[12] } Dillenius, who referred to these seven species with apparent unique characteristics as Geranium Africanum (African Geranium)^{cite207†[13] } suggested "Possent ergo ii, quibus novi generis cupido est, ea, quorum flores inaequales vel et irrregulares sunt, Pelargonia vocare" (Those who wish a new genus can therefore call those, whose flowers are unequal or irregular, ‘Pelargonia’).^{cite208†[14] } The name was then formally introduced by cite209†Johannes Burman in 1738.
L248: However cite159†Carl Linnaeus who first formally described these plants in 1753 did not recognise Pelargonium and grouped together in the same genus (Geranium) the three similar genera Erodium, Geranium, and Pelargonium.^{cite210†[15] } Linnaeus' reputation prevented further differentiation for forty years.^{cite206†[12] } The eventual distinction between them was made by cite143†Charles L’Héritier based on the number of stamens or anthers, seven in the case of Pelargonium.
L249: In 1774, cite211†P. cordatum , cite212†P. crispum , cite213†P. quercifolium and P. radula were introduced, followed by cite214†P. capitatum in 1790.^{cite215†[16] }^{cite205†[11] }

L278: ### Phylogenetic analyses
L279: 
L280: [cite225†edit ]
L281: All subdivision classifications had depended primarily on morphological differences till the era of cite226†phylogenetic analyses (Price and Palmer 1993).^{cite195†[9] } However phylogenetic analysis shows only three distinct cite227†clades , labelled A, B and C.^{cite228†[20] } In this analysis not all sections were cite229†monophyletic , although some were strongly supported including Chorisma, Myrrhidium and Jenkinsonia, while other sections were more cite230†paraphyletic .
L282: This in turn has led to a proposal, informal at this stage, of a reformulation of the infrageneric subdivision of Pelargonium.
L283: In the proposed scheme of Weng et al. there would be two cite231†subgenera , based on clades A+B, and C respectively and seven sections based on subclades.
L284: Subsequent analysis with an expanded taxa set confirmed this infrageneric subdivision into two groups which also correspond to chromosome length (<1.5 μ, 1.5-3.0μ),^{cite171†[6] } but also two subclades within each major clade, suggesting the presence of four subgenera, these correspond to clades A, B, C1 and C2 of the earlier analysis, A being by far the largest clade with 141 taxa.
L285: As before the internal structure of the clades supported monophyly of some sections (Myrrhidium, Chorisma, Reniformia, Pelargonium, Ligularia and Hoarea) but paraphyly in others (Jenkinsonia, Ciconium, Peristera). L319: ### Subgenera
L320: 
L321: [cite270†edit ]
L322: 
L323: Subgenus Magnipetala: Corresponds to clade C1, with 24 species. Perennial to short lived, spreading subshrubs, rarely herbaceous annuals. Petals five, but may be four, colour mainly white. Mainly winter rainfall region of South Africa, spreading into summer rainfall region. One species in northern Namibia and cite271†Botswana . Two species in East Africa and cite272†Ethiopia . Chromosomes x=11 and 9.
L324: Subgenus Parvulipetala: Corresponds to clade B, with 39-42 species. Perennials, partly annuals. Petals five and equal, colour white or pink to deep purplish red. Mainly South Africa, but also other southern hemisphere except South America. a few species in East Africa and Ethiopia. Chromosomes x=7-19.
L325: Subgenus Paucisignata: Corresponds to clade C2, with 25-27 species. Erect sometimes trailing shrubs or subshrubs, rarely geophytes or semi-geophytes. Petals five and equal, colour pink to red sometimes white. Summer rainfall region of South Africa, spreading into winter rainfall region and northern cite273†Namibia , with a few species in tropical Africa, Ethiopia, cite274†Somalia , cite275†Madagascar , the cite276†Arabian Peninsula and Asia Minor. Chromosomes x=mainly 9 or 10, but from 4–18.
L326: Subgenus Pelargonium: Corresponds to clade A, with 167 species. Frequently cite277†xerophytic deciduous perennials with many cite170†geophytes and succulent subshrubs, less frequently woody evergreen shrubs or annual herbs. Petals five, colour shades of pink to purple or yellow. Winter rainfall region of South Africa and adjacent Namibia, spreading to summer rainfall area, and two species in tropical Africa. Chromosomes x=11, may be 8–10.^{cite171†[6] }

L341: ## Distribution and habitat
L342: 
L343: [cite286†edit ]
L344: Pelargonium is a large genus within the family cite142†Geraniaceae , which has a worldwide distribution in temperate to subtropical zones with some 800 mostly herbaceous species.^{cite171†[6] } Pelargonium itself is cite162†native to cite287†southern Africa (including cite273†Namibia ) and cite288†Australia .
L345: Southern Africa contains 90% of the genus, with only about 30 species found elsewhere, predominantly the cite289†East African rift valley (about 20 species) and southern Australia, including cite290†Tasmania .^{cite171†[6] } The remaining few species are found in southern cite275†Madagascar , cite291†Yemen , cite292†Iraq , cite293†Asia Minor , the north of cite294†New Zealand and isolated islands in the south cite295†Atlantic Ocean (cite296†Saint Helena and cite297†Tristan da Cunha ) and cite298†Socotra in the cite299†Indian Ocean .^{cite171†[6] } The cite300†centre of diversity is in southwestern South Africa where rainfall is confined to the winter, unlike the rest of the country where rainfall is predominantly in the summer months.^{cite171†[6] } Most of the Pelargonium plants cultivated in cite301†Europe and cite302†North America have their origins in South Africa.^{cite279†[21] }
L346: ## Ecology
L347: 
L348: [cite303†edit ]
L349: Pelargonium species are eaten by the cite304†caterpillars of some cite305†Lepidoptera species, including the noctuid moth cite306†angle shades , Phlogophora meticulosa. The diurnal butterflies cite307†Cacyreus marshalli and cite308†C. tespis (Lycaenidae), native to southern Africa, also feed on Geranium and Pelargonium.^{cite309†[23] } C. marshallii has been introduced to Europe and can develop into a pest on cultivated Pelargoniums.
L350: It has naturalised along the Mediterranean, but does not survive the winter in Western Europe.^{cite310†[24] }
L351: The cite311†Japanese beetle , an important agricultural insect pest, becomes rapidly paralyzed after consuming flower petals of the garden hybrids known as "zonal geraniums" (P. × hortorum). The phenomenon was first described in 1920, and subsequently confirmed.^{cite312†[25] }^{cite313†[26] }^{cite314†[27] }^{cite315†[28] } Research conducted by Dr.
L352: Christopher Ranger with the cite316†USDA cite317†Agricultural Research Service and other collaborating scientists have demonstrated the excitatory amino acid called cite318†quisqualic acid present within the flower petals is responsible for causing paralysis of the Japanese beetle.^{cite319†[29] }^{cite320†[30] } Quisqualic acid is thought to mimic cite321†L-glutamic acid , which is a cite322†neurotransmitter in the insect neuromuscular junction and mammalian central nervous system.^{cite323†[31] }
L353: A study by the Laboratory of Apiculture & Social Insects group at the cite324†University of Sussex on the attractiveness of common garden plants to cite325†pollinators found that a cultivar of Pelargonium × hortorum was unattractive to pollinators in comparison to other selected garden plants such as cite326†Lavandula (lavender) and cite327†Origanum .^{cite328†[32] }
