L101: ## Summary
L102: Rudbeckia hirta L. (Compositae: Heliantheae: Rudbeckinae) is described and illustrated. Material in Wakehurst's North American Prairie planting would key out to the widespread Rudbeckia hirta L. var. pulcherrima Farw., in that it is a perennial, leafy in the upper half of the stem and with narrow-lanceolate leaves. The issue of the rank of the currently recognized infraspecific taxa is highlighted and the cluttering of the synonymy with the recognition of minor, often horticultural variants discussed.
L103: A commonly cultivated species, this is one of several late-season flowerers in the American Prairie at RBG, Kew at Wakehurst Place. Cultivars are commonly cultivated, offering a considerable colour variation in the ray limbs, but it appears that the true species is relatively uncommon, possibly having come in and gone out of fashion since it was first brought into cultivation.
L104: Statements of the species' distribution, habitat and ecological preferences, phenology, conservation status, and the etymology of both generic and specific epithets are given, along with an impressive array of vernacular names, reflecting its wide distribution and long cultivation. Comments are provided on the nectar, or honey, guides seen (under UV light) in Rudbeckia hirta. The guides are due to the presence of flavonol glucosides, these present only in the upper surface of the ray limbs.
L105: Historical uses of the species are noted, and phytochemical analyses have provided evidence that the anti-inflammatory properties have a factual basis.
L106: ## INTRODUCTION
L107: Rudbeckia hirta L. (Compositae: Heliantheae: Rudbeckiinae), with its distinctive yellow rays and the ‘black eye’ of the central cone of disc florets, is the subject of the present article, and considered significant enough to be designated the ‘Floral Emblem’ of Maryland in 1918, becoming the Maryland State Flower (Maryland State Archives, cite15†2022 ). Along with Coreopsis lanceolata L.
L108: (spear-leaved Coreopsis, lance-leaved Coreopsis – see Hind et al., cite16†2023 ), were both included as pioneering plants forming the ‘base layer’ of North American Prairie plantings of the Royal Botanic Gardens Kew at Wakehurst Place. They are also two with the longest flowering season (March to October) amongst both the Compositae and the prairie plants at Wakehurst Place, compared with the late summer/early autumn flowering of the symphyotrichums (e.g., Symphyotricum laeve (L.) Á. Löve & D.
L109: Löve – see Hind & Lambkin, cite17†2023b , and S. oolentangiense (Riddell) G.L. Nesom) and Liatris spp. (e.g., L. aspera Michx. – see Hind & Lambkin, cite18†2023d ; L. elegans (Walter) Michx. and L. pycnostachya Michx.). Both the Rudbeckia and Coreopsis can be found in the Atlantic Coastal Plain Prairie and the South Central Prairie communities. Hopefully, some of these other species in the prairie plantings will be illustrated in future issues of Curtis's Botanical Magazine.
L110: Rudbeckia L. is a relatively small genus endemic to the United States, with 23 species arranged in three sections: sect. Rudbeckia, with nine, mostly perennial, horticulturally important species from eastern North America, sect. Macrocline Torr. & A. Gray, consisting of 13 herbaceous perennial species (Urbatsch & Cox, cite19†2006 ), and the monotypic sect. Dracopsis (Cass.) A. Gray, containing the annual R. amplexicaulis Vahl (formerly Dracopis amplexicaulis (Vahl) Cass.).
L111: Although Cox & Urbatsch (cite20†1994 : 302) included ‘R. subgen. Laciniata’ in their key to subgenera, Cox (cite21†1991 ) in her unpublished thesis is the only author to have referred to (and described) this subgenus; it has no taxonomic nor nomenclatural value as it has never been validated. Gray (1884) recognized only two sections, the larger ‘Eurudbeckia’ (= sect. Rudbeckia) and sect. Dracopis.
L112: The majority of the horticulturally important and useful species are contained within the type section of the genus, sect. Rudbeckia, including such species as R. fulgida Aiton (orange coneflower – see Sims,  cite22†1818 ), R. hirta, the subject of this plate (black-eyed Susan – unrelated to the climbing plant Thunbergia alata Bojer ex Sims [Acanthaceae], known by the same common name), R. subtomentosa Pursh (sweet coneflower), and R. triloba L.
L113: (also known as black-eyed Susan, or brown-eyed Susan) (Perdue,  cite23†1958 ), although R. laciniata L. (cut-leaved coneflower) and occidentalis Nutt. (western coneflower), from sect Macrocline (sensu Urbatsch & Cox, cite19†2006 ) are also useful prairie plants, mostly for their large size, like R. maxima (see Hind, cite24†2018 ).
L114: Rudbeckia belongs to the Sunflower tribe, the Heliantheae which Hind (cite25†2007 ), whilst considering the tribe in the broadest sense, estimated contained about 300 genera and about 3330 species, making it the largest tribe in the family. This expanded concept of the tribe includes the Helenieae, a tribe often simplistically separated on the basis of epaleaceous receptacles, and reduced pappus.
L115: However, Robinson (cite26†1981 ) amalgamated the two, commenting that Bentham's separation was highly artificial, but in doing so Robinson recognized 35 subtribes. One of the subtribes still recognized in the Heliantheae is the Rudbeckiinae H. Rob., containing just Rudbeckia and Ratibida Raf. (e.g., Ratibida columnifera (Nutt.) Wooton & Standl. var. pulcherrima (DC.) D.J.N. Hind – see Hind, cite27†2006 ), also good in prairie plantings.
L116: The relationship between Ratibida, Echinacea Moench, and the sections of Rudbeckia were scarcely clarified by Cox & Urbatsch (cite28†1990 ), with Echinacea placed in the broad concept of Rudbeckia. Panero (cite29†2006 ) placed Echinacea within his concept of the subtribe Zinniinae.
L117: Rudbeckia hirta was originally treated as the generitype by Britton & Brown (cite30†1913 : 469), a type choice made under the American Code. Under ‘Canon 15’ (see Arthur et al., cite31†1907 : 1172), the American Code is usually seen as a mechanical choice – ‘the type is the first binomial species in order eligible …’ (following five provisions). This choice was superseded by Green's choice of Rudbeckia laciniata L. (Green in Hitchcock & Green, cite32†1929 : 183).
L118: A summary, together with commentary of other lectotypifications of Linnaean generic names, was provided by McNeill et al. (cite33†1987 ). Interestingly, the more mechanical approach appears to have been Green's choice, since R. laciniata was the first listed by Linnaeus (cite34†1753 : 906); R. hirta was the third.

L124: ## CULTIVATION
L125: 
L126: Rudbeckia hirta has a moderately long history of cultivation. Sweet (cite36†1826 – referring back to Brit. Fl.: Gard. 82) noted that it was first introduced in 1714, long before it was formally recognized and described by Linnaeus in 1753. Sutton (cite37†2001 ) was of the opinion that the species was not in cultivation (in the UK), suggesting that it might have gone in and out of fashion.
L127: Some texts (e.g., Diboll & Cox, cite38†2023 ) were adamant that the species is a biennial, but horticulturally it is often treated as an annual, with many cultivars, and a wide range of colours, from greenish-yellow, light yellow through red to a deep burgundy, bronze or mahogany, or a mix, including colour breaks or bi-coloured rays.
L128: Floras and other accounts (e.g., Britton & Brown, cite39†1898 ; Moore, cite40†1900 ; Sutton, cite37†2001 ; Urbatsch & Cox, cite19†2006 ; Wangdin & Knees, cite41†2000 ), however, consider the species as an annual, biennial or (a short-lived) perennial. Since our accession at Wakehurst Place is still flowering in 2023, the stock we have (Accession No. 2020-747*1) is clearly perennial.
L129: Although usually grown as a half hardy annual, Jeffrey (cite42†1992 : 721) listed it as Z4 (−30 to −28.5°C), RHS Find-a-plant (cite43†2023 ) listed it as H3 (−5C–1C) and ‘hardy in coastal and relatively mild parts of the UK’, in comparison to USDA (cite44†2019 ) indicating the species was hardy down to −23 °F (−5°C). Plants can be grown in a wide range of slightly acidic soils (ideally between pH 6 and 7) from clayey, loamy or sandy soils.
L130: It should be grown in fully sun, and kept slightly moist to slightly dry, but in too dry a border plants will clearly wilt, so mulch if soil prone to drying out too quickly.
L131: Rudbeckia hirta can provide cut flowers, but can also afford ‘seedheads’ for dried flower displays, remembering to hang the fruiting capitula upside down as they dry.
L132: ### Propagation
L133: Propagation by seed (achenes) is the only real method for multiplying. Seeds can be sown directly outside in the spring, early summer or autumn, in their final flowering position, or in a well-prepared area for prairie planting. Plants can flower the first season, and should be treated as hardy annuals, but perennating plants as hardy perennials. If indoor sowing is preferred, then sow the achenes onto a moist, well-drained seed compost just covered with soil or Vermiculite.
L134: 20°C+, keeping soil moist, but not wet. Germination should be within 10–15 days, but if nothing appears after 3–4 weeks, then cold stratify (see Hind, cite45†2023 ), moving the containers to −4 to +4°C for 4 weeks and return to warmth. Transplant when large enough to handle into 8 cm pots. Grow on in cooler conditions and acclimatize and plant out after last frost, spacing dependent upon the effects desired.
L135: ### Pests and diseases
L136: 
L137: Rudbeckia hirta is usually noted as of low palatability for deer etc.; bearing in mind the coarsness of the leaves this is of no surprise. Rudbeckia hirta var. pulcherrima is the larval food host for the Silvery Checkerspot Butterfly (Chlosyne nyteis) (Keener et al., cite46†2022 ). USDA (cite44†2019 ) also noted that both Downy (Sclerospora sp.) and Powdery (Erysiphe sp.) Mildew, and White Smut (Entyloma sp.) are fungal infections in the United States.
L138: ### Availability
L139: Currently, it would appear that there are only two suppliers of live plants of this species in the UK. (Cubey et al., cite47†2023 ), contrasting with Sutton's opinion from over a couple of decades earlier (Sutton, cite37†2001 ) when he noted that it was not in cultivation in the UK. Seed is available, more so for the numerous cultivars (with variously bicolored rays, or ray limbs with colour-breaks), from several well-known suppliers in both the UK and United States.
L140: Those not particularly worried about purity could settle for cv. ‘Daisy Mix’ which has almost every colour variation under the sun, or the ‘Gloriosa Daisies’ range (see Discussions and Notes below).
