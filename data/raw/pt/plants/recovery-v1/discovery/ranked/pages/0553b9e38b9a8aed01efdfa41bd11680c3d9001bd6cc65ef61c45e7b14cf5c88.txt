L28@P1-2: * Author for correspondence Abstract
L29@P2: The genus Allium has been extensively studied in recent decades to elucidate the evolutionary
L30@P2: processes shaping its phylogeny and taxonomic relationships. However, significant gaps remain,
L31@P2: particularly concerning infrageneric classification and evolutionary relationships within several
L32@P2: sections. One such example is section Codonoprasum, comprising approximately 160 diploid
L33@P2: and polyploid taxa centred mainly in the Mediterranean and western Irano–Turanian regions.
L34@P2: Despite its high species diversity, this section is characterised by taxonomic inconsistencies and
L35@P2: poorly resolved phylogenetic structure. Here, we improve current understanding of the evolution
L36@P2: of section Codonoprasum by analysing sequence variation in three genomic loci (nrITS, trnH–
L37@P2: psbA and trnL–ndhJ) and by mapping selected plant traits onto the resulting phylogenetic trees.
L38@P2: Our dataset includes 48 taxa—around 30% of the total diversity of the section—each represented
L39@P2: by multiple accessions. The results support the monophyly of section Codonoprasum and
L40@P2: indicate a close relationship with section Cupanioscordum. Within section Codonoprasum, five
L41@P2: major lineages (clades I–V) were identified, some of which were further subdivided into
L42@P2: morphologically and/or karyologically distinct subclades. The placement of taxa within these
L43@P2: lineages is evaluated in the light of existing knowledge on their morphology, karyology, and
L44@P2: geographic distribution. Overall, the phylogenetic patterns recovered here are incongruent with
L45@P2: traditional subsectional taxonomy. Genome size appears to follow a more punctuated than
L46@P2: gradual mode of evolution along the phylogeny. We interpret these findings with a focus on
L47@P2: lineage composition, interclade relationships, and their implications for understanding
L48@P2: evolutionary patterns and taxonomy within this diverse section.

L51@P2-3: phylogeny, plastid DNA, taxonomy, Introduction
L52@P3: The genus Allium L. comprises more than a thousand species (POWO 2025), making it one of
L53@P3: the largest monocotyledonous genera. Its major centre of diversity extends from the
L54@P3: Mediterranean Basin to central and northeastern Asia, with a secondary, less pronounced centre
L55@P3: in North America (Dale and Jacobsen 2002, Fritsch and Friesen 2002, Li et al. 2010). Early
L56@P3: phylogenetic studies based on the nuclear ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region and
L57@P3: several chloroplast (cpDNA) loci identified three major evolutionary lines containing 15
L58@P3: subgenera and 72 sections (Friesen et al. 2006, Li et al. 2010). Over the past 15 years, similar
L59@P3: approaches have provided valuable insights into evolutionary processes and taxonomic
L60@P3: relationships within selected subgenera—e.g. subgenus Melanocrommyum (Webb & Berth.)
L61@P3: Rouy (Gurushidze et al. 2008, 2010, Fritsch et al. 2010), subgenus Anguinum (G.Don ex
L62@P3: W.D.J.Koch) N.Friesen (Li et al. 2016, Yang et al. 2023), and subgenus Amerallium Traub
L63@P3: (Friesen et al. 2024)—as well as within particular sections, such as section Cepa (Mill.) Prokh.
L64@P3: (Gurushidze et al. 2007), section Allium (Hirschegger et al. 2010), and section Rhizirideum
L65@P3: G.Don ex W.D.J.Koch (Sinitsyna et al. 2016). Several geographically focused studies, including
L66@P3: those from western North America (Nguyen et al. 2008), Korea (Choi and Oh 2011), China (Li
L67@P3: et al. 2010), and Iran (Veiskarami et al. 2019), have contributed further insights. The most
L68@P3: comprehensive phylogeny of Allium to date was published by Han et al. (2020), who analyzed
L69@P3: 448 species using eight plastid loci and one nuclear locus to explore the role of polyploidy in
L70@P3: species diversification. Collectively, these studies substantially extend the knowledge acquired
L71@P3: through earlier morphological, anatomical, and molecular research (see e.g. Hanelt et al. 1992,
L72@P3: Friesen et al. 2006, Li et al. 2010 for reviews), building upon the first and still the only
L73@P3: comprehensive monograph by Regel (1875). Despite this progress, substantial gaps remain in
L74@P3: infrageneric taxonomy, and evolutionary relationships among subgenera, sections, and species
L75@P3-4: within the main evolutionary lineages remain insufficiently resolved (Zhang et al. 2024). Allium section Codonoprasum Rchb. (hereafter section Codonoprasum), part of the
L76@P4: largest subgenus Allium within the third evolutionary lineage, represents a remarkable example
L77@P4: of high species diversity, inconsistent taxonomy and poorly resolved phylogenetic relationships
L78@P4: (Friesen et al. 2006, Salmeri et al. 2016, Duchoslav et al. 2020, 2025). The section has its centre
L79@P4: of diversity in the Mediterranean and the western part of Irano-Turanian region (e.g.
L80@P4: Cheshmedzhiev 1975abc, Zahariadi 1975, Wilde-Duyfjes 1976, Stearn 1981, Kollmann 1984,
L81@P4: ÷zhatay 1984, 1990, 1993, Pastor and ValdÈs 1985, Tzanoudakis and Vosa 1988, Brullo et al.
L82@P4: 1996ab, 1997ab, 1998, 2007, Hanelt 1996, Trigas et al. 2010, 2017, Özhatay and Koçyiğit 2019,
L83@P4: Trigas and Bareka 2020, Brullo and Salmeri 2021). The section is burdened by an exceptionally
L84@P4: large number of described names, with new species still being added. It currently comprises
L85@P4: about 160 accepted species (POWO 2025), including many polyploid taxa (Kobrlov· et al.
L86@P4: 2024).
L87@P4: Although the section is considered morphologically relatively homogeneous, it is well
L88@P4: differentiated from other sections of subgenus Allium (Hanelt et al. 1992). Historically, several
L89@P4: subsections (Table 1) and series (Omelczuk 1962, Omelczuk-Mjakushko 1976) have been
L90@P4: proposed, with a partially flawed concept by Zahariadi (1975) leading to subsequent
L91@P4: nomenclatural inconsistencies. More commonly, a number of informal species complexes are
L92@P4: used in taxonomic literature (e.g. A. paniculatum group, A. carinatum group, A. stamineum
L93@P4: group, A. hirtovaginum P.Candargy group; Levan 1933, 1937, Jauzein and Tison 2001, Brullo
L94@P4: et al. 2007, Salmeri et al. 2016, Brullo and Salmeri 2021) to accommodate sets of
L95@P4: morphologically similar, taxonomically critical taxa. However, these groups were defined on
L96@P4: morphological grounds, and a revision supported by molecular evidence is therefore highly
L97@P4: desirable.
L98@P4: The unpublished thesis of Giacalone (2011), briefly summarised by Salmeri et al. (2014),
L99@P4: analysed phylogenetic relationships among 31 species belonging to the closely related sections
L100@P4-5: Codonoprasum, Cupanioscordum Cheshm. and Allium, using ITS and two plastid DNA regions (psbA-trnH, rbcL). The study confirmed the monophyly of section Codonoprasum, but revealed
L101@P5: that rbcL was phylogenetically uninformative and that deep nodes remained unresolved, forming
L102@P5: polytomies in both ITS and concatenated trees (Giacalone 2011). However, because the author
L103@P5: did not deposit the generated sequences in a public database, these data cannot be used for further
L104@P5: research.
L105@P5: In recent years, several studies have published ITS or plastid sequences for only a small
L106@P5: number of species from the section, mostly as supplementary evidence in the description of
L107@P5: newly recognised taxa (Jandov· et al. 2017, Bagheri et al. 2020) or within broader phylogenetic
L108@P5: analyses of the genus (e.g. Veiskarami et al. 2019, Han et al. 2020). To date, only two published
L109@P5: studies have specifically addressed phylogenetic relationships within section Codonoprasum,
L110@P5: analysing a total of 18 species using plastid DNA and/or ITS (Salmeri et al. 2016, Vojtěchová et
L111@P5: al. 2024). These studies revealed a largely resolved topology and identified two major clades
L112@P5: within the section. However, this topology conflicts with the traditionally recognised
L113@P5: subsectional taxonomy based on morphological traits (Table 1). Such discordance may reflect
L114@P5: methodological limitations (Duminil and Di Michele 2009), highlighting the need for more
L115@P5: sensitive genomic approaches, such as whole-genome genotyping. Yet these approaches
L116@P5: sacrifice the advantage of incorporating previously deposited sequences—often derived from
L117@P5: type material or otherwise difficult-to-access taxa (e.g. Salmeri et al. 2016). Furthermore, most
L118@P5: species were represented by a single accession in published phylogenetic analyses, potentially
L119@P5: masking taxonomic heterogeneity. Indeed, a recent population-level study of genome size in 25
L120@P5: taxa demonstrated considerable variation both among and within several species and subspecies
L121@P5: (Kobrlov· et al. 2024), which may have important evolutionary implications. Additionally,
L122@P5: insufficient taxon sampling (Degnan and Rosenberg 2009) in earlier studies may simply limit
L123@P5: our understanding of phylogenetic relationships within the section.
L124@P5: Several authors have also highlighted the potential importance of particular traits in the
L125@P5-6: evolution of section Codonoprasum. Similarities in traits among species may reflect either convergent evolution or shared evolutionary history. Integrating trait data with phylogenetic
L126@P6: trees allows us to distinguish whether observed trait patterns arise from independent evolutionary
L127@P6: changes or from similarity due to common ancestry (Blomberg et al. 2003, Gurushidze et al.
L128@P6: 2012, £vila-Lovera et al. 2023). Flowering time has frequently been mentioned as a trait
L129@P6: associated with deep phylogenetic divergence within the section, potentially linked to past
L130@P6: climatic conditions (e.g. Brullo et al. 2009, ÷zhatay et al. 2018). Other traits of potential
L131@P6: evolutionary relevance include genome size (Kobrlov· et al. 2024) and polyploidy (Han et al.
L132@P6: 2020). These traits may represent phenotypic characteristics whose evolution is causally
L133@P6: connected to environmental factors (Greilhuber and Leitch 2013, Faizullah et al. 2021), but they
L134@P6: may also display strong phylogenetic signal and evolve independently of environmental
L135@P6: influences (Bureš et al. 2023).
L136@P6: The main goals of the present study are therefore to: (i) infer phylogenetic relationships
L137@P6: within section Codonoprasum by sequencing three genomic loci (ITS, trnH-psbA, and trnLndhJ) using an expanded species dataset represented by multiple accessions, (ii) determine
L138@P6: whether molecular phylogenetic data support the monophyly of the section and provide evidence
L139@P6: for the delimitation of subsections, (iii) assess whether phylogenetic divergence exists within
L140@P6: some morphologically defined taxa, potentially carrying taxonomic implications, and (iv) map
L141@P6: selected plant traits onto the phylogenetic tree to identify phylogenetic structuring that may
L142@P6: support the delineation of lineages recovered in the current analyses.
L143@P6: Materials and Methods
L144@P6: Plant material and plant traits
L145@P6: The present study includes 154 newly sampled accessions representing 43 Allium taxa, including
L146@P6-7: several informally named entities. Sampling focused primarily on section Codonoprasum (41 taxa), but also incorporated species from several related sections to place the target group within
L147@P7: a broader phylogenetic context. Wherever possible, multiple individuals per species were
L148@P7: analysed. Additional sequences for 24 taxa were retrieved from GenBank and included in the
L149@P7: phylogenetic analyses. In total, 48 taxa of section Codonoprasum were included. Several recently
L150@P7: collected accessions could not be reliably identified, and some appear to represent yet
L151@P7: undescribed species. Such accessions are treated as potentially distinct taxa and are coded in the
L152@P7: trees and accompanying database at the sectional level using the abbreviation “A-cod” followed
L153@P7: by the population code. To provide an appropriate phylogenetic framework, nine species
L154@P7: belonging to sections closely related to section Codonoprasum were also analysed: section
L155@P7: Brevispatha Valsecchi (A. parciflorum Viv.), section Cupanioscordum Cheshm. (A. pentadactyli
L156@P7: Brullo, Pavone & Spamp., A. hirtovaginatum Kunth, A. cupani agg.), section Pseudoscorodon
L157@P7: Brullo, C.Brullo, Cambria, Giusso & Salmeri (A. obtusiflorum RedoutÈ) and section Allium (A.
L158@P7: vineale L., A. scorodoprasum L., A. chamaespathum Boiss., A. rotundum L.). In addition, four
L159@P7: phylogenetically distant Allium species (A. victorialis L., A. ursinum L., A. angulosum L., A.
L160@P7: tuberosum Rottler ex Spreng.) were included into analyses as outgroups. Plant material was
L161@P7: collected from natural populations as part of our ongoing research (Kobrlov· et al. 2024) and
L162@P7: subsequently cultivated in the experimental garden of Palack˝ University in Olomouc, Czech
L163@P7: Republic. All living material used for sequencing in the present study was sampled from
L164@P7: cultivated plants.
L165@P7: For accessions of section Codonoprasum, the following plant traits were compiled: (i)
L166@P7: ploidy level (x) and monoploid genome size (1Cx, in pg), both retrieved from Kobrlov· et al.
L167@P7: (2024); and (ii) flowering period (sensu ÷zhatay et al. 2018), categorised as early summer &
L168@P7: summer (up to middle August) versus late summer & autumn (from late August to October),
L169@P7: extracted from original species descriptions and regional floras. For several accessions lacking
L170@P7: previous estimates of ploidy and genome size, we determined these parameters using flow
L171@P7-8: cytometry following the same protocol and instrumentation as described in Kobrlov· et al. (2024). A complete list of analysed taxa, populations, and localities, associated plant traits,
L172@P8: voucher information, and GenBank accession numbers is provided in Supporting information,
L173@P8: Tables S1, S2.
L174@P8: DNA extraction, amplification and sequencing
L175@P8: Total genomic DNA was extracted from approximately 70 mg of fresh leaves following the
L176@P8: CTAB extraction protocol with two pre-washing steps using sorbitol solution (Inglis et al. 2018).
L177@P8: DNA quality was verified on a 1% agarose gel and its concentration measured using a Nanodrop
L178@P8: spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA). Based on previous phylogenetic studies
L179@P8: in Allium (Friesen et al. 2006, Salmeri et al. 2016, Han et al. 2020) and preliminary assessment
L180@P8: of polymorphisms (Vojtěchová et al., unpubl. results), we selected the internal transcribed spacer
L181@P8: (ITS) region of nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) and two plastid regions – 3'trnL
L182@P8: UAA-trnF-ndhJ
L183@P8: (hereafter referred to as trnL-ndhJ) and trnH
L184@P8: (GUG)-psbA (trnH-psbA) – for sequencing. The use
L185@P8: of ITS and trnH-psbA enabled us to incorporate publicly available nucleotide data (often derived
L186@P8: from type material or otherwise difficult-to-obtain taxa), although trnH-psbA has been reported
L187@P8: as less suitable for phylogenetic reconstruction within the section (Salmeri et al. 2016). The
L188@P8: second cpDNA region (trnL-ndhJ) was selected after evaluating nucleotide variability across
L189@P8: several other cpDNA regions in a subset of eight samples.
L190@P8: The PCR reactions were performed following Vojtěchová et al. (2024), using primers
L191@P8: and PCR conditions summarised in Table S3. PCR products were purified using a one-step
L192@P8: enzymatic purification protocol (Kim and Blackshaw 2001) and subsequently sequenced
L193@P8: bidirectionally by Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam).
L194@P8-9: DNA datasets, DNA sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis Nucleotide sequences obtained from each PCR product were edited and assembled into single
L195@P9: consensus sequences in Geneious 7.1.7 (Kearse et al. 2012). Partial regions were aligned using
L196@P9: MAFFT v7 (Katoh et al. 2019), and alignments were trimmed and concatenated in Geneious.
L197@P9: All newly generated sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers XXXX–
L198@P9: XXXX (after manuscript acceptance).
L199@P9: Phylogenetic analyses were performed on two accession datasets (Table 2). The first
L200@P9: (‘full’) dataset comprised 180 accessions (OTU) representing 57 taxa (species, subspecies, or
L201@P9: unclassified accessions of potentially new species assigned at the sectional level). This dataset
L202@P9: included 26 OTUs of 24 species for which ITS and trnH-psbA sequences were downloaded from
L203@P9: GenBank, and 154 OTUs of 43 taxa from our sampled material newly sequenced for ITS and
L204@P9: trnH-psbA. The second dataset (‘reduced’) was a subset of the full dataset and consisted of 154
L205@P9: OTUs from 43 taxa for which the trnL-ndhJ region was newly sequenced. Phylogenetic analyses
L206@P9: were conducted for single regions as well as concatenated regions of each dataset, resulting in
L207@P9: six (super)matrices (Table 2): F1: trnH-psbA of full dataset, F2: ITS of full dataset, F3:
L208@P9: concatenated ITS + trnH-psbA of full dataset, R1: concatenated both cpDNA regions of reduced
L209@P9: dataset, R2: ITS of reduced dataset, R3: concatenated ITS + both cpDNA of reduced dataset.
L210@P9: Allium ursinum and A. vineale were used as outgroups in analyses of the full and reduced
L211@P9: datasets, respectively.
L212@P9: For phylogenetic reconstruction under maximum parsimony (MP) we used MEGA
L213@P9: version 11 (Tamura et al. 2021), with 100 random addition sequence replications and TBR
L214@P9: branch-swapping algorithm. The best-scoring maximum likelihood (ML) trees were
L215@P9: reconstructed using RAxML (Stamatakis 2014) using specific substitution models autodetermined by ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) within IQ-TREE (Nguyen et al.
L216@P9: 2015, Trifinopoulos et al. 2016), using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Node support
L217@P9-10: was assessed using the ultrafast bootstrap approximation (Hoang et al. 2018) with 1,000 replicates. Bayesian phylogenetic trees (BA) were generated using MrBayes v3.2.6
L218@P10: (Huelsenbeck and Ronquist 2001, Ronquist and Huelsenbeck 2003) implemented in Geneious,
L219@P10: with the following settings: Substitution model: GTR+G, Rate variation: gamma, Gamma
L220@P10: Categories: 4, Chain Length: 5,000,000, Subsampling freq: 1,000, Heated chains: 4, Burn-in
L221@P10: Length: 100,000, Random seed: 5,000, Heated Chain Temp: 0.2. Bayesian 50% majority-rule
L222@P10: consensus trees were used to present relationships among accessions. Posterior probabilities (PP)
L223@P10: were classified as strong (≥0.95), moderate (0.94–0.75), or weak (0.50–0.74). Bootstrap support
L224@P10: (BS) values were categorised as strong (>85%), moderate (70–85%), weak (50–69%), or poor
L225@P10: (<50%). Phylogenetic trees were visualised in iTOL (Letunic and Bork 2016) and finalised in
L226@P10: Inkscape (inkscape.org).
L227@P10: Heterogeneity of phylogenetic signal among data partitions was examined using the
L228@P10: Incongruence Length Difference (ILD) test (Farris et al. 1994) for MP trees in PAUP* (Swofford
L229@P10: 2002). The ILD test was performed using parsimony-informative characters only, with 1,000
L230@P10: replications of heuristic searches, with 100 random addition analyses, and TBR branchswapping, using Steepest Descent, MaxTrees set to 1000, disabling the MULTREES option, and
L231@P10: excluded duplicates. In addition, the Congruence Among Distance Matrices (CADM) test was
L232@P10: conducted with 999 permutations (Campbell et al. 2011), using the R package ape (Paradis and
L233@P10: Schliep 2019), to evaluate congruence among distance matrices for ML trees.
L234@P10: Results
L235@P10: Molecular datasets
L236@P10: Descriptive statistics for the plastid and ITS alignments, together with the results of the
L237@P10-11: congruence tests for each dataset, are summarised in Table 2. The trnH-psbA alignment was 657 bp long in the full dataset and 578 bp long in the reduced dataset, whereas the trnL-ndhJ
L238@P11: alignment in the reduced dataset was 1001 bp long. Both plastid regions exhibited a low
L239@P11: proportion of parsimony-informative sites (PIS), typically less than 7% of the total alignment
L240@P11: length, although the proportion was approximately twice as high in trnL-ndhJ compared with
L241@P11: trnH-psbA. The ITS alignment in the full dataset was 687 bp long and contained 392 variable
L242@P11: characters, including 88 indels and 304 PIS. In the reduced dataset, the ITS alignment was 626
L243@P11: bp long and comprised 278 variable characters, including 23 indels and 255 PIS. The ITS region
L244@P11: therefore showed a markedly higher proportion of PIS than either plastid region, representing
L245@P11: 48% and 25% of all characters in the full and reduced datasets, respectively.
L246@P11: The consistency index (CI) and rescaled consistency index (RC) indicated a slightly
L247@P11: elevated level of homoplasy, particularly in the ITS region, whereas the retention index (RI)
L248@P11: exceeded 0.9 in most cases. ILD tests supported the homogeneity of phylogenetic signal among
L249@P11: data partitions under MP (Table 2). Only a weak signal of heterogeneity was detected in the
L250@P11: concatenated trnL-ndhJ + trnH-psbA matrix of the reduced dataset. Similarly, the CADM test
L251@P11: demonstrated congruence between the ITS and plastid region(s) in ML analyses (Table 2).
L252@P11: Molecular phylogenetic analyses
L253@P11: The MP, ML, and BI trees for each locus and for the concatenated datasets are largely congruent
L254@P11: in their overall topologies for ITS and the concatenated matrices within each dataset. In contrast,
L255@P11: the plastid DNA loci showed minor topological discrepancies between the MP/ML and BI
L256@P11: analyses. Here, we present the Bayesian trees, combining branch lengths and posterior
L257@P11: probabilities (PP) from BI with bootstrap support values from ML analyses. Partial differences
L258@P11: between the BI and ML trees are briefly noted below, whereas more detailed relationships and
L259@P11-12: their taxonomic implications are addressed in the Discussion to avoid redundancy. Plastid DNA sequences
L260@P12: In the trnH-psbA tree of the full dataset (matrix F1), the ML and BI trees have a similar topology,
L261@P12: except for the position of two outgroup taxa (A. angulosum and A. tuberosum) and three A.
L262@P12: flavum subsp. tauricum (Besser ex Rchb.) K.Richt. accessions from section Codonoprasum. In
L263@P12: the ML tree (Supporting information, Fig. S1), A. tuberosum is resolved in a more basal position,
L264@P12: while A. angulosum appears as sister to the weakly supported clade A (BS: 68%). Clade A forms
L265@P12: a basal polytomy composed of the tree A. flavum subsp. tauricum accessions and a moderately
L266@P12: supported subclade (BS: 78%) that includes all remaining accessions from sections
L267@P12: Codonoprasum, Brevispatha, Cupanioscordum and Pseudoscorodon. In contrast, in the BI tree
L268@P12: (Supporting information, Fig. S2), the two outgroup taxa together with the three A. flavum subsp.
L269@P12: tauricum accessions form a moderately supported ingroup (PP: 0.87) nested within clade A.
L270@P12: Clade A is sister to the clade comprising accessions of section Allium. Within clade A, a high
L271@P12: degree of polytomy is evident, and relationships among accessions remain largely unresolved.
L272@P12: Nevertheless, several moderately to strongly supported ingroups emerge in both analyses. For
L273@P12: instance, ingroup A1 includes some accessions of section Cupanioscordum (PP/BS: 1.00/96%),
L274@P12: while ingroup A2 contains strictly Mediterranean and Caucasian members of section
L275@P12: Codonoprasum (0.97/87%) (Supporting information, Figs S1, S2). Even within these ingroups,
L276@P12: polytomies persist, although some moderately to strongly supported subclades are reconstructed,
L277@P12: typically grouping accessions of species with similar and geographically restricted distributions.
L278@P12: The tree based on the concatenated cpDNA regions of the reduced dataset (matrix R1;
L279@P12: Supporting information, Fig. S3) supports the monophyly of section Codonoprasum, although
L280@P12: two accessions of A. cupanii agg. from section Cupanioscordum cluster together with all
L281@P12: analysed Codonoprasum accessions in a strongly supported clade A (1.00/100%). Within this
L282@P12-13: clade, two moderately to strongly supported subclades are recovered. The first subclade (0.86/81%) contains two strongly supported sister groups. One of them (A1) consists exclusively
L283@P13: of accessions of section Cupanioscordum (1.00/100%). Its sister group (A2) includes strictly
