L20@P5: Magnolias and the people who grow them are a common thread that can be traced throughout
L21@P5: my horticultural career. In the late 1960s, I was able to see flowers of the Asiatic tree magnolias,
L22@P5: particularly Magnolia campbellii and M. sargentiana, in the Valley Garden, Windsor, for the first
L23@P5: time, and thus became aware of the magnificence of these garden aristocrats. Prior to this my
L24@P5: knowledge had been restricted to the ever-present M. × soulangeana and M. stellata; beautiful as
L25@P5: they were in their many settings across suburban Britain, they did not have the ‘wow’ factor of a
L26@P5: tree magnolia in full flowering glory! Visits to many of the Cornish gardens at this time brought
L27@P5: home the sense of ‘wonderment’ that the early twentieth-century plant collectors Ernest Wilson
L28@P5: and Frank Kingdon Ward must have felt when they saw them in the wild:
L29@P5: Suddenly round a corner we come on that first magnolia in bloom. It is just below us and we look
L30@P5: right into the heart of the tree, sprouting with blossom. The sight overwhelms us. After that we
L31@P5: see scores of trees, some glowing pink, others with ivory-white flowers. From our giddy ledge
L32@P5: we look down over the wide waves of the forest beating against the cliff, where the magnolia
L33@P5: blooms toss like white horses, or lie like a fleet of pink water lilies at anchor in a sea of green surf.
L34@P5: (Kingdon Ward, 1930)
L35@P5: Yet this enthusiasm for seeing magnolias flowering for the first time is not restricted to the
L36@P5: wild. Seeing a new cultivar or species flowering for the first time in cultivation can be equally
L37@P5: appealing, as one is sharing this joy with other ‘kindred spirits’. Magnolia pseudokobus is a
L38@P5: species that has not long been in Western cultivation, and was first described by Ueda (1986)
L39@P5: from Shikoku in Japan. I and a number of fellow enthusiasts first saw this species in flower in
L40@P5: Karl Flinck’s garden in south-west Sweden, and we were struck by its flower shape and the fact
L41@P5: that its scent was quite distinct from that of other magnolias, being ‘earthier’ rather than sweet.
L42@P5: It was during the 1980s, while I was curator at the Sir Harold Hillier Gardens and Arboretum,
L43@P5: that I joined the Magnolia Society. This was established in the USA during the early 1960s.
L44@P5: Sir Harold Hillier had been a member of the Society from the outset, so I was able to read its
L45@P5: newsletters and journals in his library. It was while I was at the Arboretum that I wrote my first book
L46@P5: on magnolias, ‘Magnolias – Their Care and Cultivation’ (Gardiner, 1989), enabling me to correspond
L47@P5: with many of the names that I knew or had read about. In particular, Phil Savage (Bloomfield Hills,
L48@P5: Michigan) and plantsman Roy Lancaster were always generous with their knowledge, helping me to
L49@P5: gain a more thorough understanding of this great genus. When I became president of the Magnolia
L50@P5: Society during the late 1990s it became evident to me how friendly and approachable everyone
L51@P5: is who has an interest in this group of plants. Irrespective of their background or their country of
L52@P5: origin, they are willing to share their knowledge, whether the question is simple or complex.
L53@P5: Many of my best friends are members – an indication of the camaraderie that exists.
L54@P5: Not long after starting at the Royal Horticultural Society’s Garden at Wisley, Pauline Dean
L55@P5: introduced herself to me. She was an excellent botanical artist whose first major exhibition for
L56@P5: the RHS was on the genus Iris in 1989. She was also a great communicator, and in 1990 she
L57@P5: set up the first weekend botanical illustration workshop, which ran successfully for 12 years.
L58@P5: Quite early on I asked her to paint Magnolia sprengeri ‘Diva’ for my wife Allison and myself.

L103@P11: This ‘elegant aristocrat of American trees’ (commonly known as southern
L104@P11: magnolia or bull bay) is a large, handsome tree of the south-eastern USA,
L105@P11: from North Carolina south to central Florida and west to eastern Texas
L106@P11: and Arkansas. It grows only within a few hundred miles of the Atlantic
L107@P11: Ocean or the Gulf of Mexico. It can be found along the bluffs of the
L108@P11: Lower Mississippi River and on the borders of river swamps and ponds,
L109@P11: where it grows in association with Quercus nigra (water oak), Liquidambar
L110@P11: styraciflua (sweet gum) and Nyssa sylvatica (tupelo, sour gum). It can be
L111@P11: seen growing as a large forest tree, but is also found as scrub amongst
L112@P11: the sand dunes overlooking the Gulf of Mexico. It was commonly planted
L113@P11: in front of early homesteads in the American South, often with the lower
L114@P11: branches pegged down so that they rooted as layers. This practice not only
L115@P11: provided replacement plants, but also made the tree virtually impregnable
L116@P11: against the prevalent hurricane-force winds.
L117@P11: This magnolia is regarded as a symbol of the American South, being
L118@P11: the state flower of Mississippi and Louisiana, as well as a symbol
L119@P11: of the Confederacy.
L120@P11: It is thought that the first introduction of Magnolia grandiflora into
L121@P11: European gardens was during the early eighteenth century, when the
L122@P11: English, French and Spanish first introduced the species. However, the
L123@P11: northern French city of Nantes claims to have introduced it first, in 1711.
L124@P11: The species has since become one of the most popularly planted evergreen
L125@P11: plants around the world in warm temperate and subtropical areas.
L126@P11: In their native environment, trees of this species reach 27.5 m (90 ft)
L127@P11: in height. In south-east England, 12 m (40 ft) is the maximum height
L128@P11: that can be expected, but sites in the south of France or Italy, especially
L129@P11: bordering on the Riviera, produce specimens as large as those found in
L130@P11: North America. One of the largest specimens seen is on the Borromean
L131@P11: island of Isola Madre in Lake Maggiore, Italy, being about 18 m (60 ft) in
L132@P11: height and 12 m (40 ft) in spread.
L133@P11: There are over 100 cultivars listed, originating not only in North America
L134@P11: but also in Britain and France. The following represent some of the more
L135@P11: popular cultivars, together with some of the more recently named ones,
L136@P11: as well as illustrating the variation within the species. The majority of plants
L137@P11: in European gardens are named cultivars, such as ‘Angustifolia’, ‘Exmouth’,
L138@P11: ‘Galissonniere’ and ‘Goliath’, but many of the plants seen in North American
L139@P11: gardens are of seedling origin and are unnamed. However, since the 1970s,
L140@P11: initially in North America, there has been a move to propagate Magnolia
L141@P11: grandiflora from cuttings, thus offering gardeners plants whose habit, leaf
L142@P11: shape and colour, flower size and growth response can be predicted.
L143@P11:  ‘Charles Dickens’ is a broad-spreading cultivar with very large
L144@P11: flowers up to 15 cm (12 in) in diameter, and broad green leaves that
L145@P11: have a distinctive pale appearance on first opening. It was introduced
L146@P11: into cultivation by Jewel Templeton, who found it growing in the
L147@P11: garden of Charles Dickens in Franklin County, Tennessee. It has very
L148@P11: large bright red fruit.

L170@P16: Magnolia macrophylla (commonly known as big leaf magnolia) has a
L171@P16: restricted distribution in the south-eastern USA, being found in Georgia,
L172@P16: Alabama, Mississippi and Louisiana, and northwards to western Virginia,
L173@P16: Kentucky, Arkansas and Ohio. It was first discovered by André Michaux
L174@P16: in the mountains of South Carolina in 1759, and was introduced into
L175@P16: Britain in 1800.
L176@P16: In its native habit it is a small to medium-sized tree, 7.5 m (25 ft)
L177@P16: to 15 m (50 ft) in height, and varying in habit from a single erect to a
L178@P16: multistemmed spreading crown. In the UK it seldom reaches a height
L179@P16: of more than 12 m (40 ft).
L180@P16: This rare tree is found either individually or in small groups in
L181@P16: sheltered woodlands, especially in ravines and river valleys along with
L182@P16: Liquidambar styraciflua (sweet gum), Liriodendron tulipifera (tulip tree)
L183@P16: and Quercus texana (southern red oak). Rich, deep, moisture-retentive
L184@P16: soils just on either the acid or the alkaline side of neutral are best for
L185@P16: this species, which also requires a sunny yet sheltered site so that the
L186@P16: flamboyant foliage display is not severely reduced.
L187@P16: Magnolia macrophylla subsp. dealbata is a small to medium-sized single
L188@P16: fastigiate or more generally multistemmed tree, and is the only deciduous
L189@P16: magnolia found in Central America. It grows in the forests of the southern
L190@P16: and eastern central mountain ranges in the states of Oaxaca, San Luis
L191@P16: Potosi and Veracruz with Liquidambar (sweetgum) and Quercus (oak)
L192@P16: species at an altitude of 1,200–1,500 m (4,000–5,000 ft), where it is
L193@P16: seen in both sun and shade.
L194@P16: At Easter each year, many of these trees are pruned by the Mexican
L195@P16: people, who use the flowers to decorate their churches. The leaves, when
L196@P16: boiled, are also used medicinally as a poultice and applied to sore tendons.
L197@P16: This subspecies was the first magnolia to be printed in European literature.
L198@P16: Francisco Hernandez, the court physician to King Philip II of Spain, was
L199@P16: sent in 1570 to explore ‘New Spain’ (Mexico), and described this magnolia
L200@P16: in Plantarum Historia Mexicana in 1631.
L201@P16: The subspecies was introduced into Britain by Sir Harold Hillier during
L202@P16: the 1970s, and again by James Russell in the early 1980s. It is thought to
L203@P16: be reasonably hardy, being similar to Magnolia macrophylla subsp. ashei.
L204@P16: Until comparatively recently it was regarded as a species in its own right.
L205@P16: However, in 1989, Dorothy Johnson of the L. H. Bailey Hortorium,
L206@P16: Cornell University, Ithaca, and more recently, in 1993, John Tobe of
L207@P16: the Florida Department of Environmental Protection, Tallahassee, both
L208@P16: proposed its subspecific status.

L255@P20: This multistemmed large shrub or broad-spreading small tree is probably
L256@P20: the best known and most widely planted of all magnolias; it is the
L257@P20: archetypal magnolia. On driving into most towns and cities, especially
L258@P20: in southern England, during late March and early April, one is instantly
L259@P20: aware of its presence. White, pink and reddish-purple blooms are seen in
L260@P20: profusion on every major road or tucked under the lee of boundary walls.
L261@P20: The story of Magnolia × soulangeana can be traced back to the early
L262@P20: nineteenth century. Europe was just recovering from the Napoleonic
L263@P20: wars, during which vast armies had travelled in all directions across the
L264@P20: continent. Etienne Soulange-Bodin, a cavalry officer in Napoleon’s army,
L265@P20: had seen great botanical collections of the day at Schonbrunn (Vienna),
L266@P20: Stuttgart and Moscow during the campaigns, and had been saddened by
L267@P20: what he saw, suggesting that it would have been ‘better for both parties
L268@P20: to have stayed at home and planted their cabbages’. On his return from
L269@P20: the war, Soulange-Bodin founded the Royal Institute of Horticulture at
L270@P20: Fromont, near Paris, and became its first director. It was here in 1820 that
L271@P20: he pollinated Magnolia denudata with M. liliiflora. This flowered for the
L272@P20: first time in 1827 and ‘was remarkable for its tree like habit … and above
L273@P20: all for its wide spreading brilliant flowers, in which the purest white is
L274@P20: tinged with a purplish hue’.
L275@P20: With the upturn of economic fortunes in Britain during the 1820s,
L276@P20: new plants were being introduced by the (Royal) Horticultural Society
L277@P20: and nursery firms, including Soulange-Bodin’s first batch of Magnolia
L278@P20: × soulangeana. J. C. Loudon reported in the Gardener’s Magazine of
L279@P20: 1834 that this hybrid had ‘produced throughout April this year the most
L280@P20: beautiful effect in the Vauxhall nursery belonging to Messrs. Chandler &
L281@P20: Sons where it is trained against a wall exposed to the west’. Thus our
L282@P20: love affair with Magnolia × soulangeana began.
L283@P20: Cultivars of Magnolia × soulangeana have been raised by numerous
L284@P20: hybridisers in Europe (including Britain), Japan and North America. It is
L285@P20: probable that earlier hybrids existed in Japan prior to 1830, as it is likely
L286@P20: that both plants were growing in close proximity to each other in temple
L287@P20: gardens or nurseries. However, since that time both second- and thirdgeneration hybrids have been raised, providing a complexity of colour
L288@P20: forms from milky white through various shades of pink to an intense
L289@P20: reddish purple, while some are bi-coloured. Flower shape ranges from a
L290@P20: tulip shape to a cup-and-saucer shape and a goblet shape, while the size
L291@P20: of the blooms and the time of flowering are also quite variable, depending
L292@P20: on the cultivar and the season. In the south of England, the peak
L293@P20: flowering period is generally from the last week of March to the end of
L294@P20: April or early May, whereas in California, flowering starts in mid-February.
L295@P20: Even young plants only 2 or 3 years of age produce flowers in quantity.
L296@P20: The flowers have nine tepals and are generally of a firm constitution as
L297@P20: well as being fairly weather resistant, although they are not frost tolerant.
L298@P20: In habit they are all broad-spreading multistemmed shrubs, which in
L299@P20: time develop into plants with tree-like proportions that are generally freestanding. Their shape and size can be controlled by pruning, which is
L300@P20: done either immediately after flowering or during late summer.
L301@P20: More than 100 cultivar names of Magnolia × soulangeana are
L302@P20: recorded. Many of these have stood the test of time, having been
L303@P20: raised in the nineteenth century, while others are more recent.
L304@P20: The plant most widely cultivated as Magnolia × soulangeana has
L305@P20: cup-and-saucer-shaped flowers that are a beautiful warm pink in bud,
L306@P20: opening to white inside with flushed pink on the outside, deepening
L307@P20: to a purplish pink at the base. Consideration should perhaps be given
L308@P20: to providing the cultivar name ‘Étienne Soulange-Bodin’ for this widely
L309@P20: grown clone.
L310@P20: ‘Alba Superba’ is one of the earliest cultivars to flower. The cupand-saucer-shaped flowers have pure white tepals with basal blotches
L311@P20: of pink or purple. This cultivar was raised in Belgium during the midnineteenth century.
L312@P20: ‘Brozzonii’ is a second-generation seedling that was raised in the
L313@P20: garden of Camillo Brozzoni at Brescia, Italy, in 1873.
L314@P20: ‘Burgundy’ is a floriferous, early-flowering hybrid that was raised
L315@P20: by W. B. Clarke of San Jose in the 1930s, and named in 1943. It was
L316@P20: introduced into Britain in the 1960s. The flowers are rose pink in colour
L317@P20: and open to 20 cm (8 in) across. In North America, where light conditions
L318@P20: differ from those in the UK, the flowers are a deep purple-red that better
L319@P20: justifies the cultivar name.
L320@P20: ‘Lennei’ is an important cultivar that should not be overlooked. It
L321@P20: was raised in the province of Lombardy, northern Italy, during the midnineteenth century, by Guiseppe Manetti, who named it after Peter Joseph
L322@P20: Lenne (1787–1866), a German botanist. It was introduced into the trade
L323@P20: by A. Topf, a nurseryman based in Erfurt, Prussia. The tulip-shaped flowers
L324@P20: are rose-purple on the outside and white within.
L325@P20: ‘Norbertii’ has goblet-shaped slightly scented white flowers flushed
L326@P20: with pink, with tepals about 10 cm (4 in) long. It flowers in early April,
L327@P20: and was introduced by Cels of Montrouge, Paris.
L328@P20: ‘Picture’ (syn. ‘Wada’s Picture’) is a Japanese cultivar introduced by
L329@P20: Wada’s Nursery of Yokohama in the 1930s. It has one of the largest
L330@P20: flowers, with tepals 15–17.5 cm (6–7 in) in length and typically cup-andsaucer-shaped flowers, which are white or sometimes a pale pinkish white
L331@P20: on the inside with heavy red-purple staining on the outside. There are
L332@P20: almost certainly two clones of ‘Picture’ in cultivation in the UK. Both have
L333@P20: very large flowers, one with a more clearly defined colour separation with
L334@P20: far more white in the flower.
L335@P20: ‘Rustica Rubra’ is a Dutch cultivar that was raised at the end of the
L336@P20: nineteenth century in Boskoop. The classic goblet-shaped flowers are
L337@P20: reddish-purple on the outside and pink-white within, and appear in
L338@P20: mid-season (mid-April).
L339@P20: ‘White Giant’ was introduced by Wada’s Nursery of Yokohama in
L340@P20: Japan. It has very large goblet-shaped milky white flowers, which ‘splay’
L341@P20: open on their second day, very much like Magnolia grandiflora does!
