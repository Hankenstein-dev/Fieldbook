L214: Leucanthemum vulgare, commonly known as the ox-eye daisy, oxeye daisy, dog daisy, marguerite (cite120†French : Marguerite commune, "common marguerite") and other common names,^{cite121†[2] } is a widespread cite110†flowering plant native to cite122†Europe and the temperate regions of cite123†Asia , and an introduced plant to cite124†North America , cite125†Australia and cite126†New Zealand .
L215: ## Description
L216: 
L217: [cite127†edit ]
L218: L. vulgare is a cite128†perennial cite129†herb that grows to a height of 80 centimetres (31 inches)^{cite130†[3] } and has a creeping underground cite131†rhizome . The lower parts of the stem are hairy, sometimes densely hairy but more or less cite132†glabrous†en.wiktionary.org in the lower parts. The largest leaves are at the base of the plant and are 4–15 cm (1+1⁄2–6 in) long, about 5 cm (2 in) wide and have a cite133†petiole . These leaves have up to 15 teeth, or lobes or both on the edges.
L219: The leaves decrease in size up the stem, the upper leaves up to 7.5 cm (3 in) long, lack a petiole and are deeply toothed.^{cite121†[2] }^{cite134†[4] }^{cite135†[5] }^{cite136†[6] }^{cite137†[7] }
L220: The plant bears up to three "flowers" like those of a typical cite114†daisy . Each is a "head" or cite138†capitulum 2–7.5 cm (3⁄4–3 in) wide.^{cite130†[3] } Each head has between fifteen and forty white "petals" (cite139†ray florets ) 1–2 centimetres (3⁄8–3⁄4 in) long surrounding the yellow disc florets. Below the head is an involucre of glabrous green cite140†bracts 7–10 millimetres (1⁄4–3⁄8 inch) long with brownish edges.
L221: Flowering occurs from May to October.^{cite130†[3] } The seed-like cite141†achenes are 1–3 mm (1⁄16–1⁄8 in) long and have ten "ribs" along their edges but lack a cite142†pappus .^{cite121†[2] }^{cite134†[4] }^{cite135†[5] }
L222: Ox-eye daisy is similar to shasta daisy (cite143†Leucanthemum × superbum ) which has larger flower heads (5–12 cm or 2–4+3⁄4 in wide) and to stinking chamomile (cite144†Anthemis cotula ) which has smaller heads (1.5–3 cm or 5⁄8–1+1⁄8 in wide).^{cite134†[4] } cite145†L. maximum is also similar, usually with rays 2–3 cm (3⁄4–1+1⁄8 in) in length.^{cite130†[3] }

L227: ## Taxonomy
L228: 
L229: [cite147†edit ]
L230: 
L231: L. vulgare was first formally described in 1778 by cite117†Jean-Baptiste Lamarck , who published the description in Flore françoise.^{cite118†[1] }^{cite148†[8] }^{cite149†[9] } It is also known by the common names ox-eye daisy, dog daisy, field daisy, Marguerite, moon daisy, moon-penny, poor-land penny, poverty daisy and white daisy.^{cite121†[2] }
L232: 
L233: The species was formerly described as part of the cite150†Chrysanthemum genus.^{cite130†[3] }
L234: ## Distribution and habitat
L235: 
L236: [cite151†edit ]
L237: 
L238: Leucanthemum L.,^{cite152†[10] } field in cite153†Magdalen Island , Canada
L239: The species is native to cite122†Europe , and to cite154†Turkey and cite155†Georgia in cite156†Western Asia . It is a typical cite157†grassland perennial cite158†wildflower , growing in a variety of cite159†plant communities including cite160†meadows and cite161†fields , under cite162†scrub and open-canopy forests, and in disturbed areas. The species is widely cite163†naturalised in many parts of the world, including cite124†North America ,^{cite130†[3] } and is considered to be an cite164†invasive species in more than forty countries.
L240: It grows in temperate regions where average annual rainfall exceeds 750 mm (30 in), and often where soils are heavy and damp. It is often a weed of degraded pastures and roadsides.^{cite134†[4] }^{cite136†[6] }^{cite165†[11] }^{cite166†[12] }^{cite167†[13] }
L241: ## Ecology
L242: 
L243: [cite168†edit ]
L244: The species spreads by seeds and by shallow, creeping cite131†rhizomes . A mature plant can produce up to 26,000 seeds that are spread by animals, vehicles, water and contaminated agricultural produce, and some seeds remain viable for up to nearly forty years. It is not palatable to cattle and reduces the amount of quality pasture available for grazing.
L245: In native landscapes such as the cite169†Kosciuszko National Park in Australia, dense infestation can exclude native plants, causing soil erosion and loss of soil organic matter.^{cite134†[4] }^{cite136†[6] }^{cite166†[12] }^{cite167†[13] }
L246: This plant was top-ranked for pollen production per floral unit sampled at the level of the entire capitulum, with a value of 15.9 ± 2 μL, in a cite170†UK study of meadow flowers.^{cite171†[14] }
L247: ### As an invasive species
L248: 
L249: [cite172†edit ]
L250: 
L251: Infestation in native pasture near cite173†Guyra in Australia
L252: L. vulgare is one of the most widespread weeds in the cite174†Anthemideae . It became an cite175†introduced species via gardens into cite176†natural areas in parts of cite177†Canada ,^{cite178†[15] } the cite179†United States ,^{cite180†[16] } cite125†Australia ,^{cite134†[4] } and cite126†New Zealand .^{cite181†[17] } In some cite182†habitats it forms dense colonies displacing cite183†native plants and modifying existing communities.^{cite165†[11] }^{cite184†[18] }^{cite185†[19] }
L253: The plant commonly invades cite186†lawns , and is difficult to control or eradicate, since a new plant can regenerate from rhizome fragments^{cite165†[11] } and is a problem in pastures where beef and dairy cattle graze, as usually they will not eat it, thus enabling it to spread;^{cite181†[17] } cows who do eat it produce milk with an undesirable flavor.^{cite187†[20] } It has been shown to carry several crop diseases.^{cite188†[21] }
L254: This species has been declared an environmental weed in cite189†New South Wales and cite190†Victoria . In New South Wales it grows from cite191†Glen Innes on the cite192†Northern Tablelands to cite193†Bombala in the far southeast of the state, and there are significant populations in the Kosciuszko National Park where it has invaded subalpine grassland, snowgum (cite194†Eucalyptus pauciflora ) woodland and wetlands. In Victoria it is a prohibited species and must be eradicated or controlled.^{cite134†[4] }^{cite195†[22] }

L266: ### Tea
L267: 
L268: [cite205†edit ]
L269: 
L270: Oxeye grows wild in the Arab Valley in Southern Palestine, where the flowers are picked and dried and used by Palestinians to make a local variety of herbal tea.
L271: ### Use in horticulture
L272: 
L273: [cite206†edit ]
L274: L. vulgare is widely cultivated and available as a perennial flowering cite207†ornamental plant for gardens and designed meadow cite208†landscapes . It thrives in a wide range of conditions but prefers a sunny or part-sun location of average soil that is damp (like many in the daisy family). The plant does well in raised and mulched garden beds that retain moisture and prevent weeds. It is a cite209†mesophyte and therefore requires more or less a continuous water supply.
L275: The heads of faded and old blooms are often cite210†deadheaded to promote further blooming and to maintain the appearance of the plant. There are cite211†cultivars , such as 'May Queen', that begin blooming in early spring.

Over time oxeye daisy has become more popular as it is native to Denmark, in contrast to the marguerite daisy.^{cite220†[26] }
L286: 
L287: It is also a cite221†popular symbol of Latvia . In an open vote by the botanical society in 1994 it was selected as the national flower.^{cite222†[27] }
