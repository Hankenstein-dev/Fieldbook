L206: ## Description
L207: 
L208: [cite145†edit ]
L209: 
L210: Macadamia is a genus of cite146†evergreen trees that grows 2–12 m (7–40 ft) tall.
L211: The leaves are arranged in cite147†whorls of three to six, cite148†lanceolate to cite149†obovate or cite150†elliptic in cite151†shape , 60–300 mm (2+1⁄2–12 in) long and 30–130 mm (1+1⁄8–5+1⁄8 in) broad, with an entire or spiny-serrated margin. The flowers are produced in a long, slender, and simple cite152†raceme 50–300 mm (2–12 in) long, the individual flowers 10–15 mm (3⁄8–9⁄16 in) long, white to pink or purple, with four cite153†tepals .
L212: The fruit is a hard, woody, globose cite154†follicle with a pointed apex containing one or two seeds. The nutshell ("coat") is particularly tough and requires around 2000 cite155†N to crack. The shell material is five times harder than hazelnut shells and has mechanical properties similar to aluminum. It has a cite156†Vickers hardness of 35.^{cite157†[8] }^{cite158†[9] }

L250: 
L251: [cite201†edit ]

L254: ## Cultivation
L255: 
L256: [cite207†edit ]
L257: 
L258: Macadamia integrifolia flowers
L259: The macadamia tree is usually propagated by cite208†grafting . It does not begin to produce commercial quantities of seeds until it is 7–10 years old, but once established, it may continue bearing for over 100 years. Macadamias prefer fertile, well-drained soils, a rainfall of 1,000–2,000 mm (40–80 in), and temperatures not falling below 10 °C (50 °F) (although once established, they can withstand light frosts), with an optimum temperature of 25 °C (80 °F).
L260: The roots are shallow, and trees can be blown down in storms; like most cite117†Proteaceae , they are also susceptible to cite209†Phytophthora root disease. As of 2019, the macadamia nut is the most expensive nut in the world, which is attributed to the slow harvesting process.^{cite210†[16] }

L262: ### Cultivars
L263: 
L264: [cite211†edit ]
L265: #### Beaumont
L266: 
L267: [cite212†edit ]
L268: A cite123†Macadamia integrifolia / cite128†M. tetraphylla hybrid commercial variety is widely planted in Australia and New Zealand; Dr. J. H. Beaumont discovered it. It is high in oil but is not sweet. New leaves are reddish, and flowers are bright pink, borne on long racemes. It is one of the quickest varieties to come into bearing once planted in the garden, usually carrying a useful crop by the fourth year and improving from then on. It crops prodigiously when well pollinated.
L269: The impressive, grape-like clusters are sometimes so heavy they break the branchlets to which they are attached. Commercial orchards have reached 18 kg (40 lb) per tree by eight years old. On the downside, the macadamias do not drop from the tree when ripe, and the leaves are a bit prickly when one reaches into the tree's interior during harvest. Its shell is easier to open than that of most commercial varieties.
L270: Macadamia 'Maroochy' new growth
L271: #### Maroochy
L272: 
L273: [cite213†edit ]
L274: 
L275: A pure cite128†M. tetraphylla variety from Australia, this strain is cultivated for its productive crop yield, flavour, and suitability for pollinating 'Beaumont.'
L276: #### Nelmac II
L277: 
L278: [cite214†edit ]
L279: A South African M. integrifolia / M. tetraphylla hybrid cultivar, it has a sweet seed, which means it must be cooked carefully so that the sugars do not caramelise. The sweet seed is usually not fully processed, as it generally does not taste as good, but many people enjoy eating it uncooked. It has an open micropyle (hole in the shell), which may let in fungal spores. The crack-out percentage (ratio of nut meat to the whole nut by weight) is high. Ten-year-old trees average 22 kg (50 lb) per tree.
L280: It is a popular variety because of its pollination of 'Beaumont,' and the yields are almost comparable.

L285: A M. integrifolia / M. tetraphylla hybrid, this is a rather spreading tree. On the plus side, it is high-yielding commercially; 17 kg (37 lb) from a 9-year-old tree has been recorded, and the nuts drop to the ground. However, they are thick-shelled, with not much flavour.
L286: ## Production
L287: 
L288: [cite216†edit ]
L289: 
L290: In 2024, South Africa was the leading producer of macadamia nuts, with 87,000 tonnes,^{cite217†[17] } up from 77,000 tonnes in 2023^{cite218†[18] } and 54,000 tonnes out of global production of 211,000 tonnes in 2018.^{cite219†[19] } Macadamia is commercially produced in many countries of East Africa, Southeast Asia, South America, Australia, and North America having cite220†Mediterranean , cite221†temperate or cite222†tropical climates .^{cite219†[19] }
L291: ### History
L292: 
L293: [cite223†edit ]
L294: The first commercial orchard of macadamia trees was planted in the early 1880s by Rous Mill, 12 km (7.5 mi) southeast of cite224†Lismore, New South Wales , consisting of cite128†M. tetraphylla .^{cite225†[20] } Besides the development of a small boutique industry in Australia during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, macadamia was extensively planted as a commercial crop in Hawaii from the 1920s onward. Macadamia seeds were first imported into Hawaii in 1882 by cite226†William H.
L295: Purvis , who planted seeds that year at cite227†Kapulena .^{cite228†[21] } The Hawaiian-produced macadamia established the well-known seed internationally, and in 2017, Hawaii produced over 22,000 tonnes.^{cite229†[22] }
L296: In 2019, researchers collected samples from hundreds of trees in cite135†Queensland and compared their genetic profiles to samples from Hawaiian orchards. They determined that essentially all the Hawaiian trees must have descended from a small population of Australian trees from cite230†Gympie , possibly just a single tree.^{cite231†[23] } This lack of genetic diversity in the commercial crop puts it at risk of succumbing to pathogens (as has happened in the past to banana cultivars).
L297: Growers may seek to diversify the cultivated population by hybridizing with wild specimens.
L298: ### Shelling
L299: 
L300: [cite232†edit ]
L301: 
L302: Screw-type macadamia nut cracker on a plate of unshelled macadamias
L303: 
L304: Macadamias are the world's hardest edible nut to crack.^{cite233†[24] } Since ordinary nutcrackers apply insufficient force,^{cite234†[25] } various types of specialist macadamia nut crackers are available, many of which apply force to the cite235†micropyle , visible as a white dot, to fracture the shell.^{cite233†[24] }
L305: For commercial scale deshelling, rotating steel rollers are used.^{cite234†[25] } In South Africa, the average crack-out rate, meaning the ratio of usable nut to discarded shell, is 27.6% nut to 72.4% waste.^{cite234†[25] }

L311: ### Allergen
L312: 
L313: [cite237†edit ]
L314: Macadamia allergy is a type of food allergy to macadamia nuts which is relatively rare, affecting less than 5% of people with cite238†tree nut allergy in the United States.^{cite239†[26] } Macadamia allergy can cause mild to severe allergic reactions, such as cite240†oral allergy syndrome , cite241†urticaria , cite242†angioedema , vomiting, abdominal pain, cite243†asthma , and cite244†anaphylaxis .^{cite245†[27] } Macadamia allergy can also cross-react with other tree nuts or foods that have similar allergenic proteins, such as coconut, walnut, hazelnut, and cashew.^{cite246†[28] } The diagnosis and management of macadamia allergy involves avoiding macadamia nuts and their derivatives, reading food labels carefully, carrying an cite247†epinephrine auto-injector in case of severe reactions, and consulting a doctor for further testing and advice.
L315: ### Toxicity in dogs and cats
L316: 
L317: [cite248†edit ]
L318: Macadamias are toxic to dogs. Ingestion may result in macadamia toxicity marked by weakness and hind limb paralysis with the inability to stand, occurring within 12 hours of ingestion.^{cite249†[29] } It is not known what makes macadamia nuts toxic in dogs.^{cite250†[30] } Depending on the quantity ingested and the size of the dog, symptoms may also include muscle tremors, joint pain, and severe abdominal pain.
L319: In high doses of toxin, opiate medication may be required for symptom relief until the toxic effects diminish, with full recovery usually within 24 to 48 hours.^{cite249†[29] }
L320: Macadamias are also toxic to cats, causing tremors, paralysis, joint stiffness, and high fever.^{cite251†[31] }
