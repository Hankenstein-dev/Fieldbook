L17@P0: Abstract
L18@P0: Previous molecular phylogenetic studies of Pelargonium have remained inconclusive with respect to branching patterns
L19@P0: of major infrageneric lineages, with the exception of a basalmost generic split that reflects chromosome length
L20@P0: differences. Because of this and the lack of clearly distinguishing morphological characters, no subgeneric classification
L21@P0: has been undertaken so far. Here, we present increased phylogenetic signal using chloroplast atpB-rbcL spacer and trnLF sequences including additional taxa (110 taxa in total) and character sampling. All analyses confirmed the previously
L22@P0: recognised first split into two clades characterised by chromosome size, and also converged on four major clades (two
L23@P0: within each chromosome size group). The four major clades are further supported by synapomorphic length mutations
L24@P0: from both intergenic spacers. The evolution of characters from flower morphology and phenolic constituents was
L25@P0: examined for usefulness for clade delimitation. Although character state distributions did not generally reveal clear
L26@P0: synapomorphies for the respective lineages, differences in state distributions of floral characters and leaf phenols support
L27@P0: the circumscription of these major clades. In particular, nectar guides and petal-ratios indicate character state shifts
L28@P0: among clades. The leaf flavonoids, myricetin and prodelphinidin, exhibit differing evolutionary trends in Pelargonium
L29@P0: species with small chromosomes. In summary, all results favour the recognition of four, morphologically diagnosable,
L30@P0: lineages as subgenera and support a revised subgeneric classification of Pelargonium. In addition, a new section (stat.
L31@P0: nov.) including two subsections (comb. nov.) is segregated from an otherwise paraphyletic section Polyactium.
L32@P0: Key words: atpB-rbcL, flavonoids, floral traits, indels, trnL-F
L33@P0: Introduction
L34@P0: Pelargonium L’Héritier in Aiton (1789: 417) is an important genus of the horticulturally valuable Geranium family,
L35@P0: which consists of about 800 mostly herbaceous species with a worldwide, predominantly temperate to subtropical
L36@P0: distribution (Albers & van der Walt 2007, Fiz et al. 2008). Within Geraniaceae, the genus Pelargonium is sister to
L37@P0: the remaining genera of the family in its strict sense (Price & Palmer 1993), Erodium L’Héritier in Aiton (1789:
L38@P0: 414), Geranium Linnaeus (1737: 204) and Monsonia Linnaeus (1767: 508) incl. Sarcocaulon (De Candolle) Sweet
L39@P0: (1826: 73). Pelargonium represents the second largest genus (about 280 taxa) of Geraniaceae and is
L40@P0: morphologically distinct from the remainder of the family in having a hypanthium, consisting of an adnate nectar
L41@P0: spur with one nectary, as well as a generally zygomorphic floral symmetry (Albers & van der Walt 2007).
L42@P0: The main distribution of nearly 90% of the genus is in southern Africa, including the Republic of South Africa
L43@P0: and adjacent parts of Namibia. The highest species diversity is found in the south-western part of South Africa (van
L44@P0: der Walt & Vorster 1983). This centre of Pelargonium diversity receives rainfall exclusively in winter or, in a small
L45@P0: transition zone, throughout the year. This is in contrast to the central and eastern parts of South Africa, where
L46@P0-1: rainfall is concentrated in the summer months. Outside southern Africa, the genus Pelargonium is represented by 32 • Phytotaxa 159 (2) © 2014 Magnolia Press RÖSCHENBLECK ET AL.
L47@P1: only about thirty species, mainly distributed in mountainous habitats along the East African rift valley and in
L48@P1: southern Australia. The few remaining species are scattered across Madagascar, Arabian Peninsula, Asia Minor,
L49@P1: New Zealand, and the islands Socotra, St. Helena and Tristan da Cunha.
L50@P1: Many different growth forms occur within Pelargonium, ranging from herbaceous annuals, subshrubs and
L51@P1: shrubs to stem succulents and geophytes (van der Walt 1977, van der Walt & Vorster 1981a, 1988). The historical
L52@P1: classification of the genus into sections, as originally circumscribed by De Candolle in 1824, was largely based on
L53@P1: this diversity in growth forms. In the past thirty years there has been extensive taxonomic research at an
L54@P1: infrageneric level, based on single or combined data sets from morphology (e.g. van der Walt & Boucher 1986, van
L55@P1: der Walt & van Zyl 1988, Albers et al. 1992, Dreyer et al. 1992a, Maggs et al. 1995a), palynology (Stafford &
L56@P1: Gibby 1992), phytochemistry (Williams et al. 2000) and karyology (e.g. Albers & van der Walt 1984, Gibby et al.
L57@P1: 1990). New circumscriptions and further studies on a sectional level followed (e.g. van der Walt et al. 1995, 1997,
L58@P1: Albers et al. 1995, 2000, Dreyer & Marais 2000). On a higher taxonomic level, karyological results suggested a
L59@P1: split of the genus into two groups with large (1.5–3 µm) and small (< 1.5 µm) chromosomes, respectively (Albers
L60@P1: & van der Walt 1984, Gibby & Westfold 1986; Albers 1988, Gibby et al. 1990, 1996). Phylogenetic analyses of
L61@P1: molecular data (Price & Palmer 1993, Bakker et al. 1999a, 2000) confirmed this basal split. This division, however,
L62@P1: seemed not to be supported by any morphological synapomorphies (Bakker et al. 2004). Therefore, no formal
L63@P1: taxonomic conclusions were drawn, even though the two clades have often been referred to as “subgenera” in an
L64@P1: informal manner (e.g. Bakker & al 1999a, Bakker et al. 2004, Weng et al. 2012).
L65@P1: Based on sequence data from three genomes (Bakker et al. 2004) and in analyses of five plastid and one
L66@P1: mitochondrial marker (Weng et al. 2012), three to five main clades were further suggested between the basal
L67@P1: chromosomal split and traditional sections. Furthermore, the monophyly of several sections in their current
L68@P1: circumscription remains a controversial issue (Bakker & 2004, Albers and Becker 2010, Weng et al. 2012).
L69@P1: Currently, fifteen sections are validly recognised, as well as a tentatively circumscribed “sect. Magnistipulacea”
L70@P1: (compare Bakker et al. 1999a, 2004) which is still awaiting its formal description. A variety of molecular markers
L71@P1: (trnL-F, ITS and AFLP) have been used more recently in phylogenetic analyses within a few sections (Bakker et al.
L72@P1: 1998, Touloumenidou et al. 2004, Becker & Albers 2009). In a survey of trnL-F data, length mutations (indels)
L73@P1: were also found to be phylogenetically informative within clades of Pelargonium with small chromosomes (Bakker
L74@P1: et al. 1999a).
L75@P1: Above sectional level several non-molecular traits were tested for correlations with molecular results. These
L76@P1: included growth forms and basic chromosome numbers (Bakker et al. 1999b, 2004), stem succulence, tuber
L77@P1: formation and hypanthium length (Bakker 2005) as well as leaf venation, leaf dissection and leaf margins (Jones et
L78@P1: al. 2009). But the distribution of character states overlaps or varies among the main clades.
L79@P1: The family Geraniaceae deviates remarkably from other angiosperms in having highly rearranged plastid
L80@P1: genomes that differ in gene order, gene content and expansion of the inverted repeat (Chumley et al. 2006,
L81@P1: Guisinger et al. 2011). Pelargonium × hortorum L.H.Bailey (1916: 2531) has the largest cp genome so far
L82@P1: observed in flowering plants (Chumley et al. 2006). Moreover, in chloroplast and mitochondrial genes of
L83@P1: Pelargonium and related genera, the DNA substitutional rates are found to be noticeably increased (Parkinson et al.
L84@P1: 2005, Bakker et al. 2006, Mower et al. 2007, Guisinger et al. 2008).
L85@P1: In the current study we compiled a large atpB-rbcL spacer dataset (110 taxa) and added newly generated and
L86@P1: already published sequences of the trnL-F spacer for a combined analysis that aimed at clarifying the basalmost
L87@P1: splits and other remaining uncertainties in the phylogeny of Pelargonium. The enlarged genome of Pelargonium ×
L88@P1: hortorum might be caused by several boundary changes of the inverted repeat (Chumley et al. 2006). In
L89@P1: Pelargonium × hortorum the same authors found the atpB-rbcL region localised in the transition between the large
L90@P1: single copy region and the inverted repeat. Therefore, we expected a considerable number of microstructural
L91@P1: changes that might add further phylogenetic signal on the infrageneric level.
L92@P1: In addition, character evolution was studied for a number of non-molecular traits (floral morphology and
L93@P1: phenolic compounds) as well as for their potential use as diagnostic characters. This is the first time these have
L94@P1: been analysed in a wide study of the genus overall, although they had formerly proven valuable for earlier sectional
L95@P1: classifications. For example, floral characters have previously shown clear differences between Pelargonium
L96@P1: species and supported the division or infrasectional classification of sections (e.g. Dreyer et al. 1992a, Marais
L97@P1: 1994). Similarly, patterns of phenolic compounds often characterise infrasectional groups in Pelargonium and have
L98@P1-2: been used in delimination of sections (Albers et al. 1995, 2000, Dreyer et al. 1992a, van der Walt et al. 1995) as THE GENUS PELARGONIUM (GERANIACEAE) Phytotaxa 159 (2) © 2014 Magnolia Press • 33
L99@P2: well as uniformly characterising an otherwise macro-morphologically variable section (van der Walt et al. 1997).
L100@P2: Based on these insights from molecular, morphological and chemical data we conclude with a proposal for a new
L101@P2: subgeneric classification of the genus Pelargonium.
L102@P2: Materials and methods
L103@P2: Taxon sampling and molecular markers
L104@P2: The atpB-rbcL spacer was sequenced for a total of 104 Pelargonium species, 11 of which are included for the
L105@P2: first time in a molecular phylogenetic analysis. The atpB-rbcL intergenic spacer was chosen because it provided
L106@P2: good resolution in a variety of infrageneric studies (e.g. Setoguchi et al. 1997, Wissemann & Ritz 2005, Janssens et
L107@P2: al. 2006, Wanntorp 2006), while at the same time being present in all included taxa with their rapidly evolving
L108@P2: plastid genomes. All traditionally recognised sections and recently introduced infrageneric groups (e.g. Bakker et
L109@P2: al. 2004) were represented. Two different sampling strategies for the large sectt. Hoarea (Sweet) De Candolle
L110@P2: (1824: 649) and Pelargonium were used. Sect. Hoarea inhabits a crown position in Pelargonium phylogeny
L111@P2: (Bakker et al. 1999a, Touloumenidou et al. 2004), therefore we included species with assorted floral types
L112@P2: following Marais (1994). Sect. Pelargonium appeared as non-monophyletic with two species, P. denticulatum
L113@P2: Jacquin (1797; 5) and P. quercifolium (Linnaeus filius) L’Héritier in Aiton (1789: 422), separated from one main
L114@P2: group in Bakker et al. (2004). These two species belong to a subgroup with sticky leaves (Albers & van der Walt
L115@P2: 1984). Here, we added a further species of this group, P. glutinosum (Jacquin) L’Héritier in Aiton (1789: 426).
L116@P2: The atpB-rbcL sequences were combined with 47 new sequences of the trnL-F spacer generated from the same
L117@P2: specimen, supplemented with already published sequences by Bakker et al. (2004) to obtain an accumulated data
L118@P2: set of 104 Pelargonium taxa. Two taxa of Erodium, Geranium and Monsonia were used as an outgroup to represent
L119@P2: Geraniaceae sensu stricto (Price & Palmer 1993, Albers 1996, Fiz et al. 2008). The monotypic genus California
L120@P2: Aldasoro, Navarro, Vargas, Sáez & Aedo (2002: 213), recently separated from Erodium (Aldasoro et al. 2002), was
L121@P2: not included, because of its unclear position in phylogeny (Stevens 2001 onwards). We likewise did not include any
L122@P2: Hypseocharis Remy (1847: 238) species, which are optionally placed in Geraniaceae in its broader circumscription
L123@P2: (APG III 2009). Fresh leaf material was taken from the living collection of the Botanical Garden, University of
L124@P2: Münster, and from a few species of the wild species collection of a Pelargonium breeder (Syngenta, formerly
L125@P2: Fischer Pelargonien, Hillscheid). For P. boranense Friis & M.G.Gilbert (1976: 1705), fresh tissue dried in silica gel
L126@P2: was used. All species, origins, vouchers and gene bank accession numbers are listed in appendix I.
L127@P2: Amplification and sequencing
L128@P2: DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Quiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the
L129@P2: manufacturer’s protocol. PCR amplifications were performed with 50 µl volumes, containing 3 µl DNA template
L130@P2: (50–120 ng/µl), 4 µl dNTP mix (2,5 mM), 2 µl of each primer(10 pmol/µl) and 0,4 µl (5u/µl) Taq-Polymerase
L131@P2: (BioTherm, GeneCraft or GoTaq, Promega) on a primus 96 advanced, PEQLAB or on Robocycler Gradient 96,
L132@P2: Stratagene. The general cycling profile was an initial step of 3 min at 94°C, followed by 40 cycles of 20 sec at
L133@P2: 94°C, 30 sec at 58°C and 60 sec at 72°C, plus a final elongation of 5 min at 72°C. The following primers were
L134@P2: designed according to the sequence of P. x hortorum (DQ897681), atpBrev (5’-GAC CAA TGA TTT GGA CGA
L135@P2: TAC GCC C-3’), rbcLrev (5’-GTA TCC TTG GTT TCA TAA TCA GG-3’), trnLfor (5’- CTT ACT AAG TGA
L136@P2: TAA CTT TCA AAT TC-3’) and trnFrev (5’-CCG ACC ATT TCC AAT GCA TC-3’). PCR products were
L137@P2: controlled by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel. Bands were excised, purified using NucleoSpin Extract II
L138@P2: (Macherey & Nagel, Düren, Germany) and precipitated. The sequencing reaction was performed in a 12 μl final
L139@P2: volume with the BigDye v3.1 Terminator cycle sequencing kit (Applied Biosystems) on an ABI PRISM 3730XL
L140@P2: capillary sequencer at the UKM, University of Münster.
L141@P2: Alignment, indel coding and phylogenetic analyses
L142@P2: The consensus sequences were compiled in BioEdit v7.0.7.0 (Hall 1999) and, after an initial preliminary
L143@P2: alignment with Clustal W, manually aligned. Two hotspots with a total of 35 characters (atpB-rbcL 24 bp, trnL-F
L144@P2: 11 bp) were excluded from the analyses because of variable strings of mononucleotides. The observed indels were
L145@P2-3: coded via simple indel coding (SIC, Simmons & Ochoterena 2000) as well as modified complex indel coding 34 • Phytotaxa 159 (2) © 2014 Magnolia Press RÖSCHENBLECK ET AL.
L146@P3: (MCIC, Müller 2006), both implemented in Seqstate (Müller 2005). Maximum parsimony (MP) analyses with and
L147@P3: without indel coding were done with PAUP v4.0b10 (Swofford 2003). Heuristic searches of the individual and
L148@P3: combined datasets were carried out using the parsimony ratchet algorithm as implemented in PRAP v2.0b3 (Müller
L149@P3: 2004) with default settings. Bootstrap support values (BV) were computed using 10 000 replicates and the settings
L150@P3: as recommended in Müller (2005). Additionally Bremer support (BS) was computed for both datasets and all MP
L151@P3: analyses. For BS calculation, again PRAP was used. Ancestral states of indel characters were inferred by using the
L152@P3: simple indel coding matrix in Mesquite v2.75 (Maddison & Maddison 2011), where a parsimony ancestral state
L153@P3: optimisation on the SIC consensus tree was performed
L154@P3: The best-fit model of sequence evolution for Bayesian inference (BI) and maximum likelihood (ML) analyses
L155@P3: was determined using jModelTest (Posada 2008). For both datasets the models selected by AIC were GTR+G and
L156@P3: GTR+I+G, both within the 95%-confidence interval. BI was performed using GTR+I+G and defaults priors in Mr.
L157@P3: Bayes v.3.1.2 (Ronquist & Huelsenbeck 2003). Two runs with four chains each, sampling every 100th generation
L158@P3: and temperature set to 0.2 were conducted using 10 Million generations. To verify that convergence of the
L159@P3: independent runs was reached, the potential scale reduction factor and the average standard deviation of split
L160@P3: frequencies in MrBayes and the effective sampling size were observed using Tracer v1.5 (Rambaut & Drummond
L161@P3: 2007). Twenty five percent of the retained trees of each run were conservatively discarded as burn-in. The
L162@P3: remaining trees were pooled and a majority rule consensus tree was constructed in MrBayes. ML searches were
L163@P3: performed using the GTR+G model in RaxML v7.03 (Stamatakis 2006, Stamatakis et al. 2008). Rapid bootstrap
L164@P3: analysis with 250 replicates was followed by 10 ML searches, resulting in one best tree.
L165@P3: Non-molecular data
L166@P3: Ancestral state reconstructions were inferred for seven floral characters and 12 leaf phenolic characters on the
L167@P3: Bayesian consensus tree from the combined molecular data set, using two different approaches, which both allow
L168@P3: the coding of polymorphic character states, found for almost all floral characters. The first was a parsimony
L169@P3: ancestral state optimisation done in Mesquite. All characters were coded as multistate (morphology) or binary
L170@P3: (chemistry) and treated as unordered. As a second approach, ancestral states were reconstructed using the Baysian
L171@P3: approach (BBM; F81+G model) implemented in the software RASP 2.1b (Yu et al. 2013). Markov chain Monte
L172@P3: Carlo analyses were conducted with two runs of 10 chains with 100.000 generations each. Trees were sampled
L173@P3: every 100th generation; the burnin was set to 25%. Missing character states are not accepted by the program,
L174@P3: therefore missing characters in data on phenolic compounds were recoded as polymorphic (presence & absence).
L175@P3: Characters and their respective character states are listed in Table 1; further character specific information is
L176@P3: provided in the following.
L177@P3: All morphological character were additionally coded for a total of 229 Pelargonium species and subspecies
L178@P3: (approximately 85% of the genus) to analyse their state distribution among major clades.

L263@P6: In Pelargonium, five states were distinguished for the character “petal colour”: “pink” includes the range from
L264@P6: true pink through dark shades of pink, such as magenta and purple. Scarlet or bright clear reds are separately scored
L265@P6: as “red”. “White” means true white to light pink, but without any trace of yellow. “Yellow” includes the typical
L266@P6: Pelargonium cream tinge as well as green-yellowish petals. “Bicoloured” flowers possess clearly differently
L267@P6: coloured posterior and anterior petals. “Hypanthium length” was categorised according to published size classes
