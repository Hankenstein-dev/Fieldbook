L152: The precise cite88†taxonomy of the cite89†genus cite90†Allium is still poorly understood with incorrect descriptions being widespread. With over 850 species distributed over the cite91†Northern Hemisphere Allium is the sole genus in the cite92†Allieae , one of four cite93†tribes of cite94†subfamily cite95†Allioideae (cite96†Amaryllidaceae ).
L153: New species continue to be described^{cite97†[1] } and Allium is both highly variable and one of the largest monocotyledonous genera,^{cite98†[2] } but the precise cite88†taxonomy of Allium is poorly understood,^{cite98†[2] }^{cite97†[1] } with incorrect descriptions being widespread. The difficulties arise from the fact that the genus displays considerable polymorphism and has adapted to a wide variety of habitats.
L154: Furthermore, traditional classifications had been based on cite99†homoplasious characteristics (what turn out to be independently evolved similar features in species from different lineages).
L155: However, the genus has been shown to be cite100†monophyletic , containing three major cite101†clades , although some proposed subgenera are not.^{cite98†[2] } Some progress is being made using molecular phylogenetic methods, and the cite102†internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, including the 5.8S cite103†rDNA and the two cite104†spacers ITS1 and ITS2, is one of the more commonly used markers in the study of the differentiation of the Allium species.^{cite97†[1] }
L156: Allium includes a number of taxonomic groupings previously considered separate genera (Caloscordum Herb., Milula Prain and Nectaroscordum Lindl.) Allium spicatum had been treated by many authors as Milula spicata, the only species in the cite105†monospecific genus Milula. In 2000, it was shown to be cite106†embedded in Allium.^{cite107†[3] }
L157: ## Description
L158: 
L159: [cite108†edit ]
L160: The cite89†genus Allium are cite109†herbaceous cite110†geophytes is characterised by cite111†bulbs enclosed in membranous tunics, that may become fibrous and may be carried on cite112†rhizomes , with cite113†tepals that are free or almost free, and a subgynobasic cite114†style . The majority of cite115†species produce cite116†cysteine cite117†sulphoxides that are the source of their distinctive garlic and onion odor and taste.
L161: About twenty species are grown as cite118†edible crops , such as cite119†onions , cite120†garlic and cite121†leeks , while others are cite122†foraged from the wild, such as cite123†ramps .
L162: Many species are cite124†xerophytic and the over 850 species are found almost exclusively in the cite91†Northern Hemisphere , being particularly diverse in the warm dry summers and cool wet winters of the cite125†Mediterranean .^{cite97†[1] } The main cite126†centre of diversity is the cite127†Old World with species rich areas in cite128†Central Asia as well as the cite129†Mediterranean Basin . A second centre, in the cite130†New World , is western North America.^{cite131†[4] }^{cite132†[5] }
L163: ## History
L164: 
L165: [cite133†edit ]
L166: 
L167: cite134†Linnaeus ' description of Allium, cite135†Species Plantarum 1753
L168: Descriptions of Allium taxonomy date back at least as far as cite136†Carolus Clusius ' Rariarum plantarum historia (1601).^{cite137†[6] } When Linnaeus^{cite138†[7] } formerly described the genus Allium in his cite135†Species Plantarum (1753), there were thirty species with this name. He placed Allium in a grouping he referred to as Hexandria monogynia^{cite139†[8] } (i.e.
L169: six stamens and one cite140†pistil ) containing 51 genera in all.^{cite141†[9] } In 1763, cite142†Michel Adanson , who proposed the concept of families of plants, included Allium and related genera as a grouping within 'cite143†Liliaceae '^{cite144†[10] } as Section IV, Les Oignons (Onions), or Cepae in Latin.^{cite145†[11] } cite146†De Jussieu is officially recognised as the first formal establishment of the suprageneric grouping into families (Ordo) in 1789.
L170: In this system Allium was one of fourteen genera in Ordo VI, Asphodeles (Asphodeli), of the third class (Stamina epigyna) of cite147†Monocots .^{cite148†[12] } cite149†Jean Henri Jaume Saint-Hilaire (1805), who developed the concept of cite96†Amaryllidaceae , continued Jussieu's treatment of Allium under Asphodeli (which he considered cite150†synonymous with Adanson's Liliaceae and Jussieu's Asphodeli).^{cite151†[13] } He placed Allium in an unnamed cite152†monotypic section of Asphodeli defined as Fleurs en ombelle, racine bulbeuse.
L171: Calice à six parties egales (cite153†umbellate flowers, cite154†bulbous , cite155†calyx of six equal parts).^{cite156†[14] }
L172: Subsequently, cite157†de Candolle reverted the family name back to Liliaceae from Asphodeli.^{cite158†[15] } He divided the Liliaceae into a series of Ordres, and the second ordre was named Asphodèles, based on Jussieus' family of that name,^{cite159†[16] } in which he placed Allium.^{cite159†[16] } The term 'Alliaceae' then reappeared in its subfamilial form, Allieae, in cite160†Dumortier 's Florula Belgica (1827),^{cite161†[17] } with six genera.
L173: The 'Alliaceae' have been treated as Allieae within the family Liliaceae (or Aspholecaceae, a partial synonym) by most authorities since.
L174: cite162†Regel produced a major monograph of the genus in 1875,^{cite163†[18] } and this remained the major reference work for over 100 years till the molecularly based study of Friesen and colleagues in 2006.^{cite164†[19] } Despite recent advances the precise cite88†taxonomy of Allium remains still poorly understood with incorrect descriptions being widespread.^{cite165†[20] }^{cite98†[2] }^{cite97†[1] }
L175: ## Phylogeny
L176: 
L177: [cite166†edit ]
L178: 
L179: cite96†Amaryllidaceae : Subfamily cite95†Allioideae |
L180: Tribe cite92†Allieae (cite167†monogeneric , Allium)
L181: Tribe cite168†Tulbaghieae L182: Tribes cite169†Gilliesieae , cite170†Leucocoryneae L183: ## Subdivision
L184: 
L185: [cite171†edit ]
L186: Linnaeus originally grouped his 30 species into three cite172†alliances , e.g. Foliis caulinis planis and as the number of recognised species increased, so did the number of subgroups. Since then, many attempts have been made to divide the growing number of recognised species into infrageneric subgroupings, initially as sections, and then as subgenera further divided into sections. For a brief history, see Friesen et al. (2006)^{cite164†[19] } and Li et al.
L187: (2010)^{cite98†[2] } Regel's 1875 treatise on Allium divided his 262 species between the six sections proposed by cite173†Don , in his 1832 monograph on the genus.^{cite174†[21] } cite175†Stearn (1944) described 14 subgenera.^{cite176†[22] } cite177†Traub (1968) described 3 subgenera, 36 sections and subsections and about 600 species.^{cite178†[23] } By 1992 there were 6 sub-genera, 50 sections and subsections and 600–700 species.^{cite179†[24] } The situation was further confused by the presence of over 1,000 taxonomic names, many of which turned out to be cite150†synonyms .^{cite180†[25] }^{cite165†[20] }
L188: The modern era of cite181†phylogenetic analysis dates to 1996.^{cite182†[26] } In 2006 Friesen, Fritsch, and Blattner^{cite164†[19] } described a new classification with 15 cite183†subgenera , 56 cite184†sections , and about 780 species based on the cite185†nuclear cite186†ribosomal cite187†gene cite102†internal transcribed spacers .
L189: Some of the subgenera correspond to the once separate genera (Caloscordum, Milula, Nectaroscordum) included in the cite169†Gilliesieae .^{cite98†[2] }^{cite188†[27] } The terminology has varied with some authors subdividing subgenera into Sections and others Alliances.
L190: The term cite172†alliance has been used for both subgroupings within species, e.g. cite189†Allium nigrum , as well as infrageneric subsections. These alliances are informal groupings based on morphological similarity and reflecting hypotheses of evolutionary relationship.
L191: and can be used between any two formal ranks.^{cite190†[28] } For instance the some 70 North American species were divided into nine well-defined species alliances, of which the largest was the Allium falcifolium alliance with 31 taxa.^{cite191†[29] } These alliances are usually referred to as the Ownbey alliances, after Marion Ownbey^{cite165†[20] } and were also used by Traub.^{cite178†[23] } A number of classification schemes have chosen to retain these, the Traub system not being universally accepted.^{cite164†[19] }^{cite132†[5] }
L192: Subsequent cite192†molecular phylogenetic studies have shown the 2006 classification is a considerable improvement over previous classifications, but some of its subgenera and sections are probably not cite100†monophyletic . Meanwhile, the number of new species continued to increase, reaching 800 by 2009, 900 by 2016^{cite193†[30] } and the pace of discovery has not decreased. Detailed studies have focused on a number of subgenera, including Amerallium.
L193: Amerallium is strongly cite194†supported as monophyletic.^{cite195†[31] } Subgenus Melanocrommyum has also been the subject of considerable study (see below), while work on subgenus Allium has focused on section Allium, including cite196†Allium ampeloprasum , although cite197†sampling was not sufficient to test the monophyly of the section.^{cite198†[32] }
L194: The major cite199†evolutionary lineages or lines correspond to the three major clades. Line one (the oldest) with three subgenera is predominantly bulbous, the second, with five subgenera and the third with seven subgenera contain both bulbous and rhizomatous taxa.^{cite98†[2] } Banfi and colleagues (2011) have suggested that the phylogenetic trichotomy of this genus Allium cite200†sensu lato is sufficiently distinct as to warrant splitting it into three separate genera.
L195: Banfi's scheme thus proposes the restoring the three originally separate genera Nectaroscordum Lindl. (type: N. siculum), Caloscordum Herb. (type: C. neriniflorum) and Allium L. cite201†sensu stricto (type: A. sativum) to correspond to lines 1-3.^{cite202†[33] }

L229: Although this lineage consists of three subgenera, nearly all the species are attributed to subgenus Amerallium, the third largest subgenus of Allium. The lineage is considered to represent the most ancient line within Allium, and to be the only lineage that is predominantly bulbous, the other two having both bulbous and rhizomatous taxa. Nectaroscordum and Microscordum are bulbous, but Amerallium contains some rhizomatous elements.

L259: Taxonomy
L260: Amerallium is a relatively large subgenus with about 120–140 species. Under the alliance system of classification proposed by Ownbey (1966), species north of Mexico and two Mexican cite236†endemics were treated as eight informal alliances: the A. acuminatum, cite237†A. campanulatum , A. canadense, A. cernuum, A. falcifolium, A. kunthii, A. sanbornii, and A.
L261: validum alliances.^{cite165†[20] } Traub (1968) then arranged the cite130†New World alliances into four sections: Amerallium Traub, Caulorhizideum Traub, Lophioprason Traub, and Rhopetoprason Traub. In addition he arranged the cite127†Old World species into 6 sections.^{cite178†[23] }
L262: Since Traub's revision of the subgenus, two biogeographical sister clades (or alliances) have been recognised. The Old World clade is represented by two relatively small groups from the Mediterranean and East Asia. The larger New World clade by all North American species of Allium. The New World sections are Lophioprason, Amerallium, and Rhophetoprason, while the Old World is represented by sections Arctoprasum, Briseis, Narkissoprason, Molium, Bromatorrhiza and Rhynchocarpum.^{cite164†[19] }
L263: The subgenus is thought to originate in the Old World, with a later split, and to have its origin in the higher latitudes of cite238†East Asia , at the time of transition from cite239†Cretaceous to cite240†Tertiary , dispersing to western North America. Twelve sections were subsequently recognized, with sections Amerallium and Molium further split into two subsections.^{cite164†[19] }^{cite98†[2] }^{cite132†[5] }

L265: Distribution
L266: Amerallium is widely distributed within North America, Europe, north Africa, Ethiopia, the Caucasus, northern Iran, southeast Tibet, and southwest China.^{cite132†[5] } The greatest species diversity occurs in North America with 81 species recognized in the 2002 cite241†Flora of North America (north of Mexico)^{cite242†[38] } and a further 13 are unique to Mexico,^{cite243†[39] } and a total of 26 species recognised there.^{cite132†[5] } Within N America, the genus covers most of the area south of the cite244†53rd parallel , including the oak hillsides of California and Oregon, deserts of Nevada and Texas, alpine meadows of Utah and Idaho, prairies of Nebraska and Manitoba, and forest glades of Missouri and Arkansas.^{cite132†[5] }
