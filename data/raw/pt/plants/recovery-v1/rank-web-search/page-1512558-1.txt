L5@P0: A taxonomic survey of Lotus section Pedrosia (Leguminosae, Loteae)
L6@P0: Graeme Sandral, Margarita V. Remizowa & Dmitry D. Sokoloff
L7@P0: Summary: A taxonomic review of the section Pedrosia (excl. sect. Rhyncholotus) is presented. Members of
L8@P0: the section occur in the Macaronesian Islands, north-western part of Africa and in the Mediterranean
L9@P0: region. Centers of diversity are Canary Islands, Cape Verde, Madeira and Morocco. Twenty nine species
L10@P0: of the section are recognized; their diagnostic characters are discussed and extensively illustrated.
L11@P0: An identifi cation key to species of the section is given as well as morphological descriptions of
L12@P0: species and subspecies. Leaf morphology was found to be a particularly useful character in species
L13@P0: discrimination. Many species are quite variable, and their identifi cation is only possible using a suite of
L14@P0: several characters. Furthermore, various morphological characters have different taxonomic weight in
L15@P0: different regions (e.g. Cape Verde and Morocco). The following taxonomic decisions are adopted: (1)
L16@P0: Lotus azoricus is synonymous with L . argyrodes; (2) L . tenellus is accepted as a species distinct from L .
L17@P0: glaucus, with an area covering Tenerife and Gran Canaria; (3) all material examined from Hierro (Canary
L18@P0: Islands) is identifi ed as L . sessilifolius; (4) L . hillebrandii is treated as a subspecies of L . campylocladus; (5)
L19@P0: L . spartioides is synonymous with L . holosericus. Representatives of the section Pedrosia from Lanzarote
L20@P0: and Fuerteventura are closer to those from Madeira than to plants from other Canary Islands.

L23@P0: The genus Lotus includes agriculturally important plants such as L . corniculatus, and L . pedunculatus,
L24@P0: as well as the model legume L . japonicus and ornamentals (e.g. L . berthelotti and L . maculatus). In
L25@P0: recent times increased interest in the placement of Lotus and its species using molecular and
L26@P0: morphological data has stimulated a closer examination of these genetic resources. Such studies
L27@P0: need a sound taxonomic basis, however, Lotus is a taxonomically diffi cult genus (Gillett 1958;
L28@P0: heyn & herrnStadt 1968; kraMina 1999a,b, 2006; kraMina & Sokoloff 2004). In particular,
L29@P0: no detailed worldwide taxonomic study of the whole genus has been produced since Brand’s
L30@P0: (1898) monograph. However, in more recent times taxonomic and phylogentic studies of Lotus
L31@P0: have been undertaken, for example, the phylogeny of Lotus has been studied by means of
L32@P0: cladistic analyses based on morphological (araMBarri 2000) and molecular data (allan et al.
L33@P0: 2003, 2004; deGtjareVa et al. 2006). In addition, kirkBride (1999) published a worldwide list
L34@P0: of Lotus species, while kraMina (1999b, 2006) published taxonomic studies of the L . corniculatus
L35@P0: and L . angustissimus groups. Kramina (pers. comm.) is also preparing a monographic revision of
L36@P0: the whole section Lotus which includes many agriculturally important species. Australian and
L37@P0: Pacifi c Lotus species have been revised recently by kraMina & Sokoloff (2004). The present
L38@P0: paper contributes to this ongoing taxonomic revision of the genus Lotus through the taxonomic
L39@P0: survey of its section Pedrosia.

L96@P2: A taxonomic survey of Lotus section Pedrosia
L97@P2: to the genus Lotus, and the section Pedrosia is more diverse than the other two. The section
L98@P2: Canaria is taxonomically isolated (Degtjareva et al. 2006) and was probably established in the
L99@P2: Canaries before the radiation of Pedrosia. Other genera of the tribe do not have species endemic
L100@P2: to the Macaronesian Islands, except for Anthyllis, which has a single and morphologically poorly
L101@P2: differentiated endemic species on Madeira.
L102@P2: The most important diagnostic character of the section Pedrosia is the presence of a tooth on the
L103@P2: ventral side of the stylodium (style) (Kramina & Sokoloff 1999). This feature is shared with the
L104@P2: section Rhyncholotus, which species (L. berthelotii and its allies) are endemic to the Canary Islands.
L105@P2: Previously this section was accepted as Lotus sect. Heinekenia (nom. illeg.) by Brand (1898), as
L106@P2: a distinct genus Heinekenia by Christ (1888) and Kunkel (1974), as a subgenus Rhyncholotus by
L107@P2: Monod (1980) and as section Rhyncholotus by Sokoloff (2003a). The section Rhyncholotus differs
L108@P2: from the section Pedrosia as it includes plants that have a much more specialized corolla structure
L109@P2: (fig. 1), that is linked with an adaptation to pollination by birds. Phylogenetic data clearly show
L110@P2: monophyly of a clade comprising all studied members of Pedrosia and Rhyncholotus (Allan et
L111@P2: al. 2004; Degtjareva et al. 2006). Relationships within this clade are not completely resolved,
L112@P2: however, it seems that Rhyncholotus is embedded within the paraphyletic Pedrosia. Molecular data
L113@P2: (nuclear ribosomal ITS) alone does not show monophyly of Rhyncholotus (Allan et al. 2004) while
L114@P2: analyses of a combined data set (nrITS plus morphology) revealed its monophyly (Degtjareva et
L115@P2: al. 2006). This could imply, that species of Rhyncholotus should be better placed into the broadly
L116@P2: accepted section Pedrosia. We, however, do not pursue this amalgamation here as the phylogeny
L117@P2: of the Lotus berthelotii-group is not well resolved and, in addition, it may be reasonable to keep
L118@P2: some sections of Lotus paraphyletic (see Degtjareva et al. 2006 for discussion). These questions,
L119@P2: however, are deserving of further study.

L128@P3: into the section (subgenus) Pedrosia. Valdés (2000) also described a tooth on the stylodium

L130@P3: have not clarified these disagreements. For example, Allan et al. (2003, 2004) found L. creticus
L131@P3: nested within the section Lotea while Degtjareva et al. (2006) found its placement within the
L132@P3: Pedrosia-Rhyncholotus clade. Certainly more molecular phylogenetic data are needed to clarify the
L133@P3: placement of L. creticus. In this study, we place L. creticus tentatively in the section Pedrosia as our

L135@P3: L. creticus is morphologically and ecologically similar to L. pseudocreticus of the section Pedrosia.
L136@P3: Providing a more definitive assessment of the position of L. creticus will be the subject of further
L137@P3: studies by the authors and their colleagues in Moscow.
L138@P3: Species-level taxonomy of the section Pedrosia is problematic. Since Brand’s (1998) monographic
L139@P3: study of the genus Lotus many new species have been described in this group. Monod (1980)
L140@P3: produced a nomenclatural survey of all specific names published in the group, while Mader &
L141@P3: Podlech (1989) and Brochmann et al. (1997) clarified taxonomic problems for Pedrosia species
L142@P3: in Morocco and on Cape Verde, respectively. However, the most diverse groups of Pedrosia
L143@P3: species are those inhabiting the Canaries and Madeira, and the morphology of these species has
L144@P3: not been studied in its entirety, although several important works have been published. Davis
L145@P3: (1971), Bramwell & Davis (1972) and Bramwell (1995) conducted detailed morphological
L146@P3: and taxonomical analyses of species related to L. glaucus and L. sessilifolius. Allan et al. (2004)
L147@P3: produced a phylogeny of Canarian species based on nrITS sequences and Oliva-Tejera et al.
L148@P3: (2004) studied population genetics of representatives of Pedrosia which occur in Gran Canarian
L149@P3: pine forests.
L150@P3: Prior to this publication no comprehensive worldwide study of Pedrosia or identification key for
L151@P3: its species had been completed. Developing such a key is important for use in scientific centers
L152@P3: studying the conservation, ecology and phylogenetics of such species, as correct identification
L153@P3: of plant material is crucial in all these fields.
L154@P3: This study is based on two primary sources. The first is a living collection of Pedrosia species
L155@P3: established from seeds collected in Africa and Macaronesia primarily by G. Sandral and
L156@P3: collaborators in 2001–2004. Plants were established in a common garden at the University of
L157@P3: Western Australia Field Station in Perth and examined over two years. The second source used
L158@P3: were comprehensive collections of the herbaria BM, G, K, LE, LISU, M, MA, RNG and Z,
L159@P3: which were examined and compared with the living collection.
L160@P3: Voucher herbarium specimens have been collected from almost all accessions cultivated for this
L161@P3: study in Perth. One set of vouchers is deposited at MW and a second at K. Vouchers are cited in
L162@P3: this paper along with other studied specimens, which are identified after the description of each
L163@P3: species. Voucher numbers starting with the letters “SA” are referring to the accession numbers
L164@P3: of germplasm, which are preserved as seeds for long term conservation at the South Australian
L165@P3: Genetic Resource Center in Adelaide under the stewardship of curator Steve Hughes.

L170@P4: Morphological characters used in taxonomy of the section Pedrosia
L171@P4: As in other sections of Lotus, almost all taxonomically significant characters are variable within at
L172@P4: least some species of the section Pedrosia. Only a combination of several characters can be used to
L173@P4: identify the material and to assign specimens to particular species. That is why it is so difficult to
L174@P4: produce a useful key for the identification of these species and why many species occur twice or
L175@P4: even more than twice in the key presented here. It is important to note, that character overlapping
L176@P4: not only occurs between closely related species, whose taxonomic separation could be questioned
L177@P4: (e.g. L. glaucus, L. tenellus and L. sessilifolius), but also between less related species. For example, it
L178@P4: was difficult to explain, how the Cape Verde species L. purpureus should be distinguished from
L179@P4: clearly different species from Canary Islands.
L180@P4: It is important to point out, that plants cultivated in Perth are morphologically very similar
L181@P4: to those taken from their natural habitat and conserved as herbarium samples. At least, we
L182@P4: found no obvious deviations in taxonomically significant characters between these sources. The
L183@P4: common garden ensured, that all observable characters were robust enough to be distinguished
L184@P4: in a common environment. Also, our study of the living collection in Perth suggests that F1
L185@P4: interspecific hybrids are not frequent in natural populations in Canary Islands, Cape Verde and
L186@P4: Madeira. Indeed all plants grown from the collected seeds showed similar morphology with
L187@P4: regard to identification characters of that particular species.
L188@P4: We briefly discuss below some of the characters used in determining the taxonomy of Pedrosia
L189@P4: (also see tab. 2).
L190@P4: Growth form. Among species of the section, only L. arenarius is an annual plant, although in
L191@P4: some very rare cases individual plants did survive into the next season. The annual nature of
L192@P4: L. arenarius observed in Perth agrees with observations made in the wild (Mader & Podlech
L193@P4: 1989). This is an important taxonomic character, which distinguishes L. arenarius from the closely
L194@P4: related L. maroccanus.
L195@P4: Among the perennial plants, herbs, half-shrubs (suffrutices) and dwarf shrubs can be recognized.
L196@P4: Differences between these forms in Pedrosia are not stable and in most cases can only be used as
L197@P4: a taxonomic tendency. It should be stressed that most “perennial” plants are in fact short lived (3
L198@P4: to 6 years). This is also true for shrubby plants, such as L. arborescens and L. jacobaeus. For example,
L199@P4: these species can develop quite lignified trunks up to 2 cm thick; with second year buds located
L200@P4: sometimes around 0.8m above the ground. Such plants look like a long lived perennial, but may
L201@P4: only be two years of age. Significant variation was found for longevity, with some plants dying
L202@P4: after two seasons while others survived up to the fourth season, after which time the common
L203@P4: garden was cleared for other purposes. Some other species, such as L. emeroides, develop long
L204@P4: and often thick and lignified prostrate stems with second year buds close to the distal part of
L205@P4: the main stem. These plants had densely branching shoot systems, and were also short-lived.
L206@P4: Within the section Pedrosia, L. eriosolen seemed to be distinct in having adventitious roots (tab. 2)
L207@P4: on prostrate stems. However, this did not increase its perenniality over that of L. emeroides, L.
L208@P4: arborescens or L. jacobaeus, at least under the specific conditions of Perth.
