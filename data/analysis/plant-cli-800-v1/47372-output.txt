The coast redwood (*Sequoia sempervirens*) is the sole living species in its genus and a long-lived evergreen conifer native to a narrow coastal belt from central California to southwestern Oregon. It can live for roughly 2,000 years and is the world’s tallest living tree species, reaching about 116 meters (381 feet). Coast redwoods thrive in cool, moist forests, absorb water from coastal fog, and can regenerate from seeds, stumps, roots, and fallen branches. Their thick bark provides strong resistance to fire, insects, fungi, and decay. The lightweight, durable wood is commercially valuable, though extensive logging has greatly reduced the species’ original old-growth forests. (Sources: s1:p2–p5, p13–p14, p27, p30, p33, p39–p43)
